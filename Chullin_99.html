Daf Yomi — Chullin 99 (חולין צט)

Chapter 7: Gid HaNasheh (the sciatic nerve) · Study date: Fri, 7 August 2026

The big picture (read this first)

Yesterday’s daf derived nullification in sixty from the cooked foreleg of the nazirite’s ram — and then immediately threatened to demolish the derivation, because מֵחִדּוּשׁ לָא גָּמְרִינַן, we do not learn general rules from an anomaly. Today opens by rescuing it with one of the most useful moves in the Talmud, and then turns from theory to the kitchen.

If a source is too anomalous to teach us anything, that objection only bites when you are using it to be lenient. Learn a stringency from it and the objection evaporates — because without the derivation you would have been more lenient still.

Three movements:

  1. Rescuing sixty (99a) — the anomaly objection is answered by asking what direction the derivation runs in. Then Ravina finds a different job for a word in the baraita.
  2. Is it sixty or a hundred? (99a–99b) — Abaye corners Rav Dimi with a mishnah in Orla about teruma beans and lentils, Rav Dimi falls silent, and then Abaye rescues him — before learning that some prohibitions need nearly two hundred.
  3. Does a sinew have taste at all? (99b) — the halachic bottom line of the whole chapter arrives almost casually, through a story about a man sent back to ask his question twice.

Walking through the sugya, step by step

99a — Why the anomaly objection does not sink sixty

The setup. Where do we learn that the flavor of sacrificial food is not simply nullified? From the sin offering: ״כׇּל אֲשֶׁר יִגַּע בִּבְשָׂרָהּ יִקְדָּשׁ״ — “whatever touches its flesh shall become holy” (Vayikra 6:20). Whatever absorbs flavor from a sin offering takes on its status: disqualified if it was disqualified, and eaten under a sin offering’s stringencies if it was valid.

The question. Why go to the sin offering at all? Learn it from the nazirite’s ram, where flavor is nullified in sixty:

וּמַאי חָזֵית דְּגָמְרִינַן מֵהַאיְךְ, לִיגְמַר מֵהַאי? חִדּוּשׁ הוּא, וּמֵחִדּוּשׁ לָא גָּמְרִינַן. “And what did you see that we learn from that — let us learn from this! It is an anomaly, and from an anomaly we do not learn.”

The objection that matters. If the nazirite’s ram is disqualified as a source, then the whole of yesterday’s daf collapses: the sixty and the hundred were both derived from it.

The answer — mind the direction.

אַטּוּ אֲנַן לְקוּלָּא קָא גָמְרִינַן? לְחוּמְרָא קָא גָמְרִינַן, דְּמִדְּאוֹרָיְיתָא בְּרוּבָּא בָּטֵיל. “Is it that we learn from it as a leniency? We learn from it as a stringency — for by Torah law it would be nullified in a simple majority.”

This is the hinge of the page. Left to itself, Torah law would nullify a forbidden food in a bare majority — fifty-one to forty-nine. The nazirite’s ram is what pushes the bar up to sixty. So the derivation only ever makes us stricter, and “we do not learn from an anomaly” is a rule about not extending an anomaly’s leniencies.

Ravina’s footnote. Ravina finds a different job for the word זֶה in the baraita about the ram: it is needed for מְקוֹם הַחֲתָךְ, the place of the cut. Normally, when you slice a forbidden piece away from a permitted one, the cut surface of the permitted part is forbidden. In the nazirite’s ram, it is permitted.

99a — Abaye corners Rav Dimi: sixty, or a hundred?

The claim under attack. Rav Shmuel bar Rav Yitzchak had said (98a) that nullification requires a hundred. Rav Dimi sat and taught it.

Abaye’s challenge, from a pair of mishnayos in Orla (2:6–7). Teruma mixed into non-sacred food is treated stringently when it is mixed with its own kind, and “leniently and stringently” when mixed with a different kind. The different-kind case: גְּרִיסִין, split beans of teruma, cooked with non-sacred lentils.

  • If the beans are enough to impart flavor — forbidden, whether or not there are 101 parts.
  • If they are not enough to impart flavor — permitted, whether or not there are 101 parts.

The needle. Take that last clause: fewer than 101 parts, no flavor imparted, and still permitted. So in what quantity were the beans nullified? Sixty — against Rav Shmuel bar Rav Yitzchak.

99b — Rav Dimi falls silent, and Abaye rescues him

Rav Dimi’s first answer. The mishnah means: fewer than 101, but there are 100.

Abaye presses. Then read the first clause — teruma leaven falling into non-sacred dough. If the first clause is about 100 and the second is also about 100, what is the difference between them? Rav Dimi retreats a step: the first clause is 101, the second is 100.

The killing blow. If so, why does the mishnah say that when the leaven is enough to leaven the dough the whole is forbidden? At 101 parts leaven cannot leaven anything — so why is it not nullified? אִישְׁתִּיק — Rav Dimi was silent.

And then Abaye hands him the answer. Note who is speaking; the man who just won the argument supplies the defence:

דִּלְמָא שָׁאנֵי שְׂאוֹר דְּחִימּוּצוֹ קָשֶׁה. “Perhaps leaven is different, because its leavening power is harsh” — some leaven really can work on that much dough.

Rav Dimi’s recovery — and a startling measure. “You have reminded me,” he says, of Rabbi Yosei b’Rabbi Chanina: לֹא כׇּל הַשִּׁיעוּרִין שָׁוִין — not all measures are equal. The measure for צִיר, fish brine, is close to two hundred. As the mishnah teaches (Terumot 10:8): the brine of a non-kosher fish is forbidden; Rabbi Yehuda says a quarter-log forbids two se’ah — a ratio of about 192 to 1.

A loose thread pulled. But Rabbi Yehuda holds מִין בְּמִינוֹ לֹא בָּטֵיל, like with like is never nullified — so how is brine nullified at any ratio? Answer: שָׁאנֵי צִיר, דְּזֵיעָה בְּעָלְמָא הוּא — brine is different, it is mere sweat. It is not the fish itself, so it is not “its own kind.”

99b — Do sinews impart taste? The chapter’s practical bottom line

Back to the mishnah (96b): how do you measure whether the gid imparts flavor to the thigh? Treat the nerve as meat and the thigh as a turnip. Rav Huna: כְּבָשָׂר בְּרָאשֵׁי לְפָתוֹת — like meat cooked with turnip-heads.

But the mishnah has an opponent, and he is about to win:

רַבִּי יִשְׁמָעֵאל בְּנוֹ שֶׁל רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן בֶּן בְּרוֹקָה אוֹמֵר: אֵין בְּגִידִין בְּנוֹתֵן טַעַם. “Sinews impart no taste at all.”

If a sinew has no taste, the entire measuring apparatus is unnecessary: remove the nerve and the thigh is fine.

The story. A man brought Rabbi Chanina a thigh cooked with its gid, and Rabbi Chanina permitted it. Rabbi Yehuda bar Zevina, sitting at the gate, told him to go back — perhaps he did not understand the question. The man returned. Rabbi Chanina: “Who is this bothering me? Go tell the one sitting at the gate: sinews impart no taste. Remove the nerve, and the rest is permitted.” He had understood the question perfectly the first time.

The quieter story. When people came to Rabbi Ami with this question, he would send them to Rabbi Yitzchak ben Chalov, who permitted it in the name of Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi. Rabbi Ami did not hold that way himself — but he was unwilling to impose his own stringency on others.

וְהִלְכְתָא: אֵין בְּגִידִין בְּנוֹתֵן טַעַם — and the halacha is that sinews impart no taste.

99b end — A cliffhanger

The mishnah also said: a gid cooked with other sinews, and he cannot identify which is which — all are forbidden. The Gemara asks the obvious question: לִיבַּטֵיל בְּרוּבָּא, let it simply be nullified by the majority! The answer waits for tomorrow’s first line.

Key concepts & terms

Term Meaning
מֵחִדּוּשׁ לָא גָּמְרִינַן (mechiddush lo gamrinan) We do not derive general rules from an anomalous case — but only when deriving a leniency.
לְקוּלָּא / לְחוּמְרָא (lekula / lechumra) Toward leniency / toward stringency. The direction of a derivation decides whether the anomaly objection applies.
בְּרוּבָּא בָּטֵיל (berubba batel) Nullified in a simple majority — what Torah law would say without the derivation from the nazirite’s ram.
מְקוֹם הַחֲתָךְ (mekom hachatach) The surface of the cut where a forbidden piece was severed from a permitted one; normally forbidden.
גְּרִיסִין (grisin) Split beans — the teruma food in the Orla case, cooked with non-sacred lentils.
שְׂאוֹר דְּחִימּוּצוֹ קָשֶׁה (se’or dechimutzo kasheh) Leaven whose leavening power is harsh — potent enough to act even in a very large mixture.
צִיר (tzir) Fish brine. Its measure is close to 200, showing that measures are not uniform.
זֵיעָה בְּעָלְמָא (zei’ah be’alma) “Mere sweat” — brine is a secretion, not the fish itself, so it is not “its own kind.”
מִין בְּמִינוֹ לֹא בָּטֵיל (min bemino lo batel) Rabbi Yehuda’s rule: a forbidden food mixed with its own kind is never nullified.
אֵין בְּגִידִין בְּנוֹתֵן טַעַם (ein begidin benoten ta’am) Sinews impart no taste — the accepted halacha, which makes the gid removable without forbidding the meat.

Who’s who in today’s daf

Tannaim

  • Rabbi Yehuda (bar Ilai) — fourth-generation Tanna, student of Rabbi Akiva; the anonymous “Rabbi Yehuda” of the Mishnah. Here: brine’s measure, and min bemino is never nullified.
  • Rabbi Yishmael b’Rabbi Yochanan ben Beroka — fourth-generation Tanna, son of Rabbi Yochanan ben Beroka. His ruling that sinews have no taste becomes the halacha.

Eretz-Yisrael Amoraim

  • Rabbi Chanina (bar Chama) — first generation, Tzippori; a leading colleague-student of Rebbi. Here: permits the thigh, twice.
  • Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi — first generation, Lod; contemporary of Rabbi Yochanan. The lenient ruling is transmitted in his name.
  • Rabbi Yochanan (bar Nappacha) — second generation, head of the Tiberias academy. Named here only as background to the transmission.
  • Rabbi Ami (Rabbi Ammi) — third generation, succeeded Rabbi Yochanan’s circle in Tiberias. Sends questioners to a more lenient colleague rather than rule strictly for them.
  • Rabbi Yosei b’Rabbi Chanina — second generation, Caesarea; student of Rabbi Yochanan. Source of “not all measures are equal.”
  • Rabbi Yitzchak ben Chalov — a minor figure known mainly as a transmitter of lenient rulings in Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi’s name; I would not want to guess at more than that.
  • Rabbi Yehuda bar Zevina — an obscure figure; he appears here as the sceptic at the gate, and I would not want to place his generation confidently.

Babylonian Amoraim

  • Rav Huna — second generation, head of the academy at Sura and the foremost student of Rav. Here: the mishnah’s measure means meat cooked with turnip-heads.
  • Abaye — fourth generation, head of Pumbedita; the great disputant of Rava. Here he both demolishes Rav Dimi’s position and then supplies its defence.
  • Rav Dimi — fourth generation; a traveller between Eretz Yisrael and Babylonia who transmitted Eretz-Yisrael teachings. Here on the receiving end of Abaye’s questioning.
  • Rav Shmuel bar Rav Yitzchak — third generation; his “one hundred” is the position under examination all through 99a–99b.
  • Ravina — fifth/sixth generation, of the circle that redacted the Talmud. Here: the word זֶה teaches about the place of the cut.

A look-alike worth flagging: Rabbi Chanina (Eretz-Yisrael Amora, above) is not Rabbi Yosei b’Rabbi Chanina, who is his junior by a generation and belongs to Caesarea.

One line to carry with you

“We do not learn from an anomaly” is not a rule about sources — it is a rule about directions. An odd case cannot make you lenient; it can always make you strict.
