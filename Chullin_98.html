Daf Yomi — Chullin 98 (חולין צח)

Chapter 7: Gid HaNasheh (the sciatic nerve) · Study date: Thu, 6 August 2026

The big picture (read this first)

Yesterday (97) landed us on the rule that when tasting can’t settle a mixture, we fall back on nullification in sixty. Today’s daf asks the obvious next question:

Where does the number sixty actually come from — and is it even sixty? And, finishing yesterday’s loose end, does a non-kosher egg impart flavor at all?

Two threads run through the page:

  1. The egg — Abaye finishes answering Rav Idi bar Avin: an egg only “gives taste” when there’s a chick (efroach) inside; a merely non-kosher egg with no chick is close to tasteless — and yet, the Gemara concludes, even that egg forbids a mixture. Then a whole chain of Sages fixes the egg’s ratio at sixty-one (the disputed point: sixty-one including the egg, or sixty besides it).

  2. The source of nullification — the anchor is a startling Torah case: the cooked foreleg (zeroa besheila) of the nazirite’s ram (Bamidbar 6:19). That foreleg is a priest’s portion, yet the Torah has it cooked together with the whole ram, which stays permitted to the (non-priest) nazirite. From this “permitted thing emerging from a forbidden one” the Sages derive the nullification ratio — sixty (counting meat and bones) or one hundred (meat alone).

Along the way we meet old precedents that permitted mixtures at forty-five and forty-seven to one — a reminder that the fixed “sixty” was still crystallizing.

Walking through the sugya, step by step

98a — Does a non-kosher egg give flavor?

Abaye’s answer (finishing 97b). The egg that imparts flavor is one that contains a chick (beitzat efroach) — the chick is what flavors the dish. A non-kosher egg with no chick barely gives taste, matching the “mere egg-water” proverb.

Rav Idi bar Avin objects from a baraita (Tosefta, Terumot): kosher eggs boiled with non-kosher eggs are all forbidden if there’s enough to impart flavor — so non-kosher eggs do flavor! Abaye: there too it’s a kosher egg with a chick, called “non-kosher” because the chick forbids it.

The reisha/seifa fencing. The Gemara tries to infer from the latter clause (which explicitly mentions a chick) that the first clause has no chick — then reads the latter clause as merely explaining the first. The upshot: the first clause really does speak of an egg without a chick, and it still forbids the mixture. So a non-kosher egg is treated as imparting flavor (nullified in sixty, not counted — as Rav Nachman held on 97b).

98a — Don’t shrink the measure

The olive-bulk of fat. A kezayis of forbidden fat fell into a pot of meat. Rav Ashi (the text’s “Rav Asi”) thought to count what the pot absorbed toward the sixty; the Rabbis pushed back: does the pot absorb only the permitted meat and not the forbidden fat? You can’t treat the absorbed volume as all-permitted.

The half-olive-bulk. A half-kezayis of fat fell in, and Mar bar Rav Ashi reasoned he’d only need thirty half-olive-bulks to nullify it. His father Rav Ashi rebuked him: “Don’t treat rabbinic measures lightly” — and besides, R’ Yochanan holds a half-measure is forbidden by Torah law (chatzi shiur asur min haTorah). So you still need a full sixty times its own volume.

98a — Fixing the egg at sixty-one, and old precedents

The long chain. Rav Shemen bar Abba → Rav Idi bar Idi bar Gershom → Levi bar Perata → R’ Nachum → R’ Biryam → in the name of an elder named R’ Yaakov → the Sages of the Nasi’s house (dvei nesiya): a non-kosher egg is forbidden in sixty, permitted in sixty-one.

R’ Zeira’s probe. He notes to Rav Shemen bar Abba that this sets a firm boundary — yet the two giants R’ Yaakov bar Idi and R’ Shmuel bar Nachmani, both citing R’ Yehoshua ben Levi, left it ambiguous: is “sixty-one” counting the egg, or sixty-one besides it? R’ Chelbo in the name of Rav Huna resolves it: sixty besides it → forbidden; sixty-one besides it → permitted.

The forty-somethings. People brought mixtures to Rabban Gamliel bar Rabbi (permitted at 45-to-1, citing his father’s 47) and to R’ Shimon bar Rabbi (permitted at 43-to-1, citing his father’s 45). But R’ Chiyya rejected one: “Is there even thirty here?!” — and R’ Chanina clarifies that R’ Chiyya’s “thirty” was hyperbole (guzma), not a real thirty-threshold; the mixture plainly lacked even that.

98a–98b — Sixty or a hundred? The nazirite’s ram

The dispute. R’ Chiyya bar Abba citing R’ Yehoshua ben Levi citing bar Kappara: all Torah prohibitions are nullified in sixty. R’ Shmuel bar Rav Yitzchak counters that Rav Asi cites the same chain for one hundred.

Both derive from one verse — “the priest shall take the cooked foreleg (zeroa besheila) of the ram” (Bamidbar 6:19). A baraita reads besheila: the first tanna — a whole foreleg; Rabbi Shimon ben Yochai — one cooked with the entire ram. Since the foreleg belongs to the priest but is cooked in with the ram the nazirite eats, we have a permitted result out of a forbidden ingredient — the model for nullification.

How 60 and 100 split. “Sixty” measures meat and bones of the foreleg against meat-and-bones of the ram (≈1:60); “one hundred” measures meat alone against the ram’s meat (≈1:100).

Is the ram even a valid source? The baraita’s “this is a permitted thing from a forbidden context” seems to exclude all other prohibitions — maybe only the ram nullifies! Abaye: the exclusion is needed only against R’ Yehuda, who holds same-type doesn’t nullify (min b’mino lo batel) — the baraita teaches that here it does. Then: let R’ Yehuda learn from the ram that min b’mino nullifies generally! The Gemara answers that R’ Yehuda’s lo batel comes instead from the blood of the bull and the goat (Vayikra 16:18), which stay distinct though mixed — and we don’t derive general rules from the ram, which is a chidush (you may even nullify it lechatchila). But then why derive the 60/100 measure from a chidush? Because there we learn it as a stringency (l’chumra) — without the verse we’d have said Torah-law nullifies in a mere majority (rov).

Rava’s alternative. The baraita’s “this” is needed for taam k’ikar — in kodashim, absorbed flavor normally forbids; the verse teaches that here the ram stays permitted (even to non-priests).

Who’s who in today’s daf

A big cast today. Title tells: Rav/Rava/Ravina = Babylonian Amora; Rabbi = Tanna or Eretz-Yisrael Amora; “bar Rabbi” / “Rebbi” points to the household of R’ Yehuda HaNasi. Obscure names in the transmission chain are flagged rather than dressed up.

Tannaim (Mishnaic era)

  • R’ Yehuda HaNasi (“Rebbi,” here “Abba”) — final-generation Tanna, redactor of the Mishnah; cited by his sons as the father whose lenient precedents (47-to-1, 45-to-1) they follow.
  • Rabban Gamliel bar Rabbi — son of R’ Yehuda HaNasi, Nasi after him; a transitional Tanna. Permits at 45-to-1.
  • Rabbi Shimon bar Rabbi — another son of R’ Yehuda HaNasi, transitional Tanna; permits at 43-to-1.
  • Rabbi Chiyya (R’ Chiyya the Great) — a transitional Tanna/Amora, disciple-colleague of Rebbi who came from Bavel to Eretz Yisrael; famed for compiling baraitot. Look-alike alert: not the same as R’ Chiyya bar Abba the Amora below.
  • Rabbi Shimon ben Yochai (Rashbi) — 4th-generation Tanna, leading student of R’ Akiva; here he reads besheila as “cooked with the whole ram.”
  • Rabbi Yehuda (bar Ilai) — 4th-generation Tanna, student of R’ Akiva; the “first tanna” in many disputes and the source of min b’mino lo batel.
  • bar Kappara — transitional Tanna/Amora, student of R’ Yehuda HaNasi in Eretz Yisrael, with his own baraita collection; the ultimate source (via R’ Yehoshua ben Levi) of the sixty-vs-hundred rule.

Eretz-Yisrael Amoraim

  • Rabbi Yochanan — R’ Yochanan bar Nappacha, 2nd-gen EY Amora, head of Tiberias; cited for “half-measure is Torah-forbidden.”
  • Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi — 1st-gen EY Amora of Lod (Lydda), a towering figure roughly contemporary with R’ Yochanan; the pivot of the egg and the sixty/hundred traditions.
  • Rabbi Zeira — 3rd-gen Amora who ascended from Bavel to Eretz Yisrael, known for his piety; presses Rav Shemen bar Abba on the sixty-one boundary.
  • Rav Shemen bar Abba — EY Amora, a student of R’ Yochanan in Tiberias; heads the egg-ratio chain. (Sometimes written “Rav Shimon bar Abba.”)
  • Rabbi Yaakov bar Idi — EY Amora (2nd–3rd gen); one of the “two giants” who transmit R’ Yehoshua ben Levi’s egg ruling.
  • Rabbi Shmuel bar Nachmani — 3rd-gen EY Amora, celebrated aggadist, student of R’ Yonatan; the other “giant” in that pair.
  • Rabbi Chiyya bar Abba — 3rd-gen EY Amora, a leading student of R’ Yochanan who transmitted much of his teaching. Look-alike alert: distinct from R’ Chiyya the Tanna above.
  • Rabbi Shmuel bar Rav Yitzchak — 3rd-gen EY Amora; raises the “one hundred” version against R’ Chiyya bar Abba.
  • Rav Asi — 3rd-gen Amora, Babylonian by origin who settled in Eretz Yisrael (the “R’ Ami and R’ Asi” pair); cited here for the hundred-fold measure. Look-alike alert: not Rav Ashi the Babylonian redactor below (and note the pot-incident in 98a:7, printed “Rav Asi,” is really about the Babylonian Rav Ashi).
  • Rabbi Chanina — most likely R’ Chanina bar Chama, 1st-gen EY Amora of Tzippori; explains R’ Chiyya’s “thirty” as guzma. (Same probable identification as on daf 97.)

Babylonian Amoraim

  • Abaye — 4th-gen Babylonian Amora, head of Pumbedita; fields the egg question and the min b’mino derivation. Perennial partner-disputant of Rava.
  • Rava — 4th-gen Babylonian Amora of Machoza; offers the taam k’ikar reading of the baraita.
  • Rav Idi bar Avin — Babylonian Amora, contemporary of Abaye and Rava; objects that non-kosher eggs must impart flavor.
  • Rav Ashi — 6th-gen Babylonian Amora, head of Mata Mechasya (Sura) and principal redactor of the Talmud; rules on the pot-cases and rebukes his son on measures.
  • Mar bar Rav Ashi — a.k.a. Tavyomi, 7th-gen Babylonian Amora, son of Rav Ashi; tries the “thirty half-olives” shortcut and is corrected. Look-alike alert: distinct from Rav Acha bar Rav Ashi (met on 97).
  • Rav Huna — 2nd-gen Babylonian Amora, head of Sura; cited (via R’ Chelbo) that “sixty-one besides it” permits.
  • Rabbi Chelbo — Babylonian Amora who emigrated to Eretz Yisrael, a student of Rav Huna; transmits that ruling.

Uncertain / obscure (transmission chain — details are genuinely thin):

  • Rav Idi bar Idi bar Gershom, Levi bar Perata, Rabbi Nachum, Rabbi Biryam, and “the elder named Rabbi Yaakov” are little-documented links in the egg-ratio chain. Treat them as early Amoraim (mostly Eretz-Yisrael-oriented, given the company they keep) without over-specifying — the Talmud itself introduces R’ Yaakov only as “a certain elder.”

One line to carry with you

The Torah itself modeled nullification — a priest’s portion cooked into the nazirite’s whole ram, and the whole stays permitted — and the Sages’ only argument is the exact ratio: sixty counting the bones, or one hundred counting the meat alone.
