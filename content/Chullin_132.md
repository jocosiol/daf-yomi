## The big picture (read this first)

The daf opens mid-argument. Yesterday closed with **Ulla** handing the priestly gifts to a
*kohenet* — the daughter of a priest — and **Rava** objecting: her meal-offering is eaten, a
kohen's is wholly burned, so surely "kohen" in the Torah means the man and not his daughter.
Today Ulla turns the objection inside out: *mitunakh* — "**from your own burden**." The very verse
that had to spell out "Aaron **and his sons**" for the meal-offering proves that an unqualified
"kohen" would ordinarily have swept the daughters in too. Otherwise, why bother excluding them?

> The move to hold onto: when the Torah goes out of its way to say "males only" in one place, it
> quietly tells you that everywhere it *didn't* say so, the females were included. The exception
> is the proof of the rule.

Three study-halls then line up on the question, and they split three ways on the same word. From
there the daf turns to the **edges of the obligation**: is a hybrid (*kilayim*) or a *koy* — an
animal no one can classify as domesticated or wild — subject to the gifts? The Rabbis and R'
Eliezer disagree, and the Gemara squeezes out a startling middle ruling: **half the gifts**.

Then a **new mishna** (still inside chapter 10) resets the frame to the practical question of the
whole chapter: **who is exempt?** A firstborn lost in a herd, an animal slaughtered for a priest
or a gentile, a partnership, a sale of innards with a gift still inside. The Gemara on it is the
warmest stretch of the daf — **Rabbi Tavla's** scheme to get a poor priest free partnerships,
**Rav Naḥman** dismantling it, the butchers of **Huzal** twenty-two years under the ban, and
**Rav Ḥisda's** rulings on how the gifts are divided and how — like kings — they are eaten.

## Key concepts & terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| מַתְּנוֹת כְּהוּנָּה — *mattenot kehuna* | The priestly gifts; here the foreleg, jaw, and maw owed to the kohen from a non-sacred animal. |
| כֹּהֶנֶת — *kohenet* | A female priest — the daughter of a kohen. The daf's opening question: may she receive the gifts? |
| מִטּוּנָךְ — *mitunakh* | "From your own burden" — I answer your challenge with the very source you raised it from. |
| מִיעוּט אַחַר מִיעוּט — *mi'ut achar mi'ut* | A restriction after a restriction — a doubled exclusion, which by rule serves to *include* rather than exclude. |
| סָתוּם מִן הַמְפוֹרָשׁ — *satum min hameforash* | Learning the unspecified case from the explicit one — here, reading a bare "kohen" in light of "Aaron and his sons." |
| כִּלְאַיִם — *kilayim* | A hybrid — offspring of two species, e.g. a goat crossed with a ewe or with a doe. |
| כּוֹי — *koy* | An animal of unresolved status, sharing traits of both domesticated (*behema*) and wild (*chaya*) animals. |
| שֶׂה וַאֲפִילּוּ מִקְצָת שֶׂה — *seh va'afilu miktzat seh* | "A sheep — even partially a sheep": is a half-domesticated animal bound by a law written for "a sheep"? |
| חוֹשְׁשִׁין לְזֶרַע הָאָב — *choshshin lezera ha'av* | "We are concerned for the father's seed" — does an animal's species follow its sire, or only its dam? |
| חֲצִי מַתָּנוֹת — *chatzi mattanot* | Half the gifts — the compromise the Rabbis are said to require when the species is in doubt. |
| הַדִּין עִם הַטַּבָּח — *hadin im hatabach* | "The claim is with the slaughterer" — the kohen demands the gifts from whoever does the slaughtering, even a priest-butcher. |
| קָבַע מְסַחְתָּא — *kava mesachta* | He set up a butcher's stall — the point at which a priest-butcher becomes obligated without delay. |
| שַׁמְתָּא — *shamta* | Excommunication; the ban Rav Ḥisda pronounces on a priest who withholds the gifts from a fellow priest. |
| לְמׇשְׁחָה — *lemoshcha* | "For prominence" (Num. 18:8) — the gifts are eaten in a manner of greatness: roasted, and with mustard. |

## Who's who in today's daf

**Tannaim**
- **The school of Rabbi Yishmael** — the study-hall of the third-generation Tanna R' Yishmael; reads a bare "kohen" as excluding the daughter.
- **The school of Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov** — a Tannaitic study-hall (the name covers two sages of that name); reads the doubled "kohen" as *including* the daughter.
- **Rabbi Eliezer** (ben Hyrcanus) — second-generation Tanna of Lod, disciple of R' Yoḥanan ben Zakkai; exempts hybrids and the *koy* from the gifts.
- **Rabbi Shimon** (bar Yoḥai) — fourth-generation Tanna, disciple of R' Akiva; his baraita makes belief in the Temple service, not expertise, the test for a priest's share.

**Eretz-Yisrael Amoraim**
- **Ulla** (bar Yishmael) — third-generation Amora who traveled between the land and Babylon; gives the gifts to a female priest.
- **Rabbi Yoḥanan** — second-generation Amora, head of the academy at Tiberias; rules (via Ravin) that a *koy* owes the Rabbis *all* the gifts.
- **Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi** — first-generation Amora of Lod; with the "elders of the south," sets the two-or-three-week grace for a new priest-butcher.
- **Rabbi Aḥa bar Ḥanina** "of the south" — Eretz-Yisrael Amora; carries R' Yehoshua ben Levi's ruling to Babylon.
- **Ravin** and **Rav Yitzḥak bar Yosef** — Amoraim who shuttled teachings from the land to Babylon ("when Ravin came…").

**Babylonian Amoraim**
- **Rava** — fourth-generation Amora of Meḥoza; raises the challenge to Ulla, and teaches "the claim is with the slaughterer." (**Rava** רבא, not **Rabbah** רבה.)
- **Rav Kahana, Rav Pappa, Rav Yeimar, Rav Idi bar Avin** — later Amoraim, each an Israelite who ate the gifts "on account of his wife," a priest's daughter. (This **Rav Kahana** is the later one, a contemporary of Rav Pappa, not Rav's disciple.)
- **Ravina** and **Mareimar** — sixth-generation Amoraim of the Rav Ashi circle; Mareimar transmits the string of final rulings.
- **Rav Adda bar Ahava** — the earlier one, a disciple of Rav; exempts the son of a female Levite from the five-*sela* redemption.
- **Rav Huna bar Ḥiyya** — third-generation Amora, associate of Rav Sheshet; explains the Rabbis to mean "obligated in *half* the gifts."
- **Rabbi Zeira** — third-generation Amora (Babylon-born, emigrated to the land); objects from the *koy* baraita.
- **Rav Oshaya** — an Amora who resolves the firstborn mishna; here best read as the transmitter who narrows the case to a priest's re-sold firstborn.
- **Rabbi Tavla** — Babylonian Amora, contemporary of Rav Naḥman; advises the poor priest to seek partnerships.
- **Rav Naḥman** (bar Yaakov) — third-generation Amora of Neharde'a; overturns Rabbi Tavla's scheme.
- **Rav Ḥisda** — third-generation Amora of Sura; pronounces the ban and rules on dividing and eating the gifts.
- **Rabba bar Rav Sheila** — Babylonian Amora; reports the butchers of Huzal under Rav Ḥisda's ban.
- **Rava** and **Rav Naḥman bar Yitzḥak** — fine a withholder a thigh, and a cloak, respectively (RNbY, fourth generation, head of Pumbedita).
- **Rabba bar bar Ḥana** — Babylonian-born Amora who studied in the land; transmits R' Yoḥanan on eating from an un-tithed animal.

## Walking through the sugya, step by step

### 132a — the *kohenet*, the hybrid, and the *koy*

**Ulla's answer — "from your own burden."** Rava had argued that "kohen" excludes the daughter.
Ulla replies *mitunakh*: the meal-offering passage writes **"Aaron and his sons"** (Lev. 6:7)
precisely because a plain "kohen" would have *included* the daughters — so the very need to
exclude them there proves they are included wherever the Torah does not exclude them. Hand the
gifts to the *kohenet*.

**Three schools, one word.** The Gemara lines up the tannaitic readings of "kohen":
- **School of R' Yishmael:** "kohen" — **and not** *kohenet*; learn the unqualified "kohen" **from
  the explicit** "Aaron and his sons" (*satum min hameforash*). She is excluded.
- **School of R' Eliezer ben Yaakov:** "kohen" — **even** *kohenet*. Deut. 18:3 says "kohen"
  twice, and each exclusion is **a restriction after a restriction, which serves only to
  amplify** (*ein mi'ut achar mi'ut ela lerabot*). She is included.

**And in practice.** **Rav Kahana, Rav Pappa, Rav Yeimar,** and **Rav Idi bar Avin** — Israelites
all — each **ate the gifts "on account of his wife,"** a priest's daughter. **Ravina** reports
**Mareimar's** ruling: the halakha is like **Rav** (Levites' status stays in doubt, so the gifts
are not forced from them), like **Rav Ḥisda** (no payment for damaging the gifts), and like
**Ulla** (the gifts may go to a *kohenet*). And like **Rav Adda bar Ahava**: a **female Levite**
who bears a son — even to an Israelite — that son is **exempt from the five-*sela* redemption**,
counted as a Levite's child.

**The hybrid and the *koy*.** A baraita: the foreleg, jaw, and maw **apply to a hybrid and to a
*koy*.** **R' Eliezer** distinguishes: a hybrid of **goat and ewe** is obligated; of **goat and
doe** (*tzviya*), **exempt** — for the verse says "ox **or sheep**" (Deut. 18:3), a domesticated
animal, and a doe is not one. The Gemara narrows the live dispute to **a deer that mated with a
female goat**: both sides are unsure whether **paternity counts** (*choshshin lezera ha'av*), and
so they really argue over **"a sheep — even partially a sheep."** The Rabbis say partial counts;
R' Eliezer says it does not.

**The half-gift.** If the Rabbis hold "even partially a sheep," why not have the priest **take
half** and demand proof for the rest? **Rav Huna bar Ḥiyya:** that is exactly what "obligated"
means here — **obligated in half the gifts.** **R' Zeira objects** from the *koy* baraita, which
lists a *koy* as **obligated in the gifts** — not "in half." **The answer:** the same baraita
teaches that its **fat and blood** are forbidden, and you cannot forbid "half" a fat or "half" a
blood — so to keep the list uniform, it simply omitted the word "half" for the gifts too.

**R' Yoḥanan's harder line.** When **Ravin came**, he reported **R' Yoḥanan**: for the Rabbis a
*koy* owes **all** the gifts. The proof: "**im** shor" (why the extra "if"?) includes the hybrid;
"**im** seh" includes the *koy*. R' Eliezer needs "im" instead **to divide** — obligation on
either animal alone — and the Rabbis get "to divide" from **"from those who slaughter"**
(*me'et zovchei hazavach*). And R' Eliezer uses *that* phrase for **Rava's** teaching: **the claim
is with the slaughterer** (*hadin im hatabach*).

### 132a (Mishna) — who is exempt from the gifts

The chapter's new mishna gathers the exemptions:
- A **blemished firstborn** (itself exempt) lost among **a hundred** ordinary animals: if a
  **hundred people** each slaughter one, **all are exempt** (each pleads his was the firstborn); if
  **one person** slaughters all, **one** animal is exempt for him.
- One who **slaughters for a priest or for a gentile** is **exempt**. A partner with them **must
  mark** the animal. If the seller said "**except for the gifts**," the buyer is **exempt**.
- "**Sell me a cow's innards**," gifts included: he gives them to the kohen and **does not deduct**
  their value; if he bought **by weight**, he gives them and **does deduct**.

### 132b — Rabbi Tavla, the ban, and the king's table

**Why not claim from both sides?** On the hundred-animal case: let the priest tell each
slaughterer, "**if it is the firstborn, it is all mine; if not, give me my gifts**"! **Rav
Oshaya:** the mishna speaks of a firstborn that already **came to a priest** and was **sold on to
an Israelite with its blemish** — so the priest cannot now claim the whole animal.

**"The claim is with the slaughterer."** Why say "slaughters *for* a priest or gentile" rather
than simply "a priest and a gentile are exempt"? **Rava:** to teach *hadin im hatabach* — a
**priest-butcher slaughtering for an Israelite is obligated.** Rava reads it from the verse:
"**from the people**" — not from the priests — yet "**from those who slaughter**" — even a
priest-butcher is included.

**Rabbi Tavla's scheme.** A poor priest, Rabbi Tavla's host, was told: **partner with the
Israelite butchers** — since the partnership makes *them* exempt from the gifts, they will take
you on for free. **Rav Naḥman obligated** the butcher anyway. "But Rabbi Tavla exempted us!"
— "**Go remove** the gifts, or **I will remove Rabbi Tavla from your ear**" (i.e., refute him).
Rabbi Tavla came to ask why. Rav Naḥman cited **R' Yehoshua ben Levi and the elders of the
south**: a priest-butcher is exempt for his **first two or three weeks**, obligated thereafter.
"Then grant him the weeks!" — "That is only when he **has not set up a stall**; here he
**established a butcher shop** (*kava mesachta*)," so he owes at once.

**The ban of Huzal.** **Rav Ḥisda:** a priest who withholds the gifts — let him be under the
**ban of the God of Israel.** **Rabba bar Rav Sheila:** the butchers of Huzal have been under
Rav Ḥisda's ban **twenty-two years.** For what point? Not "we ban no longer than that" — a baraita
has one **struck until his soul departs** over a neglected positive mitzva (sukka, lulav, tzitzit),
so the ban could stand indefinitely. **Rather:** we **fine them without warning** (*b'lo
atrayta*) — as **Rava** seized a **thigh** and **Rav Naḥman bar Yitzḥak** a **cloak** from
withholders.

**Dividing, and the king's table.** **Rav Ḥisda:** foreleg to one priest, maw to one, jaw split
**to two.** But in the West they **divide bone by bone**, each to two! — There it was **a large
bull**, whose limbs are big enough to satisfy two apiece. **Rabba bar bar Ḥana / R' Yoḥanan:** it
is forbidden to eat from an animal whose gifts are unseparated — like eating *tevalim* — **but the
halakha is not so.** **Rav Ḥisda:** the gifts are eaten **only roasted, only with mustard** — from
"**for prominence**" (*lemoshcha*), the way kings eat. And: withhold a gift from a priest **not
expert** in all twenty-four gifts — but **that is wrong**, for **R' Shimon** makes the test
**belief in the Temple service**, not expertise (the baraita's list of rites runs into 133a).

## The distinctions, side by side

The *koy* is the daf's model of one case weighed on several tests at once — sometimes ruled like a
domesticated animal, sometimes like a wild one, sometimes stringent as both.

| Test applied to the *koy* | Ruled like… | The resulting law |
|---|---|---|
| Its forbidden fat (*chelev*) | domesticated (*behema*) | forbidden to eat |
| Covering its blood after slaughter | wild (*chaya*) | required |
| Its blood and its sciatic nerve | both | forbidden |
| The foreleg, jaw, and maw | domesticated (per the Rabbis) | obligated — R' Eliezer exempts |

## One line to carry with you

> Read the exception as a witness for the rule: because one verse had to say "the sons of Aaron,"
> every other "kohen" was quietly holding the door open for his daughter all along.

- q: How does Ulla answer Rava's objection that "kohen" should exclude a priest's daughter?
  opts:
    - He concedes the point and limits his practice to gifts that a woman happens to be carrying.
    - The very need to write "Aaron and his sons" proves a plain "kohen" would have included daughters.
    - He argues that a female priest is fully equal to a male priest for every law in the Torah.
    - He shows that the gifts were never really a matter of priestly status in the first place.
  correct: b
  why: Mitunakh — the explicit exclusion for the meal-offering proves that an unqualified "kohen" ordinarily includes the daughter.

- q: On what grounds does the school of R' Eliezer ben Yaakov include a female priest in the gifts?
  opts:
    - The word "kohen" is written twice, and a restriction after a restriction serves to amplify.
    - A female priest offers the same meal-offering as a male, so the two are simply equated.
    - The gifts are a monetary matter, and money laws never distinguish between men and women.
    - The verse says "from the people," and a priest's daughter counts among the ordinary people.
  correct: a
  why: Two exclusions ("kohen" … "kohen") make a mi'ut achar mi'ut, which by rule includes rather than excludes — so the daughter is in.

- q: Why did Rav Kahana, Rav Pappa, Rav Yeimar, and Rav Idi bar Avin eat the priestly gifts?
  opts:
    - Each had been formally adopted into a priestly family and so acquired a share in the gifts.
    - Each was himself a kohen who slaughtered his own animals and kept his own gifts.
    - Each ate them on account of his wife, who was the daughter of a priest.
    - Each held that the gifts had lapsed entirely in the absence of the Temple service.
  correct: c
  why: They were Israelites who partook of the gifts "on account of his wife," a kohenet — the practical upshot of Ulla's ruling.

- q: To which single case does the Gemara narrow the live dispute between R' Eliezer and the Rabbis?
  opts:
    - A ewe crossed with a goat, where both parents are plainly domesticated animals.
    - A deer that mated with a female goat, where paternity alone is in question.
    - A koy whose parentage is entirely unknown and cannot be traced at all.
    - A firstborn animal that was lost among a large herd of ordinary animals.
  correct: b
  why: Only the deer-and-female-goat case turns on whether we are "concerned for the father's seed," i.e. whether the sire's species matters.

- q: What does Rav Huna bar Ḥiyya say the Rabbis mean when they call the hybrid "obligated"?
  opts:
    - Obligated to give the entire set of gifts, exactly like an ordinary domesticated animal.
    - Obligated only if the owner cannot bring proof about the animal's paternity.
    - Exempt in practice, since the doubt is never resolved in the priest's favor.
    - Obligated in half the gifts, the priest taking half and leaving the rest in doubt.
  correct: d
  why: With the species in doubt, the priest takes half; "obligated" is shorthand for chatzi mattanot.

- q: How does the Gemara explain why the koy baraita says "obligated" rather than "obligated in half"?
  opts:
    - Because the baraita follows R' Eliezer, who exempts the koy from the gifts entirely.
    - Because a koy, unlike a hybrid, is fully domesticated and owes the whole gift.
    - Because the same list forbids its fat and blood, which cannot be split into halves.
    - Because R' Yoḥanan later corrected the baraita's wording to read "in half."
  correct: c
  why: Since fat and blood cannot be "half" forbidden, the tanna kept the list uniform and omitted "half" for the gifts as well.

- q: What does Rava derive from the phrase "from those who slaughter" (me'et zovchei hazavach)?
  opts:
    - That the obligation applies only once an ox and a sheep are both slaughtered together.
    - That the claim is with the slaughterer — even a priest-butcher slaughtering for an Israelite owes the gifts.
    - That a gentile who slaughters for an Israelite is also bound to give the gifts.
    - That the gifts may be claimed from the animal's owner rather than from the one who slaughters it.
  correct: b
  why: Hadin im hatabach — "from the people, not the priests, yet from those who slaughter" folds even a priest-butcher into the obligation.

- q: Why did Rav Naḥman reject Rabbi Tavla's advice to the poor priest, even for the first weeks?
  opts:
    - Because the priest had already established a butcher shop, so he owed the gifts at once.
    - Because a priest may never enter a business partnership with an Israelite butcher.
    - Because Rabbi Tavla lacked the standing to exempt anyone from a Torah obligation.
    - Because the grace period applies only to priests who slaughter for gentiles.
  correct: a
  why: The two-or-three-week grace holds only for one who has not set up a stall; here he kava mesachta and is obligated immediately.

- q: According to Rav Ḥisda, how are the priestly gifts to be eaten?
  opts:
    - Boiled and unsalted, in the plain manner of the ordinary Israelite household.
    - Only by a priest expert in all twenty-four gifts, and only within the Temple courtyard.
    - Roasted and with mustard, in the manner of kings, from the word "for prominence."
    - Raw and immediately, so that none of the priest's portion is left to spoil.
  correct: c
  why: The word lemoshcha — "for greatness" — teaches they are eaten as kings eat, roasted and with mustard.
