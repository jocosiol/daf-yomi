## The big picture (read this first)

Today's page is a hinge. It **closes the book on blood** — the last of the *tereifa*-juices sugya, and then
Shmuel's practical laws of *melichah*, the salting every kitchen still uses to draw blood out of meat — and
then it **opens the book on meat-and-milk**, the long sugya that will run for pages: what exactly the Torah
forbids when it says *"you shall not cook a kid in its mother's milk."*

> **הָא תְּלָתָא ״גְּדִי״ כְּתִיבִי, וַאֲנַן שִׁיתָּא דָּרְשִׁינַן!** "The word *kid* is written only **three**
> times, yet we expound **six** laws from it!" Shmuel reads each mention of *gedi* as both an inclusion and
> an exclusion — stretching the prohibition to reach forbidden fat, a carcass, a fetus, and pulling it back
> from blood, the placenta, the non-kosher animal. The daf is about **how far a single word reaches.**

Two questions run underneath the whole page:

1. **How does a forbidden flavor get in?** Rava had ruled that a *tereifa*'s very juices forbid kosher meat
   salted beside it. The daf opens by asking why he needed a verse for that — and a *baraita* about salting
   a kosher fish with a non-kosher one becomes the testing-ground (113a).
2. **Can a prohibition land where one already sits?** *Issur chal al issur* — does the ban on meat-in-milk
   take hold on forbidden fat, which is already forbidden to eat? This drives Shmuel's "six from three" and
   the closing dispute of Rabbi Ami and Rabbi Asi (113b).

In between sit Shmuel's melichah rulings — salt and rinse, a perforated vessel, the intestines that hold no
blood, and the man who breaks the animal's neck to trap blood in the meat and sell you water by weight.

## Key concepts & terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **מָלִיחַ / תָּפֵל** *(maliach / tafel)* | **Salted** versus **unsalted.** The fish *baraita* turns entirely on which fish was salted: an unsalted (*tafel*) fish emits no juice, so it cannot pass flavor to its neighbor. |
| **פָּרוֹשֵׁי קָא מְפָרֵשׁ** *(paroshei ka mefaresh)* | "**It is explaining.**" A way to read a *baraita*'s later clause not as a new case but as a gloss on the first — here, so the opening "permitted" is not contradicted by what follows. |
| **מוֹלֵחַ וּמֵדִיחַ** *(moleach u'meidiach)* | "**Salt and rinse.**" Shmuel's rule that meat is rid of its blood only by thorough salting and thorough rinsing; a *baraita* adds a rinse *before* — rinse, salt, rinse. |
| **אֵין מַחֲזִיקִין דָּם בִּבְנֵי מֵעַיִים** *(ein machzikin dam)* | "**We do not presume blood in the intestines.**" Rav Mesharshiya: the rectum, intestines, and spiral colon are not assumed to hold blood, so they are not forbidden if unsalted. |
| **מַכְבִּיד אֶת הַבָּשָׂר** *(machbid et habasar)* | "**Makes the meat heavy.**" Breaking the animal's neck before it dies traps blood in the limbs — weighing the meat down, so the seller **robs people** (*gozel et habriyot*) who pay by weight. |
| **גְּדִי** *(gedi)* | The "**kid**" of *"you shall not cook a kid in its mother's milk."* Shmuel reads each of its three mentions to both include a case (fat, carcass, fetus) and exclude one (blood, placenta, non-kosher). |
| **בַּחֲלֵב אִמּוֹ** *(ba'chalev imo)* | "**In its mother's milk.**" Its three mentions exclude the milk of a male, of a slaughtered animal, and of a non-kosher animal — and, for Rabbi Yosei HaGelili, exclude birds, which have no mother's milk. |
| **חַיָּה וָעוֹף** *(chaya va'of)* | An **undomesticated animal and a bird.** Rabbi Akiva holds their meat-in-milk is forbidden only by rabbinic law, since the threefold "kid" excludes the *chaya*, the bird, and the non-kosher animal. |
| **שְׁנֵי כְתוּבִים הַבָּאִים כְּאֶחָד** *(shnei ketuvim ha'ba'im k'echad)* | "**Two verses that come as one.**" Two verses teaching the same thing — by one view they cannot teach it further, so "kid of the goats" does not fix "kid" as a goat everywhere. |
| **אִיסּוּר חָל עַל אִיסּוּר** *(issur chal al issur)* | "**A prohibition taking effect on a prohibition.**" Whether a new ban lands on an already-forbidden item — the axis of Shmuel's "six from three" and of the Rabbi Ami / Rabbi Asi dispute at the end. |
| **תֵּיקוּ** *(teiku)* | The **unresolved dilemma**: is the neck-breaker's fault only robbery — so for one's *own* meat it is fine — or is the trapped blood itself a prohibition even then? Left standing. |

## Who's who in today's daf

**Tannaim**

- **Rabbi Akiva** — third-generation Tanna, of Bnei Brak; holds that meat of an undomesticated animal or a
  bird cooked in milk is forbidden only rabbinically, deriving the exclusions from the threefold "kid."
- **Rabbi Yosei HaGelili** — third-generation Tanna, a contemporary and frequent disputant of Rabbi Akiva;
  proves that a bird is excluded from Torah-level meat-in-milk, since it "has no mother's milk."
- **Rabbi Chiyya** (the Elder) — a transitional figure between Tannaim and Amoraim, born in Babylonia,
  moved to Eretz Yisrael, colleague-disciple of Rebbi; his ruling on breaking the neck is transmitted here
  by Shmuel. (Rabbi Chiyya **the Elder** — not to be confused with Rabbi Chiyya bar Abba, a later Amora.)

**Babylonian Amoraim**

- **Rav** (Abba Arikha) — first generation, founder of Sura; fields Rav Aḥadvoi's question about the milk of
  a goat that has not yet nursed.
- **Shmuel** (of Nehardea) — first generation, Rav's great colleague; the dominant voice of the daf — the
  melichah rulings, the six derashot of "kid," and the teaching (in the name of Rabbi Elazar) about the
  impure priest and *teruma*.
- **Rabbi Elazar** — quoted twice, and the two are almost certainly different men. The **Rabbi Elazar** whom
  *Shmuel cites* on impure *teruma* is an earlier authority (I would not pin the exact identity down); the
  **Rabbi Elazar** who derives the meat-milk scope from *"and Judah sent the kid"* is the Eretz-Yisrael
  Amora Rabbi Elazar ben Pedat.
- **Rav Huna** — second generation, head of Sura after Rav; rules "salt and rinse."
- **Rav Dimi of Neharde'a** — a late Babylonian authority (later head of Pumbedita); would salt with coarse
  salt and then shake it off. (This is **Rav Dimi of Neharde'a** — distinct from the *Rav Dimi* who "went up
  and came" carrying Eretz-Yisrael traditions.)
- **Rav Sheshet** — third generation, a blind Amora renowned for his mastery of tradition; would salt one
  piece of meat at a time, prompting the Gemara's question about whether pieces absorb from one another.
- **Rav Mesharshiya** — later Amora, a student of Rava; rules that no blood is presumed in the intestines.
- **Rava** (bar Yosef bar Ḥama) — fourth generation, of Meḥoza; the opening sugya continues his ruling from
  yesterday, and the daf explains why he needed the verse "the unclean." (רבא; not **Rabba**, רבה.)
- **Rav Aḥadvoi bar Ami** — a minor Amora; asked Rav about cooking meat in the milk of a goat about to give
  birth. Beyond this exchange I would not claim much of his biography.

**Eretz-Yisrael Amoraim**

- **Rabbi Ami** and **Rabbi Asi** — third generation, the leading pair of Tiberias, often ruling together;
  here they split over whether one is flogged for cooking forbidden fat in milk. (Rabbi **Asi** — with
  *Rabbi* — the Eretz-Yisrael Amora; not **Rav Asi** the Babylonian, nor **Rav Ashi** the redactor.)

## Walking through the sugya, step by step

### 113a — Why a verse, not Shmuel's own rule

The daf opens still inside yesterday's case: kosher meat salted (in a perforated vessel) beside a *tereifa*,
which Rava forbade by reading *"the unclean"* to include the *tereifa*'s juice and gravy. **But why did Rava
need a verse at all?** He could have cited Shmuel's own principle:

> **מָלִיחַ הֲרֵי הוּא כְּרוֹתֵחַ, וְכָבוּשׁ הֲרֵי הוּא כִּמְבוּשָּׁל.** "A **salted** food is like a **boiling**
> one, and a **marinated** food is like a **cooked** one." Surely the salted kosher meat absorbed the
> *tereifa*'s flavor as if they had been cooked together!

**The answer.** Had Rava leaned only on Shmuel, we would have limited the ruling: *maliach k'roteach* would
teach only that the meat absorbs **blood** — but here, salted in a *perforated* vessel, the blood runs out,
and one might think the kosher meat stays permitted. So Rava reached for the verse to teach that even the
**juices and gravy** (*tziran v'rotvan*) are forbidden, blood or no blood.

**The fish *baraita*.** An objection: *A kosher fish salted with a non-kosher fish is permitted.* If both
were salted, the kosher fish should have absorbed the non-kosher juices — against Rava! The Gemara answers:
**no** — the case is a **salted** (*maliach*) kosher fish with an **unsalted** (*tafel*) non-kosher one, which
emits no juice at all.

The *baraita* is then turned over three times:

- **The inference from the second clause.** "*But if the kosher was salted and the non-kosher unsalted*" —
  stating this separately implies the *first* clause meant **both salted**! The Gemara answers: the second
  clause is not a new case — **פָּרוֹשֵׁי קָא מְפָרֵשׁ**, "it is *explaining*" the first: *how* is a kosher fish
  permitted? When it alone was salted.
- **A support, then its collapse.** *So it stands to reason* — if the first clause meant both salted, why
  bother stating the easier case? But this is rejected: perhaps the second clause was taught precisely **to
  shed light** on the first, so you would *not* wrongly restrict it.
- **The third clause.** *"But if the non-kosher was salted and the kosher unsalted — forbidden."* This
  implies that only *that* combination is forbidden, but **both salted is permitted** — against Rava again!
  Rejected: the third clause merely mirrors the language of the second; nothing about "both salted" can be
  drawn from it.

### 113a — Shmuel's laws of salting meat

A mnemonic (*siman*) — *"meat that is placed, departs"* — marks three of Shmuel's rulings:

**Salt and rinse.** **Shmuel:** *meat is not rid of its blood unless one salts it thoroughly and rinses it
thoroughly.* **Rav Huna** says: salt, then rinse. A *baraita* teaches: **rinse, salt, and rinse again.** And
the two do not disagree — the *baraita* speaks of meat **not yet washed** in the slaughterhouse; Rav Huna, of
meat already washed there. **Rav Dimi of Neharde'a** would salt with coarse salt (*milcha gelalanita*) and
then shake it off.

**The intestines.** **Rav Mesharshiya:** *we do not presume blood in the intestines* — glossed as the
rectum, the intestines, and the spiral colon; they need no salting on that account.

**A perforated vessel.** **Shmuel:** *salted meat may be placed only on a perforated vessel,* so the expelled
blood drains away rather than pooling and being reabsorbed.

**One piece at a time.** **Rav Sheshet** would salt meat one piece at a time. Why not two together — lest the
blood *leaves one and the other absorbs it*? But then even one piece should be forbidden — the blood leaves
one side and the other side absorbs! **Rather there is no difference**, and one may salt several pieces at
once.

**Breaking the neck.** **Shmuel, in the name of Rabbi Chiyya:** one who **breaks the animal's neck** before
its soul has departed —

> **הֲרֵי זֶה מַכְבִּיד אֶת הַבָּשָׂר, וְגוֹזֵל אֶת הַבְּרִיּוֹת, וּמַבְלִיעַ דָּם בָּאֵבָרִים.** "makes the
> meat **heavy**, **robs people**, and causes **blood to be absorbed in the limbs.**" The blood that would
> have drained at slaughter is trapped inside, so a buyer paying by weight pays for blood as if it were meat.

A dilemma (*ibaya lehu*): is Shmuel naming only the wrong of *robbery* — so that for one's **own** meat the
practice is fine — or is the trapped blood *itself* a prohibition, forbidden even for oneself? **תֵּיקוּ** —
it stands unresolved.

### 113a–113b — The meat-and-milk mishnayot

**Mishna (bird on the table).** *One who places bird-meat with cheese on the table does not violate a Torah
prohibition.* The Gemara infers: then *eating* them together **would** violate one — so bird-in-milk is
Torah law, against Rabbi Akiva! It re-reads the mishna: one who puts them on the table *will not come to* a
Torah prohibition — since eating them is only rabbinic, as Rabbi Akiva holds.

**Mishna (the combinations).** Kosher meat in kosher milk — **forbidden** to cook and to benefit from.
Kosher meat in **non-kosher** milk, or non-kosher meat in kosher milk — **permitted** to cook and to benefit
from. **Rabbi Akiva:** undomesticated animal and bird are **not** Torah-level, for the threefold *"kid"*
excludes the *chaya*, the bird, and the non-kosher animal. **Rabbi Yosei HaGelili:** *"you shall not eat any
carcass"* stands beside *"you shall not cook a kid in its mother's milk"* — what is forbidden as a carcass is
forbidden to cook in milk; a bird might therefore be forbidden, but *"in its mother's milk"* excludes it, for
a bird has no mother's milk.

**Gemara — from where?** **Rabbi Elazar** (ben Pedat): the verse *"and Judah sent the kid of the goats"*
(Gen. 38:20) —

### 113b — Six laws from three "kids"

— specifies *the goats* here, which implies that **everywhere else "kid" alone includes even a cow or a ewe.**
Why not derive the opposite, that "kid" always means a goat? Because a second verse, *"the skins of the kids
of the goats"* (Gen. 27:16), specifies goats too — and **two verses that come as one do not teach** their
common point further. (And for the view that they *do* teach, two extra exclusions — *"the goats"* with the
definite article — bar it.)

Then Shmuel's great derashah. Each of the three *"kid"*s does double duty:

> **״גְּדִי״ – לְרַבּוֹת... ״גְּדִי״ – לְהוֹצִיא...** "*Kid* — to **include**... *kid* — to **exclude**..."

*Kid* includes forbidden **fat**, a dead **carcass**, and a **fetus**; and excludes **blood**, the
**placenta**, and the **non-kosher** animal. And the three *"in its mother's milk"*s exclude the milk of a
**male**, of a **slaughtered** animal, and of a **non-kosher** animal.

**Six from three?** The Gemara protests: only three *"kid"*s are written, yet we expound six laws. The
answer: **Shmuel holds a prohibition takes effect on a prohibition**, so fat and carcass come from a single
verse (both are already-forbidden items on which the milk-ban lands). Blood is **not called "kid"** at all,
and a placenta is a **mere secretion** — neither needs its own verse. That leaves two "kid"s: one to include
the fetus, one to exclude the non-kosher animal.

**But does Shmuel hold that?** He also said, in Rabbi Elazar's name, that an impure priest who eats **impure
*teruma*** is **not** liable to death — *"and die therein"* excludes what is *already desecrated* — which
sounds like a prohibition **cannot** land on a prohibition. Three answers:

1. **Generally he holds it does**, and *teruma* is different because the verse *expressly excludes* the
   already-desecrated case.
2. **Generally he holds it does not**, and meat-in-milk is different because the verse *expressly includes*
   (the repeated "kid").
3. The meat-milk teaching is **his own** view; the *teruma* teaching is **his teacher's** (Rabbi Elazar's).

**Milk of a goat not yet nursed.** **Rav Aḥadvoi bar Ami asked Rav:** what of cooking in the milk of a goat
that has not yet nursed but is about to give birth? Rav: since Shmuel needed *"in its mother's milk"* to
exclude a **male** — because a male can **never** attain motherhood — it follows that this goat, which
**will** become a mother, is already **forbidden**.

**Fat in milk.** It was stated: one who cooks forbidden **fat in milk** — **Rabbi Ami and Rabbi Asi** split,
one saying he is **flogged**, one saying he is **not**. Do they argue about *issur chal al issur*? **No:** all
agree it does not take effect, and all agree there is no flogging for **eating**. They argue about
**cooking** — the one who says *flogged* holds cooking is a **single** prohibition (fat may be cooked
without eating, so the milk-ban is the only ban here); the one who says *not flogged* holds the Torah
expressed *eating* in the *language of cooking* — a thread that runs straight into tomorrow's daf.

## The distinctions, side by side

The heart of 113b is Shmuel's reading of the verse: three mentions of *"kid,"* each pulling the prohibition
one way and pushing it back another, plus three of *"in its mother's milk."* The grid is the sugya:

| Mention | *"Kid"* — includes | *"Kid"* — excludes | *"In its mother's milk"* — excludes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **First** | forbidden **fat** (*chelev*) | **blood** (*dam*) | milk of a **male** |
| **Second** | a dead **carcass** (*meta*) | the **placenta** (*shilya*) | milk of a **slaughtered** animal |
| **Third** | a **fetus** (*shelil*) | a **non-kosher** animal (*temeia*) | milk of a **non-kosher** animal |

That is nine readings from a verse mentioned three times — which is exactly why the Gemara asks how three
words yield six laws, and answers with *issur chal al issur* and the "blood is not a kid, placenta is a
secretion" trimming.

## One line to carry with you

> One word, *gedi*, said three times, is stretched to catch forbidden fat, a carcass, and a fetus — and
> pulled back from blood, placenta, and the non-kosher beast. The whole meat-and-milk prohibition is drawn
> out of how far a single word can reach.
