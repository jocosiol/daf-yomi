## The big picture (read this first)

Yesterday's clean halakhic engine — *kanus b'me'av*, the udder permitted and the stomach forbidden —
gives way here to something the Talmud does just as seriously: it tells you **how a ruling actually
happened**. The stringent line "a roasted udder, untorn, is forbidden" was hung on Rav, and the daf goes
looking for its source. The answer is startling: Rav was never *taught* it. He **built the fence
himself**, in a place where people did not even know meat-and-milk was forbidden.

> **בִּקְעָה מָצָא וְגָדַר בָּהּ גָּדֵר** — "He found an open valley and fenced it in." A decree does not
> always descend from Sinai through a chain of *tannaim*. Sometimes a teacher walks into a town, hears a
> woman ask *how much milk it takes to cook a quarter of meat*, and understands that here, now, the
> udder itself must be forbidden — not because it is milk, but because these people will otherwise cook
> real meat in real milk.

From there the page becomes a string of table-side stories, each carrying a *halakha* inside it — trust
in a pious household, the stringencies a traveler must carry, libation wine, lost property, tefillin and
tzitzit and who is exempt — and it ends by turning to a new organ, the **liver**, and the question that
will run into tomorrow: it bleeds into everything around it, so does it forbid the meat it is cooked
with?

Three movements:

1. **Where Rav's stringency came from** (110a) — Rabbi Elazar's inquiry, the "valley and fence," Tatlefush,
   and the two versions of what Rav Yitzḥak bar Avudimi actually taught him.
2. **Rami bar Tamrei** (110a–110b) — Sura against Pumbedita, the Yom-Kippur-eve udders, and a volley of
   clever defenses before Rav Chisda.
3. **The liver opens** (110b) — Abaye's dilemma: liver forbids others but is not itself forbidden, and
   why the *Terumot* mishna does not settle it.

## Key concepts & terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **בִּקְעָה מָצָא וְגָדַר בָּהּ גָּדֵר** *(bik'a matza v'gadar ba gader)* | "He found an open valley and fenced it in" — the image for a sage who imposes a protective decree precisely where he finds a community lax, as Rav did with the udder in Tatlefush. |
| **תְּנָא נָמֵי רֵישָׁא** *(tena nami reisha)* | "The *tanna* stated the opening clause the same way too" — here, phrasing the permitted udder as "one *cooked*" only for symmetry with the forbidden stomach-clause, not to limit it to after-the-fact. |
| **חוּמְרֵי הַמָּקוֹם** *(chumrei hamakom)* | "The stringencies of the place" — a traveler adopts the strict customs both of the place he left and of the place he enters; Rami escaped this only by eating outside Sura's boundary. |
| **תְּחוּם** *(techum)* | The **boundary** around a town; beyond it, Rami argued, Sura's local custom against udders no longer bound him. |
| **יֵין נֶסֶךְ** *(yayin nesech)* | **Libation wine** poured for idolatry — forbidden even to derive benefit from; the grape seeds might have been soaked in it, but after twelve months any residue is gone. |
| **פּוּרְצָנֵי** *(purtzenei)* | **Grape seeds / pressings** left after winemaking, which Rami used as roasting fuel — the trigger for the libation-wine and theft challenges. |
| **יֵאוּשׁ בְּעָלִים** *(ye'ush be'alim)* | The **owners' despair** of recovering lost or abandoned property; once they give up (here, grass grew among the seeds), a finder may keep it. |
| **חוֹלֵי מֵעַיִין** *(cholei me'ayin)* | One with an **intestinal illness**; Rav Yehuda ruled him exempt from tefillin, which require a clean body he cannot keep. |
| **טַלִּית שְׁאוּלָה** *(tallit she'ula)* | A **borrowed robe**; per Rav Yehuda, it is exempt from ritual fringes for the first **thirty days** of the loan. |
| **מִצְוָה שֶׁמַּתַּן שְׂכָרָהּ בְּצִדָּהּ** *(mitzva shematan secharah b'tzidah)* | A **positive mitzva whose reward is stated beside it** in the verse — such as honoring parents; the earthly court is not charged with enforcing it by punishment. |
| **פּוֹלֶטֶת וְאֵינָהּ בּוֹלַעַת** *(poletet v'eina bola'at)* | "**Expels but does not absorb**" — the *Terumot* mishna's reason that liver forbids other food cooked with it (its blood diffuses out) yet is never itself forbidden. |

## Who's who in today's daf

**Tannaitic / transitional**

- **Rav Chiyya** (Rabbi Chiyya the Great) — a *tanna* of the generation bridging Mishna and Gemara,
  uncle and teacher of Rav; invoked here for his "sharp mind," which supposedly let a narrow teaching be
  given to Rav unspecified. (Titled *Rabbi* Chiyya as a rule; not to be confused with the Amora **Rabbi
  Chiyya bar Abba**.)

**Eretz-Yisrael Amoraim**

- **Rabbi Elazar** (ben Pedat) — third generation, leading student of Rabbi Yoḥanan; opens the inquiry
  into Rav's source on ascending to Eretz Yisrael. (Not Rabbi Eliezer the Tanna, nor Rabbi Elazar ben
  Shamua.)
- **Rabbi Yitzḥak Nappaḥa** — third generation; named only as the father of Rav Pappi's wife and "a
  master of good deeds," the guarantor of her kitchen.
- **Rav Zerika** — third/fourth generation; reports to Rav Safra that he cooked liver for Rabbi Ami.
- **Rabbi Ami** — third generation, a leading authority in Tiberias after Rabbi Yoḥanan; ate the liver
  Rav Zerika cooked.

**Babylonian Amoraim**

- **Rav** (Abba Arikha) — first generation, founder of Sura; the stringency on the udder is traced to
  him, whether as his own fence or as an over-generalized teaching.
- **Ze'eiri** — first/second generation, a Babylonian who settled in Eretz Yisrael; directs Rabbi Elazar
  to the source of Rav's teaching.
- **Rav Yitzḥak bar Avudimi** — early Amora; the *tanna* said to have taught (or not taught) Rav about
  the udder. A relatively minor figure; I would not press details of his biography.
- **Rav Kahana** — a common name across generations; here simply the tradent of the first version. Which
  Rav Kahana is not fixed, so I leave it open.
- **Rav Yosei bar Abba** — a minor tradent, source of the second version of the teaching; little is known
  of him beyond such transmissions.
- **Ravin** (Rav Avin) — fourth generation, famous for carrying Eretz-Yisrael traditions down to Babylonia
  (*ki ata Ravin*); declines the udder at Rav Pappi's until reassured. (Written רבין; distinct from Rabin
  vs. the many "Ravina"s.)
- **Rav Yitzḥak bar Yosef** — fourth generation, another Eretz-Yisrael-to-Babylonia tradent; eats the
  udder without hesitation.
- **Rav Pappi** — fourth/fifth generation, student of Rava; host of the udder dish, married to Rabbi
  Yitzḥak Nappaḥa's daughter.
- **Abaye** — fourth generation, head of Pumbedita; reassures Ravin and later frames the liver dilemma.
- **Rami bar Tamrei** (= **Rami bar Dikulei**) — a Pumbeditan folk-sage; the hero of the daf's central
  story, who out-argues Rav Chisda point by point.
- **Rav Yehuda** (bar Yeḥezkel) — second generation, founder of Pumbedita; his rulings (intestinal
  illness and tefillin, borrowed robe and fringes) are Rami's authorities. (This **Rav** Yehuda the
  Amora, not **Rabbi** Yehuda bar Ilai the Tanna.)
- **Rav Chisda** — third generation, of Sura; the judge who cross-examines Rami and concedes his
  sharpness.
- **Rav Safra** — third/fourth generation, a merchant-scholar who traveled between Babylonia and Eretz
  Yisrael; Abaye's messenger for the liver question.

## Walking through the sugya, step by step

### 110a — The baraita, and where Rav's stringency was born

The daf opens by tidying the baraita from yesterday: an udder **cooked in its own milk is permitted**,
a suckling calf's **stomach cooked in its milk is forbidden** — and the *tanna* phrased the udder-clause
too as "one *cooked*" (past tense, as if only *after the fact*) merely **for symmetry** (*tena nami
reisha*) with the forbidden stomach-clause. It is permitted even *ab initio*.

Now the search for Rav's source. **Rabbi Elazar**, on ascending to Eretz Yisrael, found **Ze'eiri** and
asked: is there a *tanna* who taught Rav that a roasted, untorn udder is forbidden? Ze'eiri pointed him
to **Rav Yitzḥak bar Avudimi**, who answered:

> **אֲנִי לֹא שָׁנִיתִי לוֹ כְּחָל כׇּל עִיקָּר, וְרַב בִּקְעָה מָצָא וְגָדַר בָּהּ גָּדֵר.**
> "I never taught him about the udder at all — Rav found an open valley and fenced it in."

**The Tatlefush story explains the fence.** Rav came to Tatlefush and overheard one woman ask another,
"*how much milk does it take to cook a quarter-weight of meat?*" He understood: these people **do not
know that meat cooked in milk is forbidden**. So he stayed on and forbade them **even udders** — a fence
around a community that would otherwise stumble into the Torah prohibition itself.

**Two versions of the teaching.** So says **Rav Kahana**. But **Rav Yosei bar Abba** transmits Rav
Yitzḥak bar Avudimi differently: "I *did* teach him — but only that the udder **of a nursing animal** is
forbidden, since it is full of milk. And because of the **sharp mind of Rav Chiyya** [Rav's teacher], he
taught it to Rav as an *unspecified* udder" — trusting Rav to understand the limitation on his own. Rav
took it as applying to **every** udder. On this reading Rav did not invent the stringency; he
**over-generalized** a narrower one.

**A dish you can trust.** **Ravin** and **Rav Yitzḥak bar Yosef** came to **Rav Pappi's** house and were
served a dish of udder. Rav Yitzḥak bar Yosef ate; Ravin did not. **Abaye** chided him: "Bereaved Ravin,
why won't you eat? Rav Pappi's wife is the daughter of **Rabbi Yitzḥak Nappaḥa**, a *master of good
deeds* — had she not seen this dish permitted in her father's house, she would never have made it." The
practice of a trustworthy, learned home is itself a warrant.

### 110a — Rami bar Tamrei in Sura

> **בְּסוּרָא לָא אָכְלִי כַּחְלֵי, בְּפוּמְבְּדִיתָא אָכְלִי כַּחְלֵי.**
> In **Sura they would not eat udders** at all; in **Pumbedita they would.**

Enter **Rami bar Tamrei** (also called **Rami bar Dikulei**), a Pumbeditan, who arrived in Sura **on the
eve of Yom Kippur**. As everyone slaughtered for the pre-fast meals, the Surans **threw their udders
away**. Rami went, gathered them, roasted them, and ate. Hauled before **Rav Chisda**, he faced a chain
of challenges — and answered every one:

**On eating Sura's udders.** *You should have taken on the stringency of the place you visited.* — "I ate
them **outside the boundary** (*techum*) of Sura," where its custom does not reach.

**On the fuel.** *With what did you roast them?* — "With grape seeds (*purtzenei*)." *Perhaps they were
from libation wine (**yayin nesech**), forbidden even in benefit!* — "They lay there **more than twelve
months**," by which time any prohibited liquid is gone.

**On taking the seeds.** *Perhaps they were stolen (**gezel**)!* — "The owners had **despaired**
(*ye'ush be'alim*) — grass was already growing among them," so they were his to take.

### 110b — Tefillin, tzitzit, and the sharp guest

Rav Chisda then noticed what Rami was *not* wearing.

**No tefillin.** — "I suffer from **intestinal illness** (*cholei me'ayin*), and Rav Yehuda ruled such a
person is exempt from tefillin," which demand a clean body he cannot maintain.

**No tzitzit.** — "It is a **borrowed robe**, and Rav Yehuda ruled that a borrowed robe is **exempt from
fringes for the first thirty days**."

**Meanwhile**, the attendants brought in a man **who would not honor his father and mother** and tied him
up to be flogged. Rami intervened:

> **כׇּל מִצְוַת עֲשֵׂה שֶׁמַּתַּן שְׂכָרָהּ בְּצִדָּהּ — אֵין בֵּית דִּין שֶׁלְּמַטָּה מוּזְהָרִין עָלֶיהָ.**
> "Any positive mitzva whose **reward is stated beside it** — the earthly court below is not charged with
> enforcing it."

Honoring parents carries its reward in the verse ("*that your days may be long*"), so the human court
does not flog for its breach. Rav Chisda said, "**I see you are very sharp**." Rami shot back: "If you
were in **Rav Yehuda's** place, I would show you sharpness far greater than this."

### 110b — The liver question opens

**Abaye** had primed **Rav Safra** before his journey: "When you go up there, ask them — **liver, how do
you treat it?**" In Eretz Yisrael Rav Safra found **Rav Zerika**, who said: "I cooked liver for **Rabbi
Ami** and he ate it." But when Rav Safra reported this, Abaye brushed it aside:

> **לְמֵיסַר נַפְשַׁהּ לָא קָא מִיבַּעְיָא לִי, כִּי קָמִבַּעְיָא לִי — לְמֵיסַר חֲבִירְתַּהּ.**
> "Whether liver forbids **itself** is not my question — of course it does not. My question is whether it
> forbids **the piece cooked with it**."

**Rav Safra pushes back.** If the *Terumot* mishna already tells you liver **is not** rendered forbidden
by its own cooking — *that* is why you don't ask about itself — then the **same mishna** should answer the
other half too:

> **הַכָּבֵד אוֹסֶרֶת וְאֵינָהּ נֶאֱסֶרֶת, מִפְּנֵי שֶׁהִיא פּוֹלֶטֶת וְאֵינָהּ בּוֹלַעַת.**
> "Liver **forbids** but is **not forbidden**, because it **expels** and does not **absorb**."

If it forbids others, your dilemma is solved — the mishna says so. **Abaye's answer:** perhaps that
mishna speaks of **forbidden liver** (the liver of a non-kosher animal or one that was not properly
handled) — whose expelled blood truly forbids its neighbor — while *permitted* liver's blood may behave
differently. And there the daf breaks off, the question live, to be taken up tomorrow.

## The distinctions, side by side

The heart of 110a–b is not a matrix of foods but of **defenses**: five times something Rami bar Tamrei
did looked forbidden, and five times he named the exact rule that cleared him.

| The act | Why it looked forbidden | Rami's answer |
|---|---|---|
| **Ate Sura's udders** | A traveler takes on the stringencies of the place he enters | He ate them **outside the *techum*** of Sura |
| **Roasted with grape seeds** | They might be from **libation wine** (benefit forbidden) | They were **over twelve months** old — the prohibition had lapsed |
| **Took the grape seeds** | They might be **stolen** property | The owners had **despaired** — grass grew among them |
| **Wore no tefillin** | Tefillin are obligatory | **Intestinal illness** exempts (Rav Yehuda) |
| **Wore no tzitzit** | Fringes are obligatory | A **borrowed robe** is exempt for **thirty days** (Rav Yehuda) |

## One line to carry with you

> A decree is not always handed down; sometimes a teacher walks into a valley, sees where the fence has
> fallen, and builds one — so that people who do not yet know the law will not fall through the gap.
