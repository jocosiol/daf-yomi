## The big picture (read this first)

Two dapim of washing law reach their end here, and then the chapter finally turns to the subject its
name promises. *Kol HaBasar* — "all flesh" — is the chapter of meat and milk, but the Sages ran the
whole discussion of washing the hands through it, because a meal is where meat, milk, and impure hands
all meet. Today drains the last of that discussion and steps across the threshold.

> The first half asks **what counts** — what water, what vessel, what act. Water must reach the hands by
> a person's **own force** (*koach gavra*); the vessel must hold a **quarter-log** (*revi'it*) and be
> whole; a cloth may sometimes stand in for washing, and sometimes not. The second half asks **who** —
> the one *fed* by another must wash (his is the mouth the food enters), the one who *feeds* need not
> (he is not eating). Then the **mishna**: you may wrap meat and cheese in one cloth so long as they do
> not touch, and two strangers may share a table, one eating meat and one cheese.

Three movements:

1. **The vessel and the water** (107a) — the irrigation channel and *koach gavra*, the perforated
   bucket, the quarter-log, and which improvised containers qualify.
2. **The cloth, and who must wash** (107a–107b) — eating with a *mappa*, the priest-only leniency, and
   the two dilemmas resolved into one clean halakha.
3. **The chapter's mishna** (107b) — meat and cheese in a single cloth, cold-on-cold, and two guests
   who may or may not share a table.

## Key concepts & terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **כֹּחַ גַּבְרָא** *(koach gavra)* | "A person's force" — the water must reach the hands by a human act; channel water, moved by the current, fails this, so one washes from the bucket instead. |
| **אֲרִיתָּא דְּדַלָּאֵי** *(arita dedalla'ei)* | An irrigation channel filled from a river by buckets — unfit for washing, since its water no longer comes by a person's force. |
| **רְבִיעִית** *(revi'it)* | A quarter-*log*, the minimum measure of water for washing the hands; needed in full for one person, only as the vessel's capacity for two. |
| **כּוֹנֵס מַשְׁקֶה** *(konness mashkeh)* | A hole large enough to let liquid in; a bucket so perforated joins channel to river for immersing, yet is no longer a vessel for pouring. |
| **שְׁיָרֵי טָהֳרָה** *(shirei tahara)* | "The remainder of purity" — leftover water from an originally valid quarter-log, which is why one *revi'it* can wash even two people. |
| **מְגוּפַת חָבִית** *(megufat chavit)* | A barrel-stopper hollowed out to hold a *revi'it* — an improvised but valid vessel for washing, unlike a sack or basket. |
| **מַפָּה** *(mappa)* | A cloth used to hold food in place of washing — permitted to priests eating *teruma*, not to others eating ordinary food in purity. |
| **דַּעְתִּי קְצָרָה** *(da'ati ketzara)* | "I am delicate" — Rav's reason for using a cloth *after* washing: he preferred not to touch the food bare-handed. |
| **אוֹכֵל מֵחֲמַת מַאֲכִיל** *(ochel mechamat ma'achil)* | One who eats by being fed by another; the final halakha is that he must still wash his hands, though he never touches the food. |
| **שַׁמָּשׁ** *(shamash)* | The attendant at a meal; he must wash because he is occupied (*tarid*) and may touch the food without noticing. |
| **שִׁיבְתָּא** *(shivta)* | An evil spirit that rests on hands unwashed since morning; Abaye's reason for one washing that is *not* about the food itself. |
| **צוֹנֵן בְּצוֹנֵן** *(tzonen betzonen)* | Cold food touching cold food — no flavor is absorbed, so no peeling is needed, only rinsing before eating. |
| **אַכְסְנָאִין** *(achsena'in)* | Guests or travelers lodging together; strangers may share one table with meat and cheese, since neither will eat the other's food. |

**Tannaim**

## Walking through the sugya, step by step

### 107a — What water, what vessel

**Wash once, stipulate for the day.** Yesterday closed with Rav's leave to wash in the morning and rely
on it all day. Its application to the water-starved **valley of Aravot** carries over: *since water is
scarce for you, wash in the morning and stipulate over your hands for the whole day.* Two readings
follow — **"some say"** this holds only in exigent circumstances (*bishe'at hadechak*), and so disagrees
with Rav, who permitted it to all; **"others say"** it holds even when not pressed, which *is* Rav's
view.

**Koach gavra.** **Rav Pappa** on the irrigation channel (*arita dedalla'ei*), fed from a river by
buckets: one may **not** wash from it, because the water arrives by the current, not by a person's force.

> **דְּלָא אָתוּ מִכֹּחַ גַּבְרָא … וְאִי מְקָרַב לְגַבֵּי דַּוְלָא … נוֹטְלִין מִמֶּנּוּ.**
> It does not come from a person's force — but if he brings his hands near the **bucket** itself, so the
> water reaches them by his own force (*koach gavra*), he may wash from it.

**The perforated bucket.** If the drawing-bucket has a hole big enough to admit liquid (*konness
mashkeh*), the hole joins the channel water to the river, and one may **immerse** his hands in the
channel as in the river. But that same bucket is now **invalid for pouring** — **Rava**: a vessel
perforated to *konness mashkeh* is no longer a vessel, and one may not wash from it. One hole, two
opposite results, depending on whether you immerse or pour.

**The quarter-log.** **Rava** again: a vessel **without a *revi'it*** of water in it cannot be used to
wash. An objection: elsewhere Rava said a vessel that cannot *contain* a *revi'it* — implying that if it
*can* contain one, it serves even when less than that is now inside. **The resolution:** the strict
statement (actual *revi'it*) is for **one** person; the lenient one (mere capacity) is for **two**. A
baraita seals it — *a quarter-log washes one, and even two* — because the leftover water comes **from
the remainder of an originally valid measure** (*shirei tahara*); without an initial *revi'it* it serves
no one.

**Three particulars.** **Rav Sheshet** presses **Ameimar**: are you strict about the **vessel**
(*mana*)? Yes. About the water's **appearance** (*chazuta*)? Yes. About its **measure** (*shiura*)? Yes.
*Some say* Ameimar answered differently — strict about vessel and appearance, **not** about measure,
citing the same *quarter-log-for-two* baraita — but the Gemara rejects that inference: the baraita's
leniency is only *shirei tahara*, and proves nothing about the measure in general. **Rav Yaakov** of
Nehar Pekod made himself a *revi'it* glass; **Rav Ashi** in Huzal, a *revi'it* earthenware jug.

**What can be a vessel.** **Rava**: a **barrel-stopper** hollowed out to hold a *revi'it* may be washed
from — improvised, but now a vessel. A baraita agrees, and extends it: leather wineskins (*chemet*,
*kefisha*) fixed for the purpose, yes; but a **sack** or a **basket**, even if they happen to hold
water, **no** — they were never made to hold liquid.

**Eating with a cloth.** A dilemma: may one eat while holding the food in a **cloth** (*mappa*) instead
of washing — or do we fear he will touch it anyway? A proof is drawn from **Rabbi Tzadok**, who took
food of less than an egg-bulk in a cloth (a Sukka mishna) — but it is inconclusive, since that mishna
may teach only about *sukka* and the after-blessing, not about washing. Then a live case: **Shmuel**
found **Rav** eating with a cloth, and challenged him —

### 107b — Who must wash

**"Do we act this way?"** Rav's answer: **I did wash** — but *da'ati ketzara*, I am delicate, and would
rather not hold the food bare-handed; the cloth is on top of washing, not instead of it. So the cloth
alone settles nothing.

**The priest-only leniency.** In Eretz Yisrael **Rabbi Zeira** found **Rabbi Ami** and **Rabbi Asi**
eating with worn scraps of wineskin and rebuked them: *could two such great men misread the story of
Rav and Shmuel?* — where Rav had in fact washed. But Rabbi Zeira had forgotten **Rav Tachlifa bar
Avimi** in Shmuel's name: the cloth was permitted **to those who eat *teruma*** (priests, careful by
habit), **not** to those who merely eat ordinary food in purity. **And Rabbi Ami and Rabbi Asi were
priests.**

**Is the one *fed* obligated?** First dilemma: one who eats **by being fed** (*ochel mechamat ma'achil*),
never touching the food — must he wash? Proof from **Rav Huna bar Sechora**, who cut a slice and placed
it in **Rav Hamnuna's** mouth. Inconclusive: perhaps Rav Hamnuna had washed anyway. Another proof, from
**Rav**: do not put a slice into the **attendant's** (*shamash*) mouth unless you know he washed — which
suggests one who is fed *does* wash. But **the attendant is different** (*tarid*): occupied with his
work, he may touch the food inadvertently, so he alone must wash regardless. (Along the way, **Rav
Pappa** settles a side-clash between **Rav** and **Rabbi Yochanan** on whether the attendant re-blesses
each slice: *with* an important person present, no; *without* one, yes.)

**Is the one who *feeds* obligated?** Second dilemma: must the *feeder* (*ma'achil*), whose hands touch
the food, wash? Proof from the **school of Menashe**: a woman rinses **one hand** and gives bread to her
child — and **Shammai the Elder** would not feed with one hand on Yom Kippur, so they decreed he feed
with two, forcing both to be washed. Sounds like the feeder must wash. But **Abaye**: that washing is
about **Shivta**, the morning spirit on unwashed hands — not about the food.

**The ruling.** The decisive story: **Shmuel's father** found young Shmuel crying — his teacher had
struck him for feeding the teacher's son without washing. The father: *the teacher himself erred, and
struck you for his own mistake.*

> **אוֹכֵל מֵחֲמַת מַאֲכִיל – צָרִיךְ נְטִילַת יָדַיִם, מַאֲכִיל – אֵינוֹ צָרִיךְ.**
> One who **eats** by being fed must wash the hands; one who **feeds** another need not.

**The mishna.** With washing finished, the chapter's own subject: one may **wrap meat and cheese in a
single cloth**, provided they do not touch; **Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel** adds that two guests
(*achsena'in*) may eat at one table, one meat and one cheese, without concern.

**Cold on cold.** The Gemara: and if they *do* touch, so what? *Cold food on cold food* (*tzonen
betzonen*) absorbs nothing! **Abaye**: true, no **peeling** (*kelipa*) is needed — but **rinsing**
(*hadacha*) still is, and the decree guards against eating them unrinsed.

**Strangers, not friends.** On the two guests, **Rav Chanan bar Ami** in Shmuel's name: only when they
**do not know each other** — for then neither eats the other's food; **acquainted**, it is forbidden.
A baraita agrees. Then **Rav Yeimar bar Shelemya** asks **Abaye** about two **brothers** who are
particular about each other's food — and Abaye answers with the paradigm of **Baitos's cakes**: allow
no visible exception, *lest people say all Syrian cakes are forbidden but Baitos's are permitted* — a
back-and-forth that spills into tomorrow's daf.

## The distinctions, side by side

The daf's spine is a single question asked of four people at the table — *must this one wash?* — and the
answers do not all run the same way:

| At the meal | Must wash the hands? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| One who **eats** bread himself | Yes | the base obligation |
| One **fed** by another (*ochel mechamat ma'achil*) | **Yes** (final halakha) | the food enters *his* mouth; he is the eater |
| One who **feeds** another (*ma'achil*) | **No** (final halakha) | his hands touch it, but he is not eating |
| The meal's **attendant** (*shamash*) | Yes | occupied (*tarid*), he may touch the food unawares |

---

## One line to carry with you

> The table sorts people by what they are doing: the one who eats must wash, the one who only serves the
> food need not — the obligation follows the mouth, not the hands.
