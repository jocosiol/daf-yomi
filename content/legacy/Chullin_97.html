Daf Yomi — Chullin 97 (חולין צז)

Chapter 7: Gid HaNasheh (the sciatic nerve) · Study date: Wed, 5 August 2026

The big picture (read this first)

We’re in chapter 7, whose subject is the gid hanasheh — the sciatic nerve, forbidden ever since Yaakov wrestled the angel (Bereishit 32:33). But by daf 97 the Gemara has widened its lens to a question that sits at the very heart of kashrut:

When a forbidden food is mixed or cooked into permitted food, when does the whole thing become forbidden — and when is the forbidden part “nullified” so the food stays permitted?

The idea powering the whole page is טַעַם כְּעִיקָּר — “taste is like the essence.” A forbidden food doesn’t forbid only through its physical substance; it forbids through the flavor it imparts (noten taam). That gives two very different ways a mixture can end up permitted:

  1. By taste — if a reliable taster detects no forbidden flavor, the mixture is fine.
  2. By ratio — when we can’t rely on tasting, we fall back on a fixed measure: בְּשִׁישִׁים, nullification in sixty (one part forbidden to sixty parts permitted).

97a builds the taste route; 97b builds the sixty route — and then stress-tests it on the gid, the udder, and a non-kosher egg.

Walking through the sugya, step by step

97a — Why does cheilev forbid, but the gid might not?

The opening answer. Cheilev (forbidden fat) is different from the gid: its flavor permeates throughout the animal (mefaʿpeaʿ). The nerve barely gives taste of its own — a live debate on the previous daf — so the two aren’t automatically judged alike.

The challenge — R’ Yochanan and the goat. Rabba bar bar Chana reports a real case in the synagogue of Maon: a young goat was roasted together with its cheilev. R’ Yochanan ruled, “Peel it and eat it until you reach the fat.” Startling — if flavor permeates everything, the whole goat should be forbidden! The lesson: roasting doesn’t drive flavor through the way boiling does.

Three resolutions for why the goat wasn’t forbidden throughout:

  • it was a lean goat (kachush) — too little fat to spread its flavor; or
  • it was really a kidney roasted in its fat, and a membrane blocks the fat from penetrating (Rav Huna bar Yehuda); or
  • the case was actually a small non-kosher fish (kilkhit) that fell into a stew-pot (ilpas), and R’ Yochanan told them to have a gentile cook (kapeila arma’a) taste it — the cook found no forbidden flavor, so it was permitted (Ravin bar Rav Adda).

Rava’s move — from a difficulty to a principle. Rava was troubled by a baraita: a pot used for meat shouldn’t be used for milk, and if it was, the milk is forbidden if the absorbed meat imparts flavor — likewise teruma absorbed in a pot renders later non-sacred food sacred if it imparts flavor. Fine for teruma, he says: a kohen may taste and check. But meat-in-milk — who tastes it? No Jew is allowed to. R’ Yochanan’s kilkhit ruling answers it: here too we rely on a gentile cook to report whether flavor was imparted.

The summary rule. Rava lays out the whole framework: sometimes we rely on a Jew tasting, sometimes on a gentile cook tasting, and sometimes — where neither works — on sixty.

97b — When you can’t taste: the measure of sixty

Sorting the cases. When the foods are different types (min b’she’eino mino) and a permitted Jew can taste — e.g. teruma with non-sacred food — a Jew tastes. When the mixture is potentially forbidden to all Jews — meat-in-milk — a gentile cook tastes. But when they’re the same type (min b’mino) and flavor can’t be told apart, or no gentile taster is available, we fall back on sixty times as much permitted food.

The salted-thigh dispute. Thighs were salted with the gid still inside in the Exilarch’s house. Ravina forbids; Rav Acha bar Rav Ashi permits; asked to decide, Mar bar Rav Ashi says, “My father permitted it” — ruling leniently. Then Rav Acha bar Rav presses Ravina on his reasoning. The hinge is Shmuel’s “maliach k’rotcha, kavush k’mevushal“ (salted is like boiling-hot, marinated is like cooked). The Gemara reasons that “boiling-hot” here means like roasting, not like boiling — and since roasting doesn’t drive the nerve’s fat through the thigh, salting shouldn’t either. This is a difficulty (קַשְׁיָא) for Ravina’s stringency.

What joins the count of sixty? Rabbi Chanina: you measure against the broth (rotev), the sediment/spices (kipah), the pieces (chatichot), and the pot itself — all of it combines to nullify. (Two readings of “the pot”: the pot’s own volume, or what the pot absorbed from the permitted food.)

How to estimate the forbidden flavor. R’ Abbahu in the name of R’ Yochanan: assess the forbidden food “as though it were onion or leek (kaflot)” — gauge how strongly that would flavor the dish. R’ Abba pushes back: why not assess it as pepper, which never nullifies (not even in a thousand)? Abaye answers: the Sages determined nothing imparts flavor more strongly than onion and leek — so that’s the yardstick.

The tricky trio — gid, udder, egg. Rav Nachman gives the counting rules: a gid is nullified in sixty and is not itself counted; an udder (kachal) cooked with meat — the milk is nullified in sixty and the udder is counted with the meat; a non-kosher egg is nullified in sixty and is not counted.

The udder, taken apart. R’ Yitzchak son of Rav Mesharshiyya sharpens it: even with sixty-to-one, the udder itself stays forbidden (the meat drives flavor into the milk inside it), and if that udder later falls into a second pot it forbids that pot too — unless there’s sixty against the whole udder. Rav Ashi recalls a dilemma from Rav Kahana’s study hall — do we measure against the udder’s whole volume or only the milk that came out? — and the Gemara concludes we measure the whole thing, because the Sages effectively treated the udder like an intrinsic piece of non-kosher meat (chatichah d’neveilah).

Does an egg even give taste? Rav Idi bar Avin needles Abaye: people call something tasteless “mere egg-water (mei beitzim)” — so how can a forbidden egg impart flavor at all? Abaye begins to answer (“here we are dealing with…”) — and the sugya carries into 98.

Key concepts & terms

Term Meaning
גִּיד הַנָּשֶׁה — gid hanasheh The sciatic nerve, forbidden to eat (Bereishit 32:33). Gives little taste of its own.
חֵלֶב — cheilev Forbidden fats. Strong flavor — the paradigm food that “gives taste” and permeates (mefaʿpeaʿ).
טַעַם כְּעִיקָּר — taam k’ikar “Taste is like the essence” — forbidden flavor forbids, not just substance. The core idea of the daf.
נוֹתֵן טַעַם — noten taam “Imparts flavor.” Whether a food noten taam decides whether it forbids the mixture.
קַפִּילָא אֲרַמָּאָה — kapeila arma’a A gentile cook — relied on to taste a doubtful mixture that no Jew may taste.
בִּיטּוּל בְּשִׁישִׁים — bittul b’shishim Nullification in sixty. The fallback ratio when taste can’t be assessed.
מִין בְּמִינוֹ / מִין בְּשֶׁאֵינוֹ מִינוֹ “Type with its own type” vs. “type with a different type” — same food (flavor undetectable → use the ratio) vs. different foods (a taster can help).
מָלִיחַ כְּרוֹתֵחַ / כָּבוּשׁ כִּמְבוּשָּׁל “Salted is like boiling-hot / marinated is like cooked” — Shmuel; can salting transfer flavor like heat?

בָּצָל וְקַפְלוֹט — batzal v’kaflot Onion and leek — the Sages’ benchmark for the strongest flavor-giver, used to estimate a forbidden food.

מֵי בֵּיצִים — mei beitzim “Egg-water” — proverbial for tasteless; raised to question whether an egg imparts flavor at all.

Who’s who in today’s daf

A field guide to every sage named on 97, using the title tells (Rav = Babylonian Amora; Rabbi = Tanna or Eretz-Yisrael Amora; Rava/Ravina live in Bavel despite the -a ending). Where a minor figure’s details are thin, that’s flagged rather than invented.

  • Rabba bar bar Chana (רבה בר בר חנה) — Babylonian Amora, ~3rd gen, but famous as a traveler who carried R’ Yochanan’s teachings from Eretz Yisrael back to Bavel; here he reports the Maon-synagogue case. Don’t confuse with Rabbah (Rabbah bar Nachmani, head of Pumbedita) or with Rava — three similar-looking names, three different men.
  • Rabbi Yochanan (רבי יוחנן) — i.e. R’ Yochanan bar Nappacha, the towering Eretz-Yisrael Amora (2nd gen), head of the academy in Tiberias; his rulings anchor the Yerushalmi. Teacher of R’ Abbahu and R’ Elazar; brother-in-law and study-partner of Reish Lakish. His goat/kilkhit rulings drive all of 97a.
  • Rav Huna bar Yehuda (רב הונא בר יהודה) — a Babylonian Amora (later generations, in Rava’s orbit); a minor voice here, offering the “kidney-and-membrane” resolution. (Not to be confused with the great Rav Huna, head of Sura, whose position from 96b is what the daf opens by challenging.)
  • Ravin bar Rav Adda (רבין בר רב אדא) — a lesser-known Babylonian Amora; details on him are sparse, so treat the identification lightly. He offers the kilkhit (non-kosher fish) resolution.
  • Rava (רבא) — Rava bar Yosef bar Chama, 4th-gen Babylonian Amora, head of the academy at Machoza; the classic disputant of Abaye (“Abaye and Rava” runs through Shas). Here he builds the taster-framework. Look-alike alert: Rava ≠ Rabbah.
  • Shmuel (שמואל) — 1st-gen Babylonian Amora, head of the academy at Nehardea, colleague-and-disputant of Rav; a giant of civil law. His maxim “maliach k’rotcha, kavush k’mevushal” is the pivot of the salted-thigh dispute.
  • Ravina (רבינא) — here Ravina I, a 6th-gen Babylonian Amora and close colleague of Rav Ashi at Mata Mechasya; the pair are traditionally credited with the “sealing” of the Talmud. He forbids the salted thighs.
  • Rav Acha bar Rav Ashi (רב אחא בר רב אשי) — a Babylonian Amora of the Rav Ashi generation who permits the thighs. Look-alike alert (as you flagged): distinct from Mar bar Rav Ashi below — both carry “bar Rav Ashi,” but they’re different people.
  • Mar bar Rav Ashi (מר בר רב אשי) — also called Tavyomi; 7th-gen Babylonian Amora, son of Rav Ashi the redactor, and himself later head of the Sura/Mata Mechasya academy. He decides leniently: “My father permitted it.”
  • Rav Acha bar Rav (רב אחא בר רב) — as the printed text reads; a Babylonian Amora who challenges Ravina’s reasoning. Note: some texts read “Rav Acha bar Rava,” and many identify this speaker with Rav Acha bar Rav Ashi of the previous line — a genuine textual/identity question, so hold it loosely.
  • Rabbi Chanina (רבי חנינא) — most likely R’ Chanina bar Chama, a 1st-gen Eretz-Yisrael Amora (transitional figure) based in Tzippori, a senior colleague of R’ Yochanan’s circle. He states which components join the count of sixty. (There are several R’ Chaninas, so this identification is the most probable rather than certain.)
  • Rabbi Abbahu (רבי אבהו) — 3rd-gen Eretz-Yisrael Amora in Caesarea, a leading student of R’ Yochanan, well known for his dealings with the Roman authorities. Here he transmits R’ Yochanan’s onion-and-leek yardstick.
  • Rabbi Abba (רבי אבא) — a 3rd-gen Amora who moved between Bavel and Eretz Yisrael (a nachota, one of the “descenders” who carried rulings between the centers); he presses Abaye with the pepper objection. (Which exact R’ Abba is debated, so keep it general.)
  • Abaye (אביי) — 4th-gen Babylonian Amora, head of Pumbedita, raised by his uncle Rabbah bar Nachmani; the perennial disputant of Rava. He fields both the pepper objection and the egg-water objection.
  • Rav Nachman (רב נחמן) — Rav Nachman bar Yaakov, 3rd-gen Babylonian Amora at Nehardea, tied to the Exilarch’s court and a master of civil law; he lays down the gid / kachal / egg counting rules. (Distinct from Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak.)
  • Rabbi Yitzchak son of Rav Mesharshiyya (רבי יצחק בריה דרב משרשיא) — a Babylonian Amora, a relatively minor figure; he sharpens the udder ruling. Details beyond this are thin.
  • Rav Ashi (רב אשי) — 6th-gen Babylonian Amora, head of the academy at Mata Mechasya (Sura) for roughly half a century and the principal redactor of the Babylonian Talmud. He recalls the udder dilemma from his student days.
  • Rav Kahana (רב כהנא) — the one whose study hall Rav Ashi attended, i.e. Rav Kahana of Pum Nahara, a 5th-gen Babylonian Amora and one of Rav Ashi’s teachers. Look-alike alert: there are several Rav Kahanas spread across the generations of the Talmud.
  • Rav Idi bar Avin (רב אידי בר אבין) — a Babylonian Amora (contemporary of Abaye and Rava); he raises the “egg-water” objection that a forbidden egg can’t really impart flavor.

One line to carry with you

A forbidden food is judged by the flavor it gives — so a mixture is permitted either when a reliable taster tastes nothing forbidden, or, when tasting can’t help, when there is sixty times more permitted food than forbidden.
