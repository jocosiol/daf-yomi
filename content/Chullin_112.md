## The big picture (read this first)

Yesterday closed on **Chizkiya in Abaye's name**: the fish is permitted with *kutaḥ*, but the radish is
forbidden. Today opens by finishing exactly that — *why* the radish, and which cut vegetables join it —
and then keeps pulling the same thread through the whole daf: **once a forbidden or meaty taste is loose,
how strong does it have to be, and how close does it have to get, to actually forbid?**

> **הַאי אִיתֵיהּ לְאִיסּוּרָא בְּעֵינֵיהּ, וְהַאי לֵיתֵיהּ לְאִיסּוּרָא בְּעֵינֵיהּ.** "In this case the
> forbidden thing is there **in its own substance**; in that case it is not." Rav Naḥman's line about the
> two jugs is the daf in miniature: a taste that stays *itself* forbids; a taste that dissolves away does
> not.

The page is a string of test cases, each turning that same dial:

1. **The cut vegetables** (112a) — the sharp radish absorbs and forbids, a cucumber can be scraped clean,
   chard is out but turnip stalks are in, and alternating the two rescues the knife.
2. **Proximity, not contact** (112a) — a jug of **salt** may not stand beside the *kutaḥ*, but a jug of
   **vinegar** may: the difference is whether a stray drop stays substantive or melts away.
3. **The bird in the kutaḥ** (112a) — Rav Ḥinnana permits it by refining Shmuel's *maliach k'rotei'ach*:
   "salted is like boiling" only bites when the food is *too salty to eat*.
4. **Bread cut on meat** (112a) — forbidden only when the blood is ruddy, soaked clear through, and
   viscous; runny blood is nothing.
5. **The vessel under the spit** (112a–b) — no drip-pan until the redness is gone, and Rav Ashi's two
   lumps of salt.
6. **Salted together** (112b) — fish and birds, soft skin against hard; and kosher meat beside a
   *tereifa*, where a single word of the verse forbids even its juices.

## Key concepts & terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **חוּרְפֵּיהּ** *(churpei)* | The **pungency / sharpness** of a food. A radish is so sharp that it actively draws the fat off a meat knife into itself and cannot merely be scraped — so it is forbidden with *kutaḥ*. |
| **קִישּׁוּת** *(kishut)* | A **cucumber** — not sharp, so it absorbs only lightly; one **scrapes the place of the cut** to remove the fat residue and may then eat it with *kutaḥ*. |
| **סִילְקָא** *(silka)* | **Chard / beets** — cut with a meat knife it is **forbidden** with *kutaḥ*, as it draws in the knife's flavor; turnip stalks (*kilchei d'lifta*) are permitted, and alternating the two nullifies the meat taste. |
| **כַּמְכָּא** *(kamka)* | The Aramaic name for ***kutaḥ*** — the Babylonian milk dip (whey, bread-mold, and salt) against which every case on the daf is tested: may this food be eaten *with* it? |
| **מָלִיחַ הֲרֵי הוּא כְּרוֹתֵחַ** *(maliach k'rotei'ach)* | "**Salted is like boiling.**" Shmuel's principle, refined here: it applies only to food **so salty it cannot be eaten as is**; *kutaḥ*, which is eaten despite its salt, counts as cold. |
| **קְלִיפָה** *(kelipa)* | **Peeling** — removing the outer layer of a *roasted* food that met the forbidden substance, since roasting softens the flesh and drives the taste deeper than a rinse can reach. |
| **פִּילֵי** *(pilei)* | **Cracks / splits** in roasted meat. If the bird that fell into *kutaḥ* has cracks, the milk runs inside them and the whole piece is forbidden — peeling no longer helps. |
| **כִּכָּר שֶׁחָתַךְ עָלֶיהָ בָּשָׂר** *(kikar shechatach aleha basar)* | A **loaf on which roasted meat was cut** — forbidden to eat, but only when the blood is *ruddy*, *passed clear through* the loaf, and *viscous*; runny blood (*kelishta*) is no problem. |
| **תִּימְרָא** *(timra)* | The **column of smoke** rising off roasting meat — Rav Pappa's sign that the blood is fully expelled and a drip-pan may now be set beneath (a sign Rav Ashi then rejects). |
| **דּוּחְקָא דְסַכִּינָא** *(duchka d'sakina)* | The **pressure of the knife** — why a loaf *cut* on roasted meat is stricter than meat merely dripping: the blade squeezes out blood the finished roast would otherwise no longer give off. |
| **כְּלִי מְנוּקָּב** *(kli menukav)* | A **perforated vessel**, whose holes let salting-blood drain away — as opposed to a solid one where it pools. The fish-and-birds ruling makes sense *only* in a perforated vessel. |
| **צִיר וְרוֹטֶב וְקֵיפָה** *(tzir, rotev, keipa)* | **Juice, gravy, and congealed fat/spices.** From "**הַטְּמֵאִים**" ("*the* unclean," Lev. 11:31) Rava learns that a forbidden food's very exudates — not only its flesh — are forbidden. |

## Who's who in today's daf

**Babylonian Amoraim**

- **Shmuel** (of Nehardea) — first generation, Rav's great colleague; the source of *maliach k'rotei'ach*
  and of the loaf-cut-on-meat ruling. His practice (throwing the loaf to his dog) frames the whole
  discussion.
- **Rav Huna** — second generation, head of Sura after Rav; would give the questionable loaf to his
  attendant rather than eat it himself, being *of delicate constitution* (*anina daatei*).
- **Rav Yehuda** (bar Yeḥezkel) — second generation, founder of Pumbedita; the tradent of Shmuel's rulings
  carried over from yesterday's daf.
- **Rav Naḥman** (bar Yaakov) — third generation, of Nehardea; rules on the two jugs (salt forbidden
  beside *kutaḥ*, vinegar permitted), transmits Shmuel on the loaf and on the drip-pan, and gives the
  fish-and-birds ruling. (Rav *Naḥman bar Yaakov* — not Rav Naḥman bar Yitzchak.)
- **Rav Dimi** — the questioner who presses Rav Naḥman on the jugs. Several Amoraim bear this name; the one
  who "went up and came" carrying Eretz-Yisrael traditions is best known, and I would not pin this
  exchange to a single biography with confidence.
- **Rava** (bar Yosef bar Ḥama) — fourth generation, of Meḥoza; praises Rav Ḥinnana's leniency and supplies
  its logic, eats the reddened loaf and calls it "meat wine," and reads "the unclean" to forbid the
  *tereifa*'s juices. (רבא; not to be confused with **Rabba**, רבה.)
- **Rav Ḥinnana, son of Rava, of Pashronya** — a minor figure, fixed almost entirely by this one ruling;
  he permitted the bird that fell into the *kutaḥ*. Beyond Rava's praise of him here, I would not claim
  much.
- **Mar Zutra** — reporting **in the name of Rav Pappa** — gives the rising smoke as the sign the blood is
  spent.
- **Rav Pappa** — fifth generation, student of Rava; the source (via Mar Zutra) of the smoke sign.
- **Rav Ashi** — sixth generation, redactor of the Talmud at Sura-Mata Meḥasya; rejects the smoke sign
  (the top may still be raw), prescribes the two lumps of salt, and fields Rav Aḥa's challenge about the
  loaf. (Rav *Ashi*, not Rav *Asi*.)
- **Rav Aḥa, son of Rav Ika** — presses Rav Ashi: if the finished roast no longer bleeds, how can a loaf
  cut on it be forbidden? (Rav Aḥa **son of Rav Ika** — distinct from Rav Aḥa bar Rav Ashi.)
- **Rav Mari bar Raḥel** — a later figure, remembered as a son of Raḥel, Shmuel's daughter, taken captive;
  his kosher meat was salted beside a *tereifa* and he brought the case to Rava. The captivity detail is
  traditional; treat the finer biography lightly.

## Walking through the sugya, step by step

### 112a — The cut vegetables, and the jugs

The page picks up mid-sentence, still on yesterday's radish. Why is a **radish** (*tzenon*) cut on a meat
knife forbidden with *kutaḥ*, while other things are not? Because of its **sharpness**:

> **דְּאַגַּב חוּרְפֵּיהּ בָּלַע.** "Because of its sharpness it absorbs" the fat off the blade — deeply
> enough that scraping will not save it.

A **cucumber** (*kishut*) is milder: one simply **scrapes the place of the cut** and eats it. **Turnip
stalks** (*kilchei d'lifta*) are likewise permitted; **chard** (*silka*) is forbidden, as it too draws in
the knife's taste. And a neat rescue: if one **alternated** the blade between chard and turnip, the turnip
stalks **nullify** the meat flavor, and even the chard is permitted.

**From taste to mere nearness.** **Rav Dimi asked Rav Naḥman:** may one set a **jug of salt** (for salting
meat) **beside a jug of *kamka*** — lest a little *kutaḥ* fall unseen into the salt and later reach the
meat? "**Forbidden.**" And a jug of **vinegar** beside the *kutaḥ*? "**Permitted.**" Rav Dimi pressed for
the difference; Rav Naḥman deflected — *when you have thought long enough to eat a kor of salt, you'll see
it* — and the Gemara supplies the reason:

> **הַאי אִיתֵיהּ לְאִיסּוּרָא בְּעֵינֵיהּ, וְהַאי לֵיתֵיהּ לְאִיסּוּרָא בְּעֵינֵיהּ.** A drop of *kutaḥ*
> that lands in **salt** stays discernible **in its own substance** and is not nullified — so it forbids.
> A drop that lands in **vinegar** melts away and imparts no flavor — so there is nothing to fear.

### 112a — The bird in the kutaḥ, and refining "salted is like boiling"

A **young bird** (*bar gozala*) **fell into a jug of *kamka***, and **Rav Ḥinnana, son of Rava, of
Pashronya, permitted it**. **Rava** marveled: *who is wise enough to permit such a case if not he?* The
logic:

> **כִּי אָמַר שְׁמוּאֵל מָלִיחַ הֲרֵי הוּא כְּרוֹתֵחַ — הָנֵי מִילֵּי הֵיכָא דְּאֵינוֹ נֶאֱכָל מֵחֲמַת
> מִלְחוֹ.** Shmuel's "salted is like boiling" bites only where the food is **so salty it cannot be eaten
> as is**. But *kutaḥ* **can** be eaten despite its salt — so it counts as cold, imparts no flavor, and
> the bird needs only a rinse of the spot.

But the leniency is fenced at once. **This holds only for a raw bird.** If it was **roasted** (*tzli*), the
softened flesh has drunk deeper and it **requires peeling** (*kelipa*). If it has **cracks** (*pilei*), the
milk has run inside and it is **entirely forbidden**. And if it was **spiced** (*tablin*), the spices open
the flesh and again it is **entirely forbidden**.

### 112a — Bread cut on roasted meat

**Rav Naḥman said Shmuel:** a **loaf on which one cut** roasted meat is **forbidden to eat**, for the blood
squeezed out soaks in. But the ban is heavily conditioned — it holds only if the blood is:

- **ruddy** (*asmek*) — genuinely red, not merely damp;
- **passed clear through** (*abrei*) — visible on both faces of the loaf;
- **viscous** (*asmchei*) — thick, not thin. If it is **runny** (*kelishta*), there is no problem at all.

Then a vignette on how the sages *held* this: **Shmuel** would throw such a loaf **to his dog**; **Rav
Huna** would hand it **to his attendant**. The Gemara objects — *whichever way you look at it: if permitted,
it is permitted to Rav Huna too; if forbidden, forbidden to his attendant!* The answer: it is in fact
permitted, and **Rav Huna is simply of delicate constitution** and preferred not to eat it. **Rava** ate it
outright, and called the red liquid **"meat wine"** (*chamar basar*).

### 112a — The vessel under the spit

**Rav Naḥman said Shmuel:** one may **not set a vessel under roasting meat** to catch the fat **until all
the redness has dissipated** — before that, blood drips in and forbids the collected fat and the vessel.
*How do we know when?* **Mar Zutra, in Rav Pappa's name:** **when its smoke rises** (*timrato*).

> **וְדִלְמָא תַּתָּאָה מְטָא, עִילָּאָה לָא מְטָא?** **Rav Ashi objects:** "Perhaps the **underside**,
> nearest the coals, is roasted — but the **top** is not," and still bleeds. The smoke proves nothing.

So Rav Ashi gives a real remedy instead: there is no fix **except to place two lumps of salt** — one in the
receptacle below, one on top in the meat itself —

### 112b — The salt, the knife's pressure, and salting together

— **and then tilt the vessel** (*mishpyei*) gently: the dripping blood sticks to the salt on the sides while
the fat floats, and one pours off the clean fat.

**Rav Aḥa, son of Rav Ika, challenged Rav Ashi:** did Shmuel really permit a drip-pan once the redness is
gone? **But Shmuel forbade a loaf cut on roasted meat** — which shows the finished roast still holds blood!
Rav Ashi resolves it:

> **שָׁאנֵי הָתָם, דְּאַגַּב דּוּחְקָא דְסַכִּינָא פָּלֵיט.** "It is different there: **the pressure of the
> knife** forces out blood" the roast would otherwise no longer give off on its own. Dripping and cutting
> are not the same test.

**Salted together.** **Rav Naḥman:** if one salted **fish and birds together**, the **fish are forbidden**,
having absorbed the birds' blood. The Gemara narrows the case: in a **solid vessel** even bird-with-bird
would be forbidden (the pooled blood reaches everything); in a **perforated** one even fish-with-birds
should be permitted (the blood drains). The resolution is a matter of *timing*:

> **דָּגִים מִשּׁוּם דִּרְפוּ קְרָמַיְיהוּ קָדְמִי וּפָלְטִי, וְעוֹפוֹת קְמִיטִי.** Fish, whose **skin is
> soft**, expel their blood **first** and finish; birds, whose skin is **hard**, are still expelling
> **after** the fish are done — so the drained fish now **absorb** the birds' blood. Two birds salted
> together expel at once and neither takes from the other.

**Kosher meat beside a tereifa.** Finally, **Rav Mari bar Raḥel** had kosher **slaughtered meat** salted
(in a perforated vessel) together with the meat of a **tereifa**, and brought it to **Rava**. One might have
thought the kosher meat, busy expelling its own blood, absorbs nothing. Rava reads the verse otherwise:

> **הַטְּמֵאִים — לֶאֱסוֹר צִירָן וְרוֹטְבָן וְקֵיפָה שֶׁלָּהֶן.** "*The* unclean" (Lev. 11:31) — the extra
> word forbids not only the flesh but its **juice, its gravy, and its congealed fat and spices.** Those
> exudates flow more readily than blood, so the kosher meat takes them in even as it bleeds — and it is
> forbidden. *Why Rava reached for a verse rather than Shmuel's own rule runs straight into tomorrow's daf.*

## The distinctions, side by side

The bird that fell into the *kutaḥ* is the daf's cleanest ladder: the **same** milk contact, graded by
what state the bird was in, from a rinse to a total ban.

| State of the bird | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Raw** | Permitted — rinse the spot | *kutaḥ* is eaten despite its salt → treated as cold |
| **Roasted** | Requires **peeling** | roasting softens the flesh; taste drawn deeper |
| **Has cracks** (*pilei*) | **Entirely forbidden** | milk runs into the splits, past any peel |
| **Spiced** (*tablin*) | **Entirely forbidden** | spices open the flesh to full absorption |

Every rung is the same principle from the jugs — *how substantively does the forbidden taste get in?* — read
off a single bird.

---

## One line to carry with you

> A forbidden taste forbids only while it stays *itself*: dissolved in vinegar it is nothing, but held
> whole in salt — or driven deep by roasting, cracks, or spice — it is everything.

## Chazara — test yourself

```yaml
- q: Why is a radish cut with a meat knife forbidden to eat with kutaḥ, while a cucumber is not?
  opts:
  - The radish is eaten raw, whereas a cucumber is always cooked before serving.
  - The radish's sharpness draws the knife's fat deep in, past what scraping removes.
  - The radish is a Torah-level concern, while the cucumber is only rabbinic.
  - The radish was cut lengthwise and the cucumber only across its width.
  correct: b
  why: The radish's churpei (pungency) makes it actively absorb the blade's fat, so scraping the cut is not
    enough — unlike a milder cucumber, which one may simply scrape and eat.
- q: What rescues chard that was cut with a meat knife, so that it too may be eaten with kutaḥ?
  opts:
  - Peeling away the outer layer of the chard wherever the blade touched it.
  - Rinsing the cut in cold water before the meat flavor can set into it.
  - Alternating the knife between chard and turnip stalks, which nullify the taste.
  - Waiting until the knife has fully cooled from the heat of the slaughtered animal.
  correct: c
  why: Turnip stalks (kilchei d'lifta) are permitted, and alternating the blade between them and the chard
    nullifies the absorbed meat flavor, so the chard becomes permitted as well.
- q: Why may a jug of salt not stand beside a jug of kutaḥ, while a jug of vinegar may?
  opts:
  - Because salt is used for meat and vinegar is not, so only salt risks a meat-milk mix.
  - Because a drop of kutaḥ stays substantive in salt but dissolves away in vinegar.
  - Because salt draws the kutaḥ toward it, whereas vinegar repels and neutralizes it.
  - Because the salt jug is always the larger of the two and more likely to overflow.
  correct: b
  why: Rav Naḥman's reason is that in salt the stray kutaḥ remains in its own substance (b'einei) and is
    not nullified, so it forbids; in vinegar it melts away and imparts nothing.
- q: On what grounds did Rav Ḥinnana permit the young bird that fell into a jug of kutaḥ?
  opts:
  - That "salted is like boiling" applies only to food too salty to be eaten as is.
  - That a bird, unlike an animal, never retains enough blood to forbid a milk dish.
  - That anything which merely fell in, rather than being cooked, is always permitted.
  - That kutaḥ is only rabbinically forbidden with poultry, so leniency prevails.
  correct: a
  why: He held Shmuel's maliach k'rotei'ach bites only where the food is too salty to eat; kutaḥ is eaten
    despite its salt, so it counts as cold and imparts no flavor to the bird.
- q: Under what condition does that leniency for the bird no longer hold, so that it is entirely forbidden?
  opts:
  - If the bird was rinsed in cold water after being pulled from the kutaḥ.
  - If the bird was raw rather than cooked when it fell into the jug.
  - If the bird had cracks, into which the milk runs past any peeling.
  - If the bird was salted at the same time as it fell into the kutaḥ.
  correct: c
  why: The leniency holds only for a raw bird; a roasted one needs peeling, and one with cracks (pilei) —
    or one that was spiced — is entirely forbidden, since the milk reaches past the surface.
- q: When is a loaf on which roasted meat was cut actually forbidden to eat?
  opts:
  - Whenever any trace of moisture from the meat is visible on the loaf's surface.
  - Only when the blood is ruddy, soaked clear through, and viscous rather than runny.
  - Only when the loaf was afterward eaten together with a milk dish such as kutaḥ.
  - Whenever the knife used to cut it had earlier been used to slaughter the animal.
  correct: b
  why: Shmuel's ban applies only if the blood is ruddy (asmek), passed clear through the loaf (abrei), and
    viscous (asmchei); runny blood (kelishta) is no problem at all.
- q: Why did Rav Ashi reject the rising smoke as the sign that a drip-pan may be set beneath the meat?
  opts:
  - Because smoke rises from the fat, not the blood, and so proves nothing either way.
  - Because the underside may be roasted while the top is not yet and still bleeds.
  - Because by the time smoke rises the fat itself has already begun to burn away.
  - Because Shmuel never spoke of smoke, and one may not add signs to his ruling.
  correct: b
  why: Rav Ashi objected that the lower side, nearest the coals, may be done while the upper side is still
    raw and expelling blood — so he prescribed two lumps of salt and tilting the vessel instead.
- q: How did Rav Ashi answer Rav Aḥa, who asked how a loaf cut on meat can be forbidden if the finished
    roast no longer bleeds?
  opts:
  - That the loaf case speaks of raw meat, whereas the drip-pan speaks of roasted meat.
  - That Shmuel in fact retracted the ruling about the loaf once challenged.
  - That the pressure of the knife forces out blood the roast would not give off on its own.
  - That bread absorbs far more readily than a metal vessel set beneath the spit.
  correct: c
  why: Rav Ashi distinguished the cases — duchka d'sakina, the pressure of the knife, squeezes out blood
    that the finished roast would otherwise no longer emit, so a cut loaf is stricter than mere dripping.
- q: When fish and birds are salted together in a perforated vessel, why are the fish forbidden?
  opts:
  - Because the blood pools in the holes and is reabsorbed by whatever lies lowest.
  - Because fish, with softer skin, finish bleeding first and then absorb the birds' blood.
  - Because birds bleed far more than fish, overwhelming the perforations entirely.
  - Because fish and fowl may never be salted in one vessel under any circumstances.
  correct: b
  why: The soft-skinned fish expel their blood first and finish, while the hard-skinned birds are still
    expelling; the drained fish then absorb the birds' blood. Two birds bleed at once and neither absorbs.
- q: What did Rava derive from the word "the unclean" (ha-tmeim) regarding the kosher meat salted beside a
    tereifa?
  opts:
  - That the kosher meat is forbidden only if the two pieces actually touched.
  - That salting in a perforated vessel removes any concern, so the meat is permitted.
  - That a tereifa's juice, gravy, and congealed fat are forbidden, not only its flesh.
  - That the kosher meat may be eaten once its own blood has fully drained away.
  correct: c
  why: The added word forbids the tereifa's exudates — its juice, gravy, and keipa — which flow more
    readily than blood, so the kosher meat absorbs them even as it bleeds, and is forbidden.
```
