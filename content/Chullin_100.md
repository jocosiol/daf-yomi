## The big picture (read this first)

For three days the sugya has been about *ratios* — sixty, a hundred, nearly two hundred. Today
it turns to the things that **no ratio can help**. Some forbidden items are never nullified, no
matter how much permitted food surrounds them, and the reason has nothing to do with taste.

> A forbidden food can be too *whole*, or too *dignified*, to disappear into a mixture. A
> **בְּרִיָּה** — a complete entity — and a **חֲתִיכָה הָרְאוּיָה לְהִתְכַּבֵּד בָּהּ** — a piece you could set
> before a guest — both resist nullification, because significance is not a matter of volume.

Then the daf takes its most consequential turn. Rav teaches that a piece which has absorbed
forbidden flavor **itself becomes forbidden meat** — and can go on to forbid everything else in the
pot. And in the second half, a new mishnah reopens the oldest question in the chapter: was the
*gid* forbidden to Yaakov's children on the night of the wrestling, or only later at Sinai?

Three movements:

1. **What is never nullified** (100a) — *beriah* and the honourable piece, and why the mishnah
   had to teach both.
2. **חֲתִיכָה עַצְמָהּ נַעֲשֵׂת נְבֵלָה** (100a–100b) — Rav's ruling, Rav Safra's objection, and two
   different rescues from Abaye and Rava.
3. **The gid of a non-kosher animal** (100b) — Rabbi Yehuda against the Rabbis, and a long
   Gemara on whether one prohibition can land on top of another.

## Walking through the sugya, step by step

### 100a — Two reasons a thing refuses to be nullified

**Answering yesterday's cliffhanger.** Why is an unidentified *gid* among other sinews not
nullified by the majority? Two words:

> **בְּרִיָּה שָׁאנֵי.**
> "A complete entity is different" — and is not subject to nullification.

**The parallel case.** The mishnah also taught: a piece of **נְבֵלָה** or of non-kosher fish cooked
among kosher pieces, where he cannot identify which piece is forbidden — all are forbidden. Again:
why not nullified by the majority?

**A complication.** This is straightforward for the opinion that **כֹּל שֶׁדַּרְכּוֹ לִימָּנוֹת** — *anything*
whose manner is to be counted (rather than weighed or measured) is significant enough to resist
nullification. But according to the opinion that only **אֶת שֶׁדַּרְכּוֹ לִימָּנוֹת**, something sold
*exclusively* by the unit, counts — pieces of meat are not always sold by the piece. So what?

**The answer, and it is a lovely one:**

> **שָׁאנֵי חֲתִיכָה, הוֹאִיל וּרְאוּיָה לְהִתְכַּבֵּד בָּהּ לִפְנֵי הָאוֹרְחִים.**
> "A piece is different, since it is fit to honour a guest with, before the guests."

Its significance is *social*. A piece of meat good enough to place before a guest is a thing in its
own right, and a thing in its own right does not dissolve into a majority.

**Why both cases were needed.** From the *gid* alone you might have said: only a **בְּרִיָּה** resists,
but an ordinary piece is insignificant. From the piece alone you might have said: only something
fit to serve a guest resists, but a mere sinew is insignificant. **צְרִיכָא** — both were necessary.

### 100a — Rav: the piece itself becomes forbidden meat

**Rabbah bar bar Chana's version.** A piece of *neveilah* or non-kosher fish that fell into a pot
does not forbid the pot unless it imparts flavor to the **רוֹטֶב** (broth), the **קִיפָּה** (the sediment
of food settled in the pot), **and** the **חֲתִיכוֹת** (the other pieces).

**Rav's version — and note how he published it.** Rav **אוֹקֵי אָמוֹרָא עֲלֵיהּ**, appointed a
disseminator to stand before him and broadcast the teaching to a wide audience:

> **כֵּיוָן שֶׁנָּתַן טַעַם בַּחֲתִיכָה — חֲתִיכָה עַצְמָהּ נַעֲשֵׂת נְבֵלָה, וְאוֹסֶרֶת כׇּל הַחֲתִיכוֹת כּוּלָּן מִפְּנֵי שֶׁהֵן מִינָהּ.**
> "Once it has imparted flavor into a piece, that piece itself becomes *neveilah*, and it forbids
> all the pieces together, because they are its own kind."

This is the principle known ever since as **חֲתִיכָה נַעֲשֵׂית נְבֵלָה**. The forbidden flavor does not
merely spread — it *converts*. The kosher piece is promoted into a full forbidden object, which
then goes to work on everything around it. And because the pieces are all the same kind, and
like is not nullified in like, nothing is saved by ratio.

**Rav Safra's objection** (to Abaye). Whose view is Rav following? It must be Rabbi Yehuda, who
holds **מִין בְּמִינוֹ לֹא בָּטֵיל**. But if so, why does Rav require that it *imparted flavor* to another
piece? On Rabbi Yehuda's view the forbidden piece should forbid the whole pot immediately, flavor
or no flavor.

**Abaye's answer.** The case is **בְּשֶׁקָּדַם וְסִלְּקוֹ** — he cooked the forbidden piece with **one**
kosher piece and removed the forbidden piece *before* the others were added. The remaining
pieces are therefore forbidden only through the intermediary piece, which is why it matters
whether flavor actually got into it.

### 100b — Rava's answer: set aside its own kind

**Rava's alternative.** You need not say he removed it first. This is a case of **מִין וּמִינוֹ וְדָבָר
אַחֵר** — the forbidden food together with its own kind *and* with something else (the broth and
the spices). And the rule for that combination is:

> **סַלֵּק אֶת מִינוֹ כְּמִי שֶׁאֵינוֹ, וְשֶׁאֵין מִינוֹ רָבֶה עָלָיו וּמְבַטְּלוֹ.**
> "Set aside its own kind as though it were not there; and what is *not* its own kind, if it
> exceeds it, nullifies it."

So you do not count the meat at all — you ask only whether the broth and spices reach sixty times
the forbidden volume. Rav's ruling then applies precisely where the forbidden piece imparted its
flavor to the second piece **before** the broth and spices arrived. At that moment there are **two**
forbidden pieces, and the broth and spices — enough to nullify one piece — are no longer enough
to nullify two. The whole pot is forbidden.

### 100b — MISHNA: does the gid apply to a non-kosher animal?

> The prohibition of the *gid hanasheh* applies to a **kosher** animal and not to a non-kosher
> animal. **Rabbi Yehuda says:** it applies even to a non-kosher animal.

**Rabbi Yehuda's argument.** Was the *gid* not already forbidden to the children of Yaakov —
**״עַל כֵּן לֹא יֹאכְלוּ בְנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל אֶת גִּיד הַנָּשֶׁה״** (Bereishit 32:33) — at a time when the meat of a
non-kosher animal was still **permitted** to them? Whatever became forbidden then stays forbidden.

**The Rabbis' reply.** The prohibition was **stated at Sinai**, and merely **written in its place** —
recorded at the wrestling scene although it did not take effect then.

### 100b — GEMARA: can a prohibition land on an existing prohibition?

**The problem.** Rabbi Yehuda's position seems to require **אִיסּוּר חָל עַל אִיסּוּר** — that a second
prohibition takes effect where one already exists. But a baraita shows he holds the opposite: on
the impurity of bird carcasses he reads **״נְבֵלָה וּטְרֵפָה לֹא יֹאכַל לְטָמְאָה בָהּ״** (Vayikra 22:8) as
limiting that impurity to birds forbidden by "do not eat a carcass" — excluding a non-kosher bird,
which is *already* forbidden as non-kosher. So for Rabbi Yehuda, a prohibition does **not** land on
an existing one.

**A tempting escape, closed off.** Perhaps Rabbi Yehuda holds sinews have no taste, so the
non-kosher-meat prohibition never applies to a sinew and there is no collision? No — a baraita
teaches that for eating the *gid* of a non-kosher animal **Rabbi Yehuda gives two sets of lashes**
(one for the *gid*, one for non-kosher meat) while **Rabbi Shimon exempts entirely**. Two sets of
lashes means the *gid* does have taste and is food.

**The answer: they arrive together.** Rabbi Yehuda holds sinews *do* have taste, and also holds the
*gid* prohibition applies **בַּשָּׁלִיל**, to a fetus. So the two prohibitions come into force at the
same instant, and simultaneous prohibitions both take effect even for one who rejects
*issur chal al issur*.

**Objection.** But a mishnah (89b) has Rabbi Yehuda saying the *gid* prohibition does **not** apply
to a fetus, and that a fetus's forbidden fat is permitted. **Answer:** that is about a *kosher*
species, because of **״בַּבְּהֵמָה״** (Devarim 14:6) — read as "*inside* the animal," permitting
whatever is within a kosher animal at slaughter. For a **non-kosher** species the prohibition does
apply to the fetus.

**A sharper objection — the order of embryology.** A mishnah in *Nazir* (49b) lists what obliges a
nazirite to shave, including an olive-bulk of a corpse. Why state the olive-bulk if a whole corpse
obviously qualifies? Rabbi Yochanan: it is needed for a miscarried fetus **שֶׁלֹּא נִקְשְׁרוּ אֲבָרָיו
בְּגִידִין** — whose limbs are not yet bound together by sinews. It has no olive-bulk of flesh, yet
being a complete entity it conveys impurity. So **limbs form before sinews** — meaning the
non-kosher-meat prohibition (on the flesh) precedes the *gid* prohibition. They do *not* arrive
together.

**The resolution — the Noahide angle.** Even granting that the flesh was forbidden first, the *gid*
prohibition still takes effect, because **אִיסּוּרוֹ נוֹהֵג בִּבְנֵי נֹחַ** — it applies to the descendants
of Noah. It carries a stringency that the non-kosher-meat prohibition never had, and a prohibition
that adds something new can land on an existing one.

**And the mishnah's wording now reads perfectly.** Rabbi Yehuda's proof was precisely that the
*gid* was forbidden to **the children of Yaakov** — who were Noahides. That was never a stray
historical remark; it is the mechanism of his whole position.

The daf closes by returning to the baraita of the two sets of lashes — Rabbi Yehuda obligating,
Rabbi Shimon exempting — which is where tomorrow begins.

## The distinctions, side by side

Rav Safra asked one question — why should Rav care whether flavor reached the second piece? — and got two entirely different rescues:

| Whose answer | What the case really is | Why the flavor matters | What forbids the rest of the pot |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Abaye** | **בְּשֶׁקָּדַם וְסִלְּקוֹ** — cooked with one kosher piece, then removed before the others went in | The forbidden piece is gone; the only route left runs through the piece it flavored | That intermediary piece, now itself *neveilah* — and like is not nullified in like |
| **Rava** | **מִין וּמִינוֹ וְדָבָר אַחֵר** — its own kind *plus* something else, the broth and spices | Flavor reached the second piece **before** the broth and spices did — so two forbidden pieces, not one | **סַלֵּק אֶת מִינוֹ**: sixty is counted against broth and spices alone, enough for one piece but not two |

## Key concepts & terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **בְּרִיָּה** *(beriah)* | A complete entity. Because it is whole, it is significant, and it is never nullified in a majority. |
| **חֲתִיכָה הָרְאוּיָה לְהִתְכַּבֵּד בָּהּ** *(chatichah hare'uyah lehiskabed bah)* | A piece fit to be set before a guest. Its social dignity makes it resist nullification. |
| **כֹּל / אֶת שֶׁדַּרְכּוֹ לִימָּנוֹת** *(kol / es shedarko limanos)* | Two readings of which counted items resist nullification: anything sometimes sold by the unit, or only what is sold exclusively so. |
| **חֲתִיכָה עַצְמָהּ נַעֲשֵׂת נְבֵלָה** *(chatichah atzmah na'aseis neveilah)* | A permitted piece that absorbs forbidden flavor becomes forbidden meat in its own right, and can forbid others. |
| **רוֹטֶב / קִיפָּה / חֲתִיכוֹת** *(rotev / kipah / chatichos)* | Broth / the sediment settled in the pot / the pieces — the three components Rabbah bar bar Chana requires flavor to reach. |
| **אוֹקֵי אָמוֹרָא עֲלֵיהּ** *(oki amora aleih)* | "He set a disseminator over him" — appointing a speaker to broadcast a ruling to a large audience. |
| **בְּשֶׁקָּדַם וְסִלְּקוֹ** *(beshekadam vesilko)* | Where he removed the forbidden piece first, before the other pieces were added. |
| **מִין וּמִינוֹ וְדָבָר אַחֵר** *(min umino vedavar acher)* | Forbidden food mixed with both its own kind and something else; disregard its own kind and measure only against the rest. |
| **אֵין אִיסּוּר חָל עַל אִיסּוּר** *(ein issur chal al issur)* | A prohibition does not take effect where a prohibition already exists — so no second set of lashes. |
| **בַּשָּׁלִיל** *(bashalil)* | In a fetus. Whether the *gid* prohibition reaches a fetus decides whether the two prohibitions arrive simultaneously. |
| **נוֹהֵג בִּבְנֵי נֹחַ** *(noheg bivnei Noach)* | Applies to the descendants of Noah — the added stringency that lets the *gid* prohibition land on an existing one. |

## Who's who in today's daf

**Tannaim**

- **Rabbi Yehuda** (bar Ilai) — fourth generation, student of Rabbi Akiva. Holds the *gid* applies
  even to a non-kosher animal, and gives two sets of lashes.
- **Rabbi Shimon** (bar Yochai) — fourth generation, student of Rabbi Akiva and Rabbi Yehuda's
  contemporary. Exempts entirely; his reasoning is unpacked tomorrow.
- **The Rabbis** (*chachamim*) — the majority view of the mishnah: stated at Sinai, written in its place.

**Eretz-Yisrael Amoraim**

- **Rabbi Yochanan** (bar Nappacha) — second generation, head of the Tiberias academy. Explains
  the *Nazir* mishnah about the fetus whose limbs are not yet bound by sinews.
- **Rabbah bar bar Chana** — second generation by training; a nephew of Rabbi Chiyya who
  transmitted Eretz-Yisrael teachings in Babylonia. His version requires flavor in broth, sediment
  and pieces alike.

**Babylonian Amoraim**

- **Rav** (Abba Arikha) — first generation, founder of the academy at Sura; the towering authority
  of early Babylonian halacha. Author of *chatichah atzmah na'aseis neveilah*.
- **Abaye** — fourth generation, head of Pumbedita. Answers Rav Safra with "he removed it first."
- **Rava** — fourth generation, Machoza; Abaye's great disputant. Answers instead with the rule of
  *min umino vedavar acher*.
- **Rav Safra** — fourth generation, a colleague in Abaye and Rava's circle. Raises the objection
  to Rav's ruling.

*Look-alikes worth flagging:* **Rabbah bar bar Chana** (רבה בר בר חנה) is not **Rabbah** (רבה)
the head of Pumbedita, and neither is **Rava** (רבא) — in an unvocalised text רבה and רבא are
distinguished only by context. **Rav Safra** is a comparatively minor figure; beyond his place in
Abaye and Rava's circle I would not want to guess at his biography.

## One line to carry with you

> Nullification is not only arithmetic. A thing that is whole, or a piece dignified enough to set
> before a guest, is somebody — and what is somebody does not vanish into a majority.
