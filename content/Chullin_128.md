## The big picture (read this first)

The page divides into three movements, each circling the same nerve — **when is a part its own thing, and
when is it swallowed up in the whole?**

First, the dispute left open on 127b — *why* R' Meir and R' Shimon split over the slaughtered animal's
blood — is worked over by a whole bench of Amoraim. Rabba said it was about handles; Abaye about "grasp the
small"; now **Rava, Rav Pappa, Rav Aḥa son of Rav Ika, and Rav Ashi** each offer a different fault line,
from *is there a handle for susceptibility?* to *was the meat rendered susceptible before anyone thought to
eat it?* to *does slaughter itself, or its blood, do the rendering?* Six readings of one small argument.

Second, a run of dilemmas that all end **תֵּיקוּ** — unresolved. Each takes the "handle" idea and asks it of
a strange pair joined at the hip: a living animal and its dangling limb, a cucumber half in a pot and half
overgrowing the ground, a gourd whose half was bowed to as an idol, a fig branch hanging by its bark, a
leprous stone shared between two houses. In every case one half is susceptible and the other is not, and the
question is whether the immune half **becomes a handle** that drags impurity into its partner.

> One object, two statuses, and a seam between them: does impurity cross the seam, or stop at it? The daf
> lets the question stand.

Third — and this is the halakhic heart — the mishna's line *"the animal died"* opens the great baraita on
**what a limb must be to defile as a limb from the living.** Three tannaim divide: **R' Yosei HaGelili**
looks only at whether the body can *regrow* the part; **R' Akiva** demands sinews and bones; **Rebbi**
demands flesh, sinews and bones together. A leg joint, a kidney, an upper lip — each falls differently
across the three, and the grid below lays it out.

## Key concepts & terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **יָד** *(yad)* | A **handle** — a part that is itself immune to impurity but transmits it to (or receives it for) the food joined to it. The page's recurring question: does an immune half become a handle for its susceptible partner? |
| **מוּחְלֶפֶת הַשִּׁיטָה** *(muchlefet ha-shita)* | **"The attribution is reversed."** R' Yoḥanan's tool for a contradiction in R' Meir — swap the names on the outside mishna in *Tevul Yom* so the two statements agree. |
| **יֵשׁ יָד לְטוּמְאָה וְאֵין יָד לְהֶכְשֵׁר** *(yesh yad le-tum'a ve-ein yad le-hechsher)* | Rava's fault line — **a handle counts for transmitting impurity but not for rendering susceptible.** R' Shimon holds this; R' Meir holds a handle counts for both. |
| **הֶכְשֵׁר קוֹדֶם מַחְשָׁבָה** *(hechsher kodem machshava)* | **Susceptibility before intent** — liquid touched the food before anyone meant to eat it. Rav Pappa's reading: R' Shimon says it does not "take"; R' Meir says it does. |
| **נִתְקַנֵּחַ הַדָּם בֵּין סִימָן לְסִימָן** *(nitkane'aḥ ha-dam bein siman le-siman)* | **The blood was wiped between the cutting of the two <i>simanim</i>.** Rav Aḥa's reading — is slaughter defined from start to finish (so this is "blood of slaughter") or only at its end (so it is mere "blood of a wound")? |
| **שְׁחִיטָה מַכְשֶׁרֶת וְלֹא דָּם** *(sheḥita machsheret ve-lo dam)* | **Slaughter renders susceptible, not the blood.** Rav Ashi's reading of R' Shimon — since slaughter does not permit a dangling limb, it does not render it susceptible either. |
| **אֵבֶר מִן הַחַי** *(ever min ha-ḥai)* | A **limb severed from a living animal** — it defiles like a carcass, even though the animal lives. The baraita asks which parts qualify. |
| **בָּשָׂר הַפּוֹרֵשׁ מִן הַחַי** *(basar ha-poresh min ha-ḥai)* | **Flesh that separates from a living animal** — ritually pure, unlike a whole limb. The practical gap between "limb from the living" and "limb of a carcass." |
| **עוֹשֶׂה חֲלִיפִין** *(oseh ḥalifin)* | **Generates a replacement.** R' Yosei HaGelili's test — a body regrows lost flesh but not a lost limb, so only what is *not* regrown defiles like death. |
| **אַרְכּוּבָּה** *(arkuva)* | The **leg joint** sold with the head — sinews and bones but no flesh. The case that divides R' Akiva (defiles) from Rebbi (does not). |
| **כּוּלְיָא וְנִיב שְׂפָתַיִם** *(kulya ve-niv sfatayim)* | The **kidney and the upper lip** — flesh, no bone, and not regrown. The case that divides R' Yosei HaGelili (defiles) from R' Akiva (does not). |

## Who's who in today's daf

**Tannaim**

- **Rabbi Meir** and **Rabbi Shimon** (bar Yoḥai) — third-generation Tannaim, students of R' Akiva; their
  mishna dispute over the dangling limb is the text the Amoraim keep re-reading.
- **Rabbi Yosei HaGelili** — third-generation Tanna of the Galilee, a colleague of R' Akiva; his test for a
  defiling limb is whether the body can regrow the part. (Distinct from **R' Yosei** ben Ḥalafta — this is
  *HaGelili*, "the Galilean.")
- **Rabbi Akiva** — the master of the generation; here he needs a limb to contain sinews and bones.
- **Rebbi** (R' Yehuda HaNasi) — redactor of the Mishna; he requires flesh, sinews and bones, and he is the
  one who rules that touch is touch for every kind of impurity alike.
- **Rabbi Yehuda** (bar Ilai) — third-generation Tanna, student of R' Akiva; he reports how R' Akiva first
  taught the law of the villagers' fat and then retracted.

**Amoraim**

- **Rabbi Yoḥanan** — second-generation Eretz-Yisrael Amora of Tiberias; resolves R' Meir against R' Meir by
  "reversing the attribution."
- **Rabbi Yoshiya** — Amora who restates R' Yoḥanan's point ("according to Rebbi, the attribution is
  reversed"). A minor tradent here; I would not press the identification.
- **Rava** (bar Yosef bar Ḥama) — fourth-generation head of Maḥoza; reads the dispute as *a handle for
  impurity but not for susceptibility*. (**Rava**, רבא — not **Rabba**, רבה, just below; both are here.)
- **Rav Pappa** — fifth-generation Babylonian Amora, whose academy was at Neresh; reads it as *susceptibility
  before intent*, and later isolates the kidney and upper lip.
- **Rav Aḥa, son of Rav Ika** — fifth-generation Babylonian Amora; reads it as *blood wiped between the two
  <i>simanim</i>*.
- **Rav Ashi** — sixth-generation Amora, head of the academy at Mata Meḥasya and a chief redactor of the
  Talmud; reads it as *slaughter, not blood, renders susceptible*. (**Rav Ashi** — not **Rav Asi**, the
  earlier Eretz-Yisrael Amora who appears at the very end of the daf.)
- **Rabba** (bar Naḥmani) — third-generation head of Pumbedita; asks whether a *living* animal becomes a
  handle for its limb (left unresolved).
- **Abaye** — fourth-generation head of Pumbedita; raises the cucumber-in-a-flowerpot dilemma.
- **Rabbi Yirmeya** — fourth-generation Eretz-Yisrael Amora, known for his sharp boundary-case questions;
  asks about the worshipped half-gourd.
- **Rabbi Zeira** — third-generation Amora who went up to Tiberias; asks about the leprous stone shared by
  two houses, and later reports the study-hall discussion to Rav Asi.
- **Rav** (Abba Arikha) — first-generation founder of Sura; source (via Rav Yehuda) of the verse *ki yamut
  min ha-behema*.
- **Rav Asi** — third-generation Eretz-Yisrael Amora, constant colleague of R' Ami; closes the daf with the
  difficulty he could not solve. (Again: **Rav Asi**, not **Rav Ashi** above.)

## Walking through the sugya, step by step

### 128a — Finishing R' Yoḥanan, and four more readings

**R' Yoḥanan's reversal.** The *Tevul Yom* mishna has R' Meir hold that a sliced-but-attached piece is one
*only if the large rises with the small* — the opposite of the dangling limb. *"מוּחְלֶפֶת הַשִּׁיטָה"* — the
names are reversed. The Gemara tries to dodge (perhaps R' Meir distinguishes the light impurity of a
*tevul yom* from others), but Rebbi teaches *"אֶחָד טְבוּל יוֹם וְאֶחָד שְׁאָר טוּמְאוֹת"* — touch is touch for
all of them; so **R' Yoshiya** fixes it: *according to Rebbi*, the attribution is reversed.

Then the bench lines up:

- **Rava:** *"יֵשׁ יָד לְטוּמְאָה וְאֵין יָד לְהֶכְשֵׁר"* — everyone agrees the body is a handle; they split on
  whether a handle works for *rendering susceptible* (R' Meir: yes) or only for *conveying impurity* (R'
  Shimon).
- **Rav Pappa:** they split on *"הֶכְשֵׁר קוֹדֶם מַחְשָׁבָה"* — the blood met the meat before the owner meant it
  for a gentile's plate. This is R' Akiva's own back-and-forth about the **fat of a village slaughter** (it
  needs intent but not fresh liquid) versus R' Yehuda's **endives** (picked for a beast, later meant for a
  person, they need liquid a *second* time). R' Akiva retracted toward R' Yehuda; R' Meir holds like the
  original teaching, R' Shimon like the retraction.
- **Rav Aḥa, son of Rav Ika:** they split on *"נִתְקַנֵּחַ הַדָּם בֵּין סִימָן לְסִימָן"* — the first-*siman*
  blood wiped away before the second cut. Is slaughter one act start-to-finish (R' Meir: this is blood of
  slaughter, it renders) or only its finish (R' Shimon: this is blood of a mere wound, it does not)?
- **Rav Ashi:** they split on *"שְׁחִיטָה מַכְשֶׁרֶת וְלֹא דָּם"* — for R' Shimon it is the *slaughter*, not its
  blood, that renders meat susceptible, and since slaughter never permits a dangling limb, it never renders
  the limb either.

### 128a–b — The chain of "does it become a handle?" — five times **teiku**

Now the same shape is pressed onto pair after pair, and every time the Gemara answers *"תֵּיקוּ"*:

1. **A living animal and its limb** (Rabba). The body is a handle for the limb — but a *living* animal is
   itself immune to impurity. Does it still serve as a handle to carry impurity to the dangling limb?
2. **The cucumber in the pot** (Abaye). Planted in an unperforated pot it is "detached" and susceptible;
   grown out over the ground it draws life and turns pure. R' Shimon rules *"הַטָּמֵא בְּטוּמְאָתוֹ, וְטָהוֹר
   בְּטׇהֳרָתוֹ"* — the inside stays impure, the outside pure. Does the pure outer part become a handle for the
   impure inner one?
3. **The worshipped gourd** (R' Yirmeya). Bow to half a gourd and that half is forbidden (and, for R'
   Shimon, no longer "food" that can be defiled). Does the forbidden half become a handle for its permitted
   twin?
4. **The fig branch hanging by its bark** (Rav Pappa). Per the Rabbis, if it can still live it is pure
   (attached), if not it is impure (detached). Does a branch that can live become a handle for a shoot off
   it that cannot?
5. **The leprous corner-stone** (R' Zeira). One stone spans two houses; you *extract* the whole stone but
   *demolish* only your own half. If leprosy strikes one house, does that half become a handle carrying
   impurity into the neighbor's home?

Every one stands unresolved — a rare, honest cluster of *teiku*.

### 128b — What defiles as "a limb from the living"?

**The mishna's gap.** *"מַאי אִיכָּא בֵּין אֵבֶר מִן הַחַי לְאֵבֶר מִן הַנְּבֵלָה?"* — the practical difference is
**flesh that later separates from the limb**: from a limb-of-the-living such flesh is pure; from a limb of a
carcass an olive-bulk of it defiles.

**The source.** Rav Yehuda in the name of Rav: a limb from the living defiles from *"וְכִי יָמוּת מִן
הַבְּהֵמָה"*. But that phrase is already needed for another law — *מִקְצָת בְּהֵמָה*, that a *tereifa* which was
slaughtered does *not* defile as a carcass. The answer: the Torah wrote *min ha-behema* in two words to teach
**two** things.

**Why not flesh too?** Because a baraita divides three ways over what qualifies:

- **R' Yosei HaGelili:** *"מָה מִיתָה שֶׁאֵינָהּ עוֹשָׂה חֲלִיפִין"* — just as death makes no replacement, only
  what the body does **not regrow** defiles. A limb is not regrown; flesh is.
- **R' Akiva:** *"מָה בְּהֵמָה גִּידִים וַעֲצָמוֹת"* — as an animal has **sinews and bones**, so a defiling part
  must have sinews and bones.
- **Rebbi:** as an animal has **flesh, sinews and bones**, so must the part.

**Where they part.** Between Rebbi and R' Akiva — the *"אַרְכּוּבָּה"*, the leg joint (bones and sinews, no
flesh): R' Akiva defiles it, Rebbi does not. Between R' Akiva and R' Yosei HaGelili — *"כּוּלְיָא וְנִיב
שְׂפָתַיִם"*, the kidney and upper lip (flesh, no bone, not regrown): R' Yosei defiles them, R' Akiva does not.
The very same three-way split, the Gemara notes, is taught for **creeping things** *(bemotam)* — and both
sources are needed, since an animal defiles only in olive-bulk but by carrying, while a creeping thing
defiles in lentil-bulk but not by carrying.

### 128b — Cut, or intend, first?

A closing baraita: cut an olive-bulk of flesh from a limb-of-the-living and *then* designate it as food —
**pure** (it met no impurity while it was food). Designate it first and *then* cut — **impure**. On that last
line Rav Asi, who had missed the study hall, tells R' Zeira he is stuck: *why* should intending-then-cutting
defile it? The knot he ties here is untied only on 129a.

## The distinctions, side by side

The baraita's three tannaim, and the three parts that split them. A limb "from the living" defiles like a
carcass; the question is which parts count. Every cell traces to the walkthrough above.

| Part of the animal | R' Yosei HaGelili *(not regrown)* | R' Akiva *(sinews + bones)* | Rebbi *(flesh + sinews + bones)* |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Leg joint** *(arkuva)* — bones & sinews, no flesh | Defiles (not regrown) | Defiles (has sinews & bones) | Pure (no flesh) |
| **Kidney / upper lip** — flesh, no bone, not regrown | Defiles (not regrown) | Pure (no bones) | Pure (no bones) |
| **Ordinary flesh** — regrown by the body | Pure (regrown) | Pure (no bones) | Pure (no bones) |

---

## One line to carry with you

> Ask three sages what makes a severed limb defile like death, and you get three answers — *what cannot be
> regrown*, *what has bone in it*, *what is a whole limb of flesh and bone* — and a leg joint, a kidney and a
> lip are enough to tell them apart.

## Chazara — test yourself

```yaml
- q: What does R' Yoḥanan mean by "the attribution is reversed" (muchlefet hashita)?
  opts:
    - The names in the Tevul Yom mishna should be swapped, so that R' Meir's ruling there stops clashing with his ruling here.
    - R' Meir later retracted his whole view on the dangling limb and came around instead to the position held by R' Shimon.
    - The tractate of Tevul Yom was mistakenly ascribed to the wrong author, and all of its rulings must now be reassigned elsewhere.
    - One who immersed that day and the other categories of impurity are governed by directly opposite rules of what counts as contact.
  correct: a
  why: Muchlefet hashita — swap the names in the Tevul Yom mishna, and R' Meir's two statements no longer conflict.
- q: On Rava's reading, what exactly do R' Meir and R' Shimon dispute?
  opts:
    - Whether a living animal is at all capable of serving as a handle for any one of its own parts in the first place.
    - Whether a handle works for conveying impurity to the food but does not work for rendering that same food susceptible.
    - Whether the blood of a valid slaughter is truly counted among the seven liquids that render a food susceptible.
    - Whether a tereifa that has been properly slaughtered still defiles those who touch it exactly as a carcass would.
  correct: b
  why: Yesh yad le-tum'a ve-ein yad le-hechsher — R' Shimon splits the two, R' Meir holds a handle works for both.
- q: In Rav Pappa's reading, what does R' Akiva's teaching about the fat of a village slaughter illustrate?
  opts:
    - That forbidden fat is never susceptible to impurity at all unless and until a gentile has actually eaten some of it.
    - That a slaughter performed out in a village is simply invalid unless one first intends the animal's meat to be food.
    - That susceptibility can take hold even where the liquid reached the food before anyone had yet thought to eat it.
    - That the blood of slaughter alone can render the fat susceptible, while ordinary water is never able to do so.
  correct: c
  why: The fat is rendered susceptible by the slaughter-blood even though intent came later — hechsher kodem machshava, the crux Rav Pappa assigns to the dispute.
- q: What is the question in Rav Aḥa's reading, where the blood was wiped between the two simanim?
  opts:
    - Whether the very first cut of a single siman has already rendered the whole animal permitted for eating.
    - Whether wiping the blood away counts as an interruption serious enough to invalidate the slaughter itself.
    - Whether slaughter is reckoned from start to finish, so the first-siman blood counts as blood of slaughter.
    - Whether a limb that has been wiped entirely clean of blood can still go on to convey carcass-impurity.
  correct: c
  why: If slaughter is one act start-to-finish it is blood of slaughter (renders); if only its end counts, the first-siman blood is mere blood of a wound.
- q: Rav Ashi says R' Shimon holds "slaughter renders susceptible, and not the blood." Why does that leave the dangling limb unrendered?
  opts:
    - Because no blood at all happened to reach the dangling limb over the whole course of the slaughter.
    - Because the slaughter does not permit the dangling limb, and so it does not render that limb susceptible either.
    - Because a limb can only ever be rendered susceptible to impurity by water, and never by an act such as slaughter.
    - Because R' Shimon holds a dangling limb is never food to begin with, and so it can never be susceptible at all.
  correct: b
  why: For R' Shimon the rendering flows from slaughter's permitting the meat; since the limb stays forbidden, the slaughter does not render it.
- q: The verse "if an animal dies" (ki yamut min habehema) is stretched to two teachings. What is the second, besides that a limb from the living defiles?
  opts:
    - That flesh which separates from a living animal likewise defiles anyone who touches it, just as a carcass does.
    - That a tereifa which was slaughtered does not defile as a carcass — the sense drawn from "part of an animal."
    - That a creeping thing conveys its impurity in the small measure of a lentil-bulk rather than in an olive-bulk.
    - That one who merely carries a carcass is defiled by it even without ever coming to touch it directly at all.
  correct: b
  why: Min ha-behema, in two words, teaches both a limb from the living and miktzat behema — the slaughtered tereifa that escapes carcass-impurity.
- q: What is R' Yosei HaGelili's test for whether a part defiles as a limb from the living?
  opts:
    - Whether the part happens to contain both sinews and bones within it, just as the whole animal's body does.
    - Whether the part amounts to at least an olive-bulk, which is the minimum measure needed for defiling.
    - Whether the body is unable to regrow the part, since death itself never generates any replacement.
    - Whether the part had at some earlier point been designated as food before it came to be severed off.
  correct: c
  why: Ma mita she-einah osah chalifin — only what the body does not regrow (a limb, not flesh) defiles like death.
- q: What case marks the practical difference between Rebbi and R' Akiva?
  opts:
    - The kidney, which is made up of flesh and contains no bone within it at all.
    - The leg joint (arkuva), which has sinews and bones in it but carries no flesh at all.
    - An olive-bulk of quite ordinary flesh, the kind that the body readily regrows over time.
    - The upper lip, which the animal does not regrow again once it has been removed from it.
  correct: b
  why: The arkuva has sinews and bones but no flesh — R' Akiva deems it a defiling limb, Rebbi (who needs flesh too) does not.
- q: What case marks the practical difference between R' Akiva and R' Yosei HaGelili?
  opts:
    - The kidney and the upper lip — flesh with no bone in it, which the body does not regrow.
    - The leg joint — bones and sinews together, but with no flesh at all upon it.
    - The hide of the animal, which stretches over and covers its entire body.
    - A single strand of sinew that joins a severed limb back onto the body.
  correct: a
  why: Kulya ve-niv sfatayim have no bone but are not regrown — R' Yosei defiles them, R' Akiva (who needs bone) does not.
- q: In the closing baraita, why is flesh cut from a limb-of-the-living impure if one intended it as food and then cut it, but pure if one cut it and then intended it?
  opts:
    - Because cutting the flesh away first destroys it, whereas intending it as food first preserves its standing as food.
    - Because intent before the cut leaves the flesh as food while still on the impure limb, whereas cutting first means it meets nothing impure.
    - Because a limb severed from the living can never convey any impurity once its own flesh has already been cut away from it.
    - Because a designation of something as food only takes effect at all if it is made while the animal is still alive.
  correct: b
  why: Designated first, the flesh is food while still on the impure limb and catches impurity; cut first, it is no longer joined to the limb when designated.
```
