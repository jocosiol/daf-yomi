## The big picture (read this first)

Today's page turns yesterday's principle inside out. Rav Ashi had ruled that whatever the Torah calls
**abominable** falls under "you shall not eat" — so the Gemara now presses the obvious next step: if the
*act* is abominable, shouldn't the *product* of the act be forbidden too? It runs the claim past six cases
in a row — and every single one, it turns out, is **permitted**. An abominable act, on its own, forbids
nothing; only a verse can.

> **הִיא קֹדֶשׁ, וְאֵין מַעֲשֶׂיהָ קֹדֶשׁ.** "**It** is sacred — but its **products** are not." The line
> that breaks the whole chain. Shabbat itself is holy; food cooked on it is not thereby forbidden. And once
> the most stringent case leaks, a *kal vachomer* carries the rest, until each abominable act is quietly
> released by its own verse or its own reason.

Then the daf flips to the opposite question. **Meat cooked in milk really is forbidden** — so from *where*?
The second half gathers the sources, one school after another, and then spends its hardest energy on a
single narrow point — how we know that not only eating but **benefit** is forbidden — until the argument
becomes a lesson in method itself, on exactly what may and may not knock down a derivation.

Two movements, in order:

1. **The abominable-product principle, tested to destruction** (115a). Cooking on Shabbat, plowing with an
   ox and a donkey, muzzling and threshing, diverse seeds, crossbred animals, a mother and its young, the
   sent-away mother bird — each is raised as "let its product be forbidden," and each is let go.
2. **From where meat in milk is forbidden** (115a–115b). Reish Lakish reads it from a spare word by the
   Paschal offering; Rabbi reads it from context; the schools of R' Eliezer, R' Yishmael, and Isi ben Yehuda
   each add a verse — and the benefit-ban sets off a long duel over *kal vachomer*, *gezeira shava*, and the
   rules of the common element.

## Key concepts & terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **תִּיעַבְתִּי לְךָ** *(tiavti lecha)* | "**I have made it abominable to you.**" The engine of the first half — whatever the Torah brands abominable is forbidden to eat, which raises the question of whether its by-products are forbidden too. |
| **מַעֲשֵׂה שַׁבָּת** *(maaseh Shabbat)* | The **product of an act done on Shabbat** — e.g., food cooked in violation of Shabbat. By rabbinic penalty the cook may not eat it, but others may. |
| **הִיא קֹדֶשׁ וְאֵין מַעֲשֶׂיהָ קֹדֶשׁ** *(hi kodesh...)* | "**It is sacred, but its products are not.**" The verse "for it is sacred to you" limits Shabbat's holiness to the day itself — food cooked on it is not thereby forbidden. |
| **כִּלְאֵי הַכֶּרֶם / כִּלְאֵי זְרָעִים** *(kilei hakerem / zeraim)* | **Diverse kinds in a vineyard** (forbidden even for benefit, and burned) versus **diverse kinds of seeds** sown in a field (whose produce, the daf shows, is permitted). |
| **פֶּן תִּקְדַּשׁ → פֶּן תּוּקַד אֵשׁ** *(pen tikdash → pen tukad esh)* | "Lest it be forfeited" **read as** "lest it be burned." The word-play that confines the burn-and-benefit ban to the vineyard alone. |
| **לַגָּבוֹהַּ / לְהֶדְיוֹט** *(lagavoha / lehedyot)* | "**For the Most High** (the altar) / **for an ordinary person.**" Repeatedly, the fact that something is barred from the altar is turned into proof that it is permitted to eat. |
| **שִׁילּוּחַ הַקֵּן** *(shiluach haken)* | **Sending away the mother bird** from the nest before taking the young (Deut. 22:6–7) — the last abominable-act case tested on the daf. |
| **לֹא אָמְרָה תּוֹרָה שַׁלַּח לְתַקָּלָה** *(...shalach letakala)* | "**The Torah did not say 'send it away' toward a mishap.**" Since the Torah tells you to free the bird, it cannot be forbidden — or others would eat it unwittingly. |
| **בָּשָׂר בְּחָלָב** *(basar b'chalav)* | **Meat cooked in milk** — the prohibition the whole chapter turns on, and whose Scriptural source the second half hunts down. |
| **מְבֻשָּׁל** *(mevushal)* | "**Boiled in any way.**" A word by the Paschal offering that Reish Lakish reads as spare — teaching that there is another forbidden cooking, meat in milk. |
| **דָּבָר הַלָּמֵד מֵעִנְיָנוֹ** *(davar halamed me'inyano)* | "**A matter derived from its context.**" One of the thirteen principles — Rabbi uses it to fix the redundant verse on meat in milk, since its neighbors speak of a "two-type" case. |
| **גְּזֵירָה שָׁוָה** *(gezeira shava)* | A **verbal analogy** between two verses sharing a word — how Rabbi and Isi ben Yehuda pin the ban down. Unlike a *kal vachomer*, it cannot be refuted by mere logic. |
| **שְׁעַת הַכּוֹשֶׁר** *(sh'at hakosher)* | A "**time when it was fit.**" *Orla* never had one; meat and milk each did — the stringency that keeps the a fortiori from *orla* from standing on its own. |
| **מָה הַצַּד** *(mah hatzad)* | Derivation **from the common element** of two source cases at once, rather than from either alone — and the rules for refuting it are the daf's closing subject. |
| **מִגּוּפוֹ / מֵעָלְמָא** *(migufo / me'alma)* | "**From itself / from an external case.**" Rav Mordechai's rule in Reish Lakish's name — a common-element derivation may be refuted only from within its own sources, never from an outside case. |

## Who's who in today's daf

**Tannaim**

- **Rabbi** (Yehuda HaNasi, "Rebbi") — last-generation Tanna and redactor of the Mishnah, of Eretz Yisrael;
  derives meat-in-milk from a redundant "you shall not eat it" read *by its context*, and its benefit-ban
  from a verbal analogy on *kadosh*.
- **The school of Rabbi Eliezer** (*d'vei Rabbi Eliezer*) — the study hall of **Rabbi Eliezer ben
  Hyrcanus**, second-generation Tanna and student of Rabban Yochanan ben Zakkai; reads the placement after
  the carcass verse as forbidding benefit — "do not cook it and sell it."
- **The school of Rabbi Yishmael** (*d'vei Rabbi Yishmael*) — the study hall of **Rabbi Yishmael**,
  third-generation Tanna and master of the thirteen hermeneutical principles; the threefold "you shall not
  cook a kid" for eating, benefit, and cooking.
- **Isi ben Yehuda** — a later Tanna (also known as Yosef of Hutzal / Isi ben Gur Aryeh); derives the ban by
  a verbal analogy on *kadosh* and the benefit-ban by an a fortiori from *orla*. On the finer details of his

- **Rabbi Yochanan** (bar Nafcha) — second-generation Amora of Tiberias, head of its academy; challenges

**Babylonian Amoraim**

- **Rav Ashi** — sixth-generation Amora of Mata Meḥasya, the great redactor of the Talmud; here on the

- **Rav Mordechai** — a Babylonian Amora around Rav Ashi's time, who transmits teachings in the name of

## Walking through the sugya, step by step

### 115a — The abominable act, and why its product is permitted anyway

The daf opens still inside yesterday's rule: **whatever the Torah calls abominable (״כׇּל שֶׁתִּיעַבְתִּי
לְךָ״) is forbidden to eat.** The Gemara now tests the obvious extension — if the act is abominable, let its
*product* be forbidden — against one case after another, and releases every one.

**Food cooked on Shabbat (מַעֲשֵׂה שַׁבָּת).** If abominable acts forbade their products, then food cooked on
Shabbat should be forbidden to **everyone** — desecrating Shabbat is surely "*tiavti lecha*." Yet the mishna
back on 15a rules that one who cooks on Shabbat deliberately may not eat it himself (a rabbinic penalty),
but **others may.** How? **The answer:**

> **אָמַר קְרָא ״כִּי קֹדֶשׁ הִיא לָכֶם״ — הִיא קֹדֶשׁ, וְאֵין מַעֲשֶׂיהָ קֹדֶשׁ.** "The verse says 'for it
> is sacred to you' — **it,** Shabbat, is sacred; **its products are not** sacred," i.e., not forbidden.

**Plowing with an ox and a donkey; muzzling a cow and threshing.** These too are "*tiavti lecha*" — let the
seeds and the threshed grain be forbidden! **The answer:** a *kal vachomer* from Shabbat. Now that we have
seen the products of **Shabbat** — the more stringent prohibition — are permitted, how much more so the
products of these lighter ones. (Rashi notes the limit: we do *not* run this inference at meat-in-milk,
because there one eats the abomination **itself**; here one eats not the plowing or the muzzling but only
what comes by them.)

**Diverse seeds (כִּלְאֵי זְרָעִים).** Let them be forbidden — "*tiavti lecha*"! **The answer:** the Torah
revealed the benefit-ban specifically at **diverse kinds in a vineyard** — reading "**פֶּן תִּקְדַּשׁ**,
lest it be forfeited" as "**פֶּן תּוּקַד אֵשׁ**, lest it be burned." Since the burn-and-benefit ban is stated
of the *vineyard*, by inference **diverse kinds of seeds are permitted.** *An objection:* perhaps vineyard
kinds are forbidden both to eat and to benefit, while seed-kinds are forbidden only to **eat**? *The answer:*
seed-kinds are juxtaposed to **crossbred animals** — "*your cattle you shall not mate... your field you
shall not sow*" — and just as the **offspring** of crossbred cattle is permitted, so the **produce** of
crossbred seeds is permitted.

**Crossbred animals (כִּלְאֵי בְהֵמָה).** And the offspring of crossbred animals itself — how do we know it
is permitted? **From** the fact that the Torah **forbids crossbred animals for the altar** (*lagavoha*);
learn *by inference* that **to ordinary people** (*lehedyot*) they are **permitted.**

**A mother and its young (אוֹתוֹ וְאֶת בְּנוֹ).** Slaughtered on the same day — let the meat be forbidden!
**The answer:** the Torah forbids an animal **whose time has not come** (*mechusar zman*, under eight days
old) **for the altar**; by inference such an animal is **permitted** to ordinary people. The young slaughtered
after its mother that day is itself like *mechusar zman*, since its ban lasts only that day.

**Sending away the nest (שִׁילּוּחַ הַקֵּן).** If one took the mother bird and the court made him free her,
that bird should be forbidden forever — an abominable act was done with her! **The answer:**

> **לֹא אָמְרָה תּוֹרָה ״שַׁלַּח״ לְתַקָּלָה.** "The Torah did not say 'send it away' toward a **mishap.**" If
> the freed bird were forbidden, the Torah would not have commanded releasing her — others might eat her
> unwittingly.

So the chain closes exactly where it started: an abominable act, of itself, forbids nothing. Each product is
loosed by its own verse or its own reason.

### 115a–115b — From where is meat in milk forbidden?

Now the opposite question. Meat cooked in milk **is** forbidden — so from where?

**Reish Lakish.** From the Paschal offering: **״אַל תֹּאכְלוּ מִמֶּנּוּ נָא וּבָשֵׁל מְבֻשָּׁל״** (Ex. 12:9).
The verse could have said simply "boiled"; the extra **״מְבֻשָּׁל״**, "boiled in any way," teaches that
**there is another cooking whose product is forbidden like this one — and that is meat in milk.**

**Rabbi Yochanan objects (115b).** **״כְּעוּרָה זוֹ שֶׁשָּׁנָה רַבִּי?״** — "Is the teaching that **Rabbi**
taught *ugly*, that you go looking for your own?" Rabbi had derived it from the **redundant "לֹא
תֹאכְלֶנּוּ״** by blood (Deut. 12:24–25): the verse "is speaking of meat in milk." And how do we know it
speaks of *that* and not some other prohibition? By **דָּבָר הַלָּמֵד מֵעִנְיָנוֹ**, a matter derived from
its context — the neighboring verses deal with redeemed *pesulei hamukdashin*, a **two-type** thing (edible
like non-sacred meat, yet forbidden to shear and work like consecrated); so here too the verse means a food
of **two types** — meat in milk.

**Reish Lakish answers.** My source is still needed. From **Rabbi's** verse alone I would know only that
**eating** is forbidden; the Paschal verse teaches that even **benefit** is forbidden — just as no benefit
may be derived from a disqualified Paschal offering.

**And Rabbi — benefit from where?** From a **gezeira shava**: here "*for you are a **holy** [kadosh]
people*," and there "*there shall be no **kadesh** [sodomite]*" (Deut. 23:18) — just as there the prohibition
is one of **benefit/enjoyment**, so here benefit is forbidden.

Three more sources follow:

- **The school of Rabbi Eliezer.** From the **placement** — "you shall not eat any carcass..." ends with "you
  shall not cook a kid in its mother's milk" — teaching that **when you sell** a carcass to a gentile, **you
  shall not cook it** in milk **and** sell it. Benefit is forbidden.
- **The school of Rabbi Yishmael.** "**Do not cook a kid in its mother's milk**" is written **three times** —
  one for the ban on **eating**, one on **benefit**, one on **cooking.**
- **Isi ben Yehuda** (baraita). A **gezeira shava**: here "a **holy** people," and there "you shall be **holy**
  men... flesh torn in the field you shall not eat" (Ex. 22:30) — just as there (a *tereifa*) is forbidden,
  so here. And **benefit**? By a *kal vachomer* from **orla**: if *orla*, in whose growth **no sin** was
  committed, is forbidden for benefit, then meat in milk, in which a **sin was** committed, all the more so.

### 115b — The benefit-ban, and a duel over method

Isi ben Yehuda's *kal vachomer* from *orla* is where the daf digs in.

**The refutations start.** *What about orla — it never had a **שְׁעַת הַכּוֹשֶׁר**, a time when it was fit?*
(Meat and milk were each permitted before they met.) *Let **leavened bread on Passover** prove it — it had a
time of fitness, and is still forbidden for benefit.* *What about chametz — it is punishable by **karet**?*
*Let **diverse kinds in a vineyard** prove it — no karet, and still forbidden for benefit.*

Now the Gemara turns on its own method:

**Why two tools — a gezeira shava for eating and a kal vachomer for benefit?** Let **all** of it come by *kal
vachomer* from *orla* (forbidden both to eat and to benefit → so meat in milk, both). **The answer:** that
inference can be **logically refuted** — plowing with an ox and a donkey, or muzzling and threshing, **prove**
it: a sin was committed there and the product is **permitted.** A *kal vachomer* can be broken by such a
counter-case; a **gezeira shava cannot.** So *eating* must rest on the verbal analogy — and only then does
*benefit* follow by inference.

**Why say "let the vineyard prove it"?** Say instead "let ***orla*** prove it," let the inference **revert**,
and derive the law from the **common element** (*mah hatzad*) of *orla* and chametz — each rejected for a
stringency the other lacks. **Rav Ashi:** you cannot, **because a carcass proves it invalid** — a carcass is
forbidden to eat, yet **permitted** for benefit, so "forbidden to eat → forbidden to benefit" fails.

> **הָכִי אָמְרִינַן מִשְּׁמֵיהּ דְּרֵישׁ לָקִישׁ: כֹּל ״מָה הַצַּד״ — מִגּוּפוֹ פָּרְכִינַן, מֵעָלְמָא לָא
> פָּרְכִינַן.** **Rav Mordechai to Rav Ashi:** "We say in Reish Lakish's name — any common-element
> derivation we refute **from within itself,** never **from an outside case.**" A carcass is external, so it
> does not touch the derivation.

**So let it come by the common element** of *orla* and chametz! *It can be refuted from within* — what both
share is that they are **produce that grows from the ground** (*gidulei karka*), unlike meat and milk. **But
then diverse kinds in a vineyard can be refuted the same way — it too grows from the ground!** And here Rav
Mordechai draws the last distinction, again in Reish Lakish's name:

> A **common-element** derivation may be refuted by **any** shared factor (*kol dehu*). But a **one-from-one**
> a fortiori — the "*no, if you say...*" form — may be refuted **only** by a genuine **stringency** (a *kal
> vachomer*), not by just any factor.

So when the vineyard is brought as a single case against a single case, "it grows from the ground" — a mere
*kol dehu*, not a stringency — does **not** break it. **But,** the Gemara presses on the last line of the
amud, **let us refute all three together** — *orla*, chametz, **and** the vineyard — for **all** of them grow
from the ground... and **Rav Mordechai answers, in Reish Lakish's name** — with a distinction that spills
onto 116a.

## The distinctions, side by side

The whole first half of 115a is one shape repeated six times — an abominable act is raised, its product is
claimed to be forbidden, and the claim is let go. Read down the last column and you see the point: **the act
never forbids the product; a verse or a reason always releases it.**

| Abominable act | The product at stake | How the Gemara permits it |
|---|---|---|
| **Cooking on Shabbat** | food cooked in violation | "It is sacred" — Shabbat, not its products |
| **Plowing with ox and donkey; muzzling and threshing** | the seeds, the grain | *Kal vachomer* — if stringent Shabbat's products are permitted, all the more these |
| **Diverse seeds** | the produce | "*Pen tikdash*" confines the burn-and-benefit ban to the vineyard alone |
| **Crossbred animals** | the offspring | Barred from the altar → by inference permitted to eat |
| **A mother and its young** | the young slaughtered that day | Like *mechusar zman*, barred from the altar → permitted |
| **Sending the nest** | the freed mother bird | "The Torah did not say 'send it' toward a mishap" |

---

## One line to carry with you

> An abominable *act* forbids nothing on its own — six times the Gemara reaches for the product and six times
> the Torah hands it back. And where the ban is real, as with meat in milk, it takes a verse, an analogy, or
> a carefully-guarded inference to establish it — never logic alone, walking in off the street.

    adds that benefit is forbidden too, like a disqualified Paschal offering.
- q: How does the school of Rabbi Yishmael account for the threefold "you shall not cook a kid in its
    mother's milk"?
  opts:
  - One teaches a cow, one a ewe, and one the mother goat named in the verse itself.
  - One forbids eating, one forbids benefit, and one forbids the act of cooking.
  - One addresses a kid, one an older animal, and one the mother's own meat in its own milk.
  - Two are needed for the gezeira shava on "holy," and the third forbids the cooking.
  correct: b
  why: The school of Rabbi Yishmael reads the three mentions as three separate bans — eating, benefit, and
    cooking.
- q: Why does the Gemara insist that the ban on eating meat in milk rest on a gezeira shava, rather than
    letting the whole law come by a single kal vachomer from orla?
  opts:
  - Because orla and meat cooked in milk share no common word in the Torah, so no verbal analogy between them is possible.
  - Because a kal vachomer can be refuted — plowing and muzzling show a sin done yet the product permitted.
  - Because the Sages never permit deriving a Torah-level eating prohibition from any inference at all.
  - Because orla is stringent in bearing karet, which meat in milk lacks, breaking the inference outright.
  correct: b
  why: The a fortiori from orla can be logically refuted by ox-and-donkey and muzzling — a sin, yet permitted;
    a gezeira shava cannot be so refuted, so eating rests on it and benefit follows.
- q: Rav Ashi refutes the common-element derivation by pointing to a carcass. How does Rav Mordechai, in Reish
    Lakish's name, answer him?
  opts:
  - A carcass is itself permitted for benefit, so it can never serve to refute any derivation at all.
  - A common-element derivation may be refuted only from within its own sources, never from an outside case.
  - A carcass is forbidden by rabbinic law alone, so it carries no weight against a Torah-level inference.
  - A common-element derivation may be refuted by absolutely any shared factor, so the carcass in fact does break it cleanly.
  correct: b
  why: Kol "mah hatzad" migufo parchinan, me'alma la parchinan — a common-element derivation is refuted only
    from within its own sources, and a carcass is external.
