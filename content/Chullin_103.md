## The big picture (read this first)

Yesterday closed on a contradiction. Rabbi Yochanan and Reish Lakish had divided the verses so that
eating **a limb from the living that is also a tereifa** should split them — yet the daf ended by
saying they *agree* it is two sets of lashes. Today opens by untangling that, and the whole first
half turns on a question we met on 102: while an animal is alive, does it already stand **divided
into its limbs**?

> When two — or three — prohibitions want to land on the very same bite, the order they arrive in
> decides everything. A prohibition that finds the ground already occupied usually cannot take hold;
> but if they all come into being **בַּהֲדֵי הֲדָדֵי**, at one and the same instant, none has priority
> and all of them stick. So the fight is really about *timing* — and whether a living creature is,
> in the eye of the law, already a bundle of separate limbs.

The daf falls into two very different halves, and then a seam:

1. **How many lashes** (103a) — a limb, a tereifa, and forbidden fat, and the arithmetic of two
   versus three sets, worked through Abaye's, the *ibba'it eima*'s, and Rava's competing pictures of
   *when* each prohibition is born.
2. **How much is "eating"** (103b) — an olive-bulk split in the mouth or split before it, food stuck
   in the teeth, a half-bite vomited and swallowed again: does the **throat's pleasure** decide, or
   the **arrival in the stomach**?
3. **הֲדַרַן — a new chapter** (103b) — *Gid HaNasheh* ends, and **כׇּל הַבָּשָׂר**, *Kol HaBasar*, the
   great chapter on meat and milk, begins with its opening mishnah.

## Key concepts & terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **בַּהֲדֵי הֲדָדֵי** *(bahadei hadadei)* | "At one and the same moment." When prohibitions are born together, none has priority and all take effect. |
| **בְּהֵמָה בְּחַיֶּיהָ לְאֵבָרִים עוֹמֶדֶת** *(behemah bechayeha le'evarim omedet)* | "A living animal already stands divided into limbs" — the pivot deciding whether the limb-prohibition is present from birth or only at severance. |
| **אִיסּוּר חָל עַל אִיסּוּר** *(issur chal al issur)* | Whether one prohibition can take effect on top of an already-existing one. |
| **חֵלֶב** *(chelev)* | Forbidden fat; here the third prohibition that can stack, and the key to the whole analogy. |
| **הוּתַּר מִכְּלָלוֹ** *(hutar mikelalo)* | "Permitted as an exception to its rule" — a loophole (fat of an undomesticated animal). A prohibition *with* a loophole is weaker, so a later one can land on it; *ever min hachai* has none. |
| **נֶהֱנָה גְּרוֹנוֹ בִּכְזַיִת** *(nehenah grono bikzayit)* | "His throat took pleasure in an olive-bulk" — Rabbi Yochanan's measure: liability follows the pleasure of the throat. |
| **אֲכִילָה בְּמֵעָיו בָּעֵינַן** *(achilah bemei'av ba'einan)* | "We require eating that reaches the stomach" — Reish Lakish's measure: the olive-bulk must arrive whole. |
| **חִלְּקוֹ מִבַּחוּץ / מִבִּפְנִים** *(chilko mibachutz / mibifnim)* | Divided the olive-bulk outside the mouth (all agree exempt) versus inside it (the dispute). |
| **גְּרוֹמִיתָא זְעֵירְתָּא** *(gromita ze'irta)* | A small bone swallowed whole, an olive-bulk of meat-bone-sinew together — how one can be liable even for Reish Lakish. |
| **מְחוּסַּר קְרִיבָה** *(mechusar keriva)* | "Lacking proximity" — Rabbi Elazar's point that separated pieces are not like a missing act. |
| **בֵּין הַשִּׁינַּיִם / בֵּין הַחֲנִיכַיִים** *(bein hashinayim / bein hachanichayim)* | Food stuck *between the teeth* (excluded by all) versus on the *gums/palate* (the disputed case). |

## Who's who in today's daf

**Eretz-Yisrael Amoraim**

- **Rabbi Yochanan** (bar Nappacha) — second generation, head of Tiberias. "Throat pleasure" decides;
  two sets for limb-and-tereifa.
- **Reish Lakish** (Rabbi Shimon ben Lakish) — second generation, Tiberias; Rabbi Yochanan's
  brother-in-law and great disputant. Requires the olive-bulk to reach the stomach.
- **Rabbi Chiyya bar Abba** — third generation, Tiberias; a leading transmitter of Rabbi Yochanan's
  teachings. Reports "two" for fat from a living tereifa. Not Rabbi Chiyya the Tanna, and not Rabbi
  Chiyya bar Yosef.
- **Rabbi Ami** — third generation, a head of the Tiberias academy after Rabbi Yochanan's circle.
  Insists the count is three.
- **Rabbi Abbahu** — third generation, Caesarea; a prominent student of Rabbi Yochanan. Restates the
  ruling as three.
- **Rabbi Asi** — third generation, Tiberias, often paired with Rabbi Ami. The one who "forgot" his
  own tradition; **not** Rav Ashi the Babylonian redactor.
- **Rabbi Elazar** (ben Pedat) — third generation, moved from Babylonia to Eretz Yisrael; here he
  extends liability to a limb split outside the mouth, and gently reminds Rabbi Asi. Not the Tannaim
  named Elazar met on 102.

**Babylonian Amoraim**

- **Rav Yosef** (bar Chiyya) — third generation, Pumbedita. Resolves the contradiction by splitting
  one animal from two.
- **Abaye** — fourth generation, head of Pumbedita; Rava's lifelong disputant. Frames the "born at
  birth" case.
- **Rava** — fourth generation, Machoza. Offers the reversed case: tearing a limb *is* what makes the
  beast a tereifa.
- **Rav Dimi** and **Ravin** — fourth generation travellers (*nachotei*) who carried Eretz-Yisrael
  teachings to Babylonia, here in slightly different versions of the same exchange.
- **Rav Kahana** — a common name across several generations; here the one who supplies the
  swallowed-bone case. Given the ambiguity I would not pin down which Rav Kahana this is.
- **Rav Pappa** — fifth generation, founder of the academy at Naresh. Narrows the teeth-versus-gums
  dispute.

*Look-alikes worth flagging:* **Rabbi Asi** the Eretz-Yisrael Amora (with a *Rabbi*) is not **Rav
Ashi** the Babylonian. **Rabbi Elazar** here is the Amora ben Pedat, not the Tannaim Elazar ben
Shammua or Elazar bar Rabbi Shimon from 102. And **Rabbi Chiyya bar Abba** the Amora is not **Rabbi
Chiyya** the Tanna.

## Walking through the sugya, step by step

### 103a — Three prohibitions, and the moment each is born

**The contradiction, laid out.** One who ate a limb from a living animal that is *also* a tereifa:

> **רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן אָמַר: חַיָּיב שְׁתַּיִם, וְרַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן בֶּן לָקִישׁ אָמַר: אֵינוֹ חַיָּיב אֶלָּא אַחַת.**
> Rabbi Yochanan says he is liable for two sets of lashes; Reish Lakish says only one.

For Rabbi Yochanan this is easy — limb and tereifa come from two separate verses. For Reish Lakish
it is hard: yesterday he seemed to agree that limb-plus-tereifa is two.

**Rav Yosef resolves it by counting animals.** *Two* animals — a limb from one, tereifa flesh from
another — everyone agrees, two sets. The dispute is only about **one** animal: a limb torn from a
single beast that is itself a tereifa.

**But in one animal, where exactly is the disagreement?** Three answers, each a different snapshot of
timing.

- **Abaye.** Take an animal that became a tereifa **עִם יְצִיאַת רוּבָּהּ** — as the majority of it
  emerged from the womb, i.e. right at birth. One Sage holds **בְּהֵמָה בְּחַיֶּיהָ לְאֵבָרִים עוֹמֶדֶת**
  — even alive it already stands divided into limbs — so the limb-prohibition and the tereifa-
  prohibition are born **בַּהֲדֵי הֲדָדֵי** and both stick. The other holds it does *not* stand divided,
  so the limb-prohibition only arrives later, when the limb is actually severed, and by then the
  tereifa is already in place and blocks it.
- **וְאִיבָּעֵית אֵימָא** — or say instead: all agree it is *not* divided into limbs, and they argue
  only over whether the limb-prohibition (which binds gentiles too, and so is weightier) can land on
  an existing tereifa-prohibition.
- **Another** *ibba'it eima*: all agree it *is* divided into limbs, the animal became tereifa
  *afterward*, and they argue whether the *tereifa*-prohibition can land on the already-standing
  limb-prohibition.
- **Rava** flips the picture: one **תָּלַשׁ מִמֶּנָּה אֵבֶר וּטְרָפָהּ בּוֹ** — tore off a limb and *by that
  very act* rendered the animal a tereifa. Now the two prohibitions truly are simultaneous — unless
  you hold the beast was already divided into limbs, in which case the limb-prohibition got there
  first and the tereifa cannot pile on.

**Now add forbidden fat.** Rabbi Chiyya bar Abba in Rabbi Yochanan's name: one who ate **חֵלֶב** from
a living tereifa is liable **two**. Rabbi Ami objects — say **three**! And it is restated (Rabbi
Abbahu / Rabbi Yochanan) as three. With a beast that became tereifa at birth, "three" holds that
alive it stands divided into limbs, so *chelev*, *ever* and *tereifa* are all born together and all
stick; "two" holds it is not divided, so the limb-prohibition alone fails to land.

**Why fat is the odd one out.** The clincher is an analogy the Gemara has used before: the Torah
deliberately let the prohibitions of *nevelah* and *tereifa* take effect on top of *chelev* — because
**חֵלֶב הוּתַּר מִכְּלָלוֹ**, forbidden fat has a permitted exception (the fat of an undomesticated
animal is allowed). A prohibition with a loophole is "weaker," so a later prohibition can still land
on it. But *ever min hachai* has **no** such exception — nothing about it is ever permitted — so the
tereifa-prohibition cannot take effect on it. (This last line runs over onto 103b.)

### 103b — How much is "eating," and where does it count

The chapter's last movement leaves the arithmetic of lashes for the mechanics of a *kezayit*.

**Split it before it reaches the mouth, or inside it?** Rav Dimi, and then Ravin, bring Reish
Lakish's question to Rabbi Yochanan. If one took an olive-bulk limb and **חִלְּקוֹ מִבַּחוּץ** — divided
it outside the mouth — and ate the pieces separately, all agree he is **exempt**. But if he put a
whole olive-bulk in and split it **מִבִּפְנִים**, inside, swallowing the halves one after the other —
here they divide:

> **רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן אָמַר: חַיָּיב, הֲרֵי נֶהֱנָה גְּרוֹנוֹ בִּכְזַיִת. וְרֵישׁ לָקִישׁ אָמַר: פָּטוּר, אֲכִילָה בְּמֵעָיו בָּעֵינַן וְלֵיכָּא.**
> Rabbi Yochanan: liable — his **throat took pleasure in an olive-bulk**. Reish Lakish: exempt — we
> require an act of eating that **reaches the stomach as an olive-bulk**, and here it does not.

**Then how could Reish Lakish ever convict?** If the food is always broken up before swallowing —
**Rav Kahana:** in the case of **גְּרוֹמִיתָא זְעֵירְתָּא**, a small bone one can swallow whole, whose
olive-bulk is meat, bone and sinew together.

**Rabbi Elazar goes further** than both: even divided outside the mouth he is liable — **מְחוּסַּר
קְרִיבָה לָאו כִּמְחוּסַּר מַעֲשֶׂה דָּמֵי**, merely lacking *proximity* between the pieces is not like
lacking an *act*; he still ate a whole olive-bulk.

**Food caught in the teeth.** Reish Lakish: the olive-bulk they measure is what you swallow, **חוּץ
מִשֶּׁל בֵּין הַשִּׁינַּיִם**, not counting what stays stuck between the teeth. Rabbi Yochanan: count even
that. **Rav Pappa** narrows the real dispute: what is truly wedged *between the teeth* (**בֵּין
הַשִּׁינַּיִם**), all agree, does not count; they argue only about what sits **בֵּין הַחֲנִיכַיִים**, on the
gums — and it is the same split, throat-pleasure versus stomach-arrival.

**Half a bite, brought back up.** Rabbi Asi / Rabbi Yochanan: one who ate half an olive-bulk,
vomited it, then ate another half — **liable**, because his throat took pleasure in a full olive-bulk
even though a whole one never reached the stomach.

**And a small, very human story.** Rabbi Elazar asked Rabbi Asi about a subtler case — half an
olive-bulk vomited and then *the same piece* eaten again — and the Gemara realizes the question was
not really a question: Rabbi Asi had simply **forgotten** a ruling he himself used to say before
Rabbi Yochanan, and Rabbi Elazar was gently jogging his memory. Rabbi Asi sat silent. Rabbi Elazar
pressed: **מוֹפֵת הַדּוֹר** — "Wonder of the generation! Did you not say this many times before Rabbi
Yochanan, and did he not answer you: *his throat took pleasure in an olive-bulk*?"

### 103b — הֲדַרַן: Gid HaNasheh closes, Kol HaBasar opens

With that the seventh chapter ends — **הֲדַרַן עֲלָךְ גִּיד הַנָּשֶׁה** — and the eighth, **כׇּל הַבָּשָׂר**,
begins with the mishnah that will occupy us for pages:

> **כׇּל הַבָּשָׂר אָסוּר לְבַשֵּׁל בְּחָלָב, חוּץ מִבְּשַׂר דָּגִים וַחֲגָבִים.**
> All meat is forbidden to cook in milk, except the flesh of fish and grasshoppers.

And a rabbinic fence beside it: one may not even **bring meat up onto the table** together with
cheese — again, except fish and grasshoppers — lest the two be eaten together. Which meats fall under
the Torah's prohibition and which under the Sages', and why fish escapes entirely, is where tomorrow
begins.

## The distinctions, side by side

One question runs through the whole second half — does liability follow the **throat's pleasure** or the **arrival in the stomach**? Asked of each case in turn, it also shows where the two do *not* argue:

| The case | R' Yochanan — *נֶהֱנָה גְּרוֹנוֹ* | Reish Lakish — *אֲכִילָה בְּמֵעָיו* |
|---|---|---|
| Split **outside** the mouth, the halves eaten separately | Exempt | Exempt |
| A whole olive-bulk in, split **inside**, the halves swallowed in turn | **Liable** | **Exempt** |
| Wedged **בֵּין הַשִּׁינַּיִם**, between the teeth | Does not count | Does not count |
| Sitting **בֵּין הַחֲנִיכַיִים**, on the gums | **Counts** | **Does not count** |
| Half an olive-bulk, vomited, then another half | **Liable** | Not stated |

Two voices sit outside the grid: **Rabbi Elazar**, who convicts even in the first row — lacking mere *proximity* is not lacking an act — and **Rav Kahana**, who shows how Reish Lakish can ever convict at all, with a *גְּרוֹמִיתָא זְעֵירְתָּא* swallowed whole.

## One line to carry with you

> Whether a second prohibition can take hold is rarely about how severe it is — it is about whether
> it arrived a moment too late. Land together, and everything sticks.
