## The big picture (read this first)

Yesterday's page left Abaye's question hanging in the air: liver bleeds into whatever it touches — so
does it **forbid the piece of meat cooked with it**? Today the Gemara refuses to let it go. It brings a
real meal as proof, watches that proof crumble under Rav Ashi's cross-examination, and finally lands on
a startling resolution from Rava: perhaps the whole premise is wrong and **liver expels no blood at
all** once it meets heat.

> **כִּי הֵיכִי דְּפָלֵיט — הֲדַר בָּלַע.** "Just as it expels, it absorbs right back." Rava's lever: if
> you say the scalding vinegar drinks in the liver's blood, then the liver must drink it back too and be
> forbidden itself — which no one holds. So nothing was expelled to begin with.

That is the first half of the daf. The second half changes subject but keeps the same engine —
**absorbed flavor** — and asks how far it travels. A knife that slaughtered, a bowl that salted meat, a
radish cut on a meaty blade, a hot fish set down in a meat dish: in each, a forbidden or meaty taste has
soaked into something that then meets milk. Here **Rav and Shmuel divide** over a principle that will
echo through all of *kashrut*: is second-hand flavor — taste that passed through a vessel before
reaching your food — still strong enough to forbid?

Three movements:

1. **Closing the liver question** (111a) — the windpipe meal, Rav Ashi's objections, the boiling-vinegar
   scald, and the *baraita* that turns Abaye's dilemma into a *tannaitic* dispute.
2. **How to prepare a liver** (111a) — the artery full of blood, tearing it crosswise, the spleen that
   needs no tearing, and liver-over-meat versus udder-over-meat on the spit.
3. **Absorbed flavor through vessels** (111b) — the salted bowl, *maliach k'rotei'ach*, the cut radish,
   and Rav vs. Shmuel on the fish in the meat dish: *notein ta'am bar notein ta'am*.

## Walking through the sugya, step by step

### 111a — The liver question, run to ground

The page opens mid-argument. The worry about liver cooked with meat is really two worries: the meat
might drink in the liver's **fat**, or it might drink in its **blood** — and the Gemara suggests blood
may be **absorbed less easily** than fat. So the dilemma narrows: does permitted liver's blood cross
into the meat at all?

**A meal offered as proof.** When **Rav Safra** went up to Eretz Yisrael again he found **Rabbi Zerika**,
who told him the question needs no asking: he and Yannai, son of Rabbi Ami, were once served a whole
**windpipe with everything hanging from it** — lungs, heart, and liver cooked together —

> **קָרִיבוּ לַן קַנְיָא בְּקוֹפֵיהּ, וַאֲכַלְנָא.** "They brought us the windpipe with its attachments,
> and we ate it."

— and no one worried that the liver's blood had forbidden the rest.

**The proof is picked apart.** **Rav Ashi** (some say **Rabbi Shmuel of Zerokinya**) objects: maybe the
**mouth of the windpipe stuck out of the pot**, so the liver's blood drained off instead of soaking in;
or maybe they **scalded** the liver first (*chalit*) — like **Rav Huna**, whose liver was doused with
boiling vinegar, and **Rav Naḥman**, whose was doused with boiling water — searing the blood inside
before it could spread. Abaye's question stands unresolved.

**The scald generates its own ruling.** **Rav Pappa**, before **Rava**, thought the boiling **vinegar**
becomes forbidden, having absorbed the liver's blood. Rava overturns it with a lever:

> **אִי חַלָּא אֲסִיר — אִיהוּ נָמֵי אֲסִיר, כִּי הֵיכִי דְּפָלֵיט — הֲדַר בָּלַע.** "If the vinegar is
> forbidden, the liver is forbidden too — for just as it expels, it absorbs back."

Since everyone agrees the scalded liver stays permitted, the only coherent reading is that **no blood is
expelled at all**. (Then a small table-scene: **Rav bar Shabba** declined the boiled liver at Rav
Naḥman's; told a Torah scholar was fasting on it, Rav Naḥman said, "**Feed Shabba against his will**.")

**The dispute behind it all.** Abaye's question, the Gemara notes, is really *tannaitic*:

> **הַכָּבֵד אוֹסֶרֶת וְאֵינָהּ נֶאֱסֶרֶת... שֶׁפּוֹלֶטֶת וְאֵינָהּ בּוֹלַעַת.** **Rabbi Eliezer:** liver
> forbids others but is never forbidden — it expels and does not absorb. **Rabbi Yishmael son of Rabbi
> Yoḥanan ben Beroka:** if it was **spiced** (*metubelet*) or **thoroughly stewed** (*sheluka*), it
> forbids others *and* is itself forbidden — the spices and heavy cooking drive the blood back in.

### 111a — Preparing a liver, and the spit

**Rabba bar Rav Huna** dined at **Rabba bar Rav Naḥman's** on Shabbat — three *se'a* of fine bread
kneaded in oil and honey — and found among the dishes a liver with an **artery** (*simpona*) still full
of blood. He objected: the blood **soaked through** the liver is permitted, but blood **standing in the
vessels** is forbidden. The fix:

> **קְרַעוּ שְׁתִי וָעֵרֶב, וְחִיתּוּכָא לְתַחַת.** "Tear it lengthwise and crosswise, and lay the cut
> side downward" — so the vessel-blood runs out onto the fire.

**And only the liver.** The **spleen** (*techala*) needs no such tearing — it holds only **fat**, not
pooled blood, which is why Shmuel's servants would make him a spleen dish on his bloodletting days.

**On the spit.** An anonymous ruling: liver skewered **above** meat in the oven leaves the meat
**permitted**, because the blood merely **slides off** (*mishrak sharik*); an **udder** above the meat
**forbids** it, because the milk **clings and soaks in** (*saroche mesarech*). **Rav Dimi of Neharde'a**
taught the reverse: an udder on top is **permitted** (its milk-in-meat is only **rabbinic**), while a
liver on top is **forbidden** (blood is **Torah law**). **Mareimar** ruled publicly for the public:
liver or udder, **underneath** the meat is permitted; **on top**, only *after the fact* (*di'avad*), not
*ab initio* (*l'chatchila*). When **Rav Ashi** saw his father-in-law Rami bar Abba's son skewering liver
over meat —

### 111b — Blood that floats, and flavor through vessels

— he scolded, "**How haughty this scholar!** Granted the Sages permitted meat roasted under liver
*after the fact* — did they permit doing it *ab initio*?" And he adds: if there is a **drip-pan**
(*bei dugei*) beneath the spit, even meat placed **above** the liver is forbidden — the blood falls into
the collected fat and one may come to eat the mixture. Why is this worse than roasting **plain meat**
over such a pan?

> **דְּמָא דְּבִשְׂרָא — שָׁכֵן, דְּמָא דְּכַבְדָּא — קָפֵי.** "The blood of meat **sinks**; the blood of
> liver **floats**." Meat's blood settles below the fat and can be skimmed away; liver's blood rides on
> top and cannot be separated — so the whole panful is forbidden.

**Now the pivot to vessels.** **Rav Naḥman in Shmuel's name:** the **slaughtering knife** absorbs blood
from the animal's heat, so one may not cut **boiling** food with it; for **cold** food, some require a
rinse of the cut piece, some do not. **Rav Yehuda in Shmuel's name:** a **bowl in which meat was
salted** may not hold boiling food — and this is Shmuel to his own principle:

> **מָלִיחַ הֲרֵי הוּא כְּרוֹתֵחַ, וְכָבוּשׁ הֲרֵי הוּא כִּמְבוּשָּׁל.** "**Salted** is like **boiling**,
> and **marinated** is like **cooked**" — salt and pickling drive flavor as heat does.

**Ravin** reported the opposite in **Rabbi Yoḥanan's** name — salted is *not* like boiling. **Abaye**
refutes the citation: a bowl in **Rabbi Ami's** house, used to salt meat, was **smashed** by Rabbi Ami —
a student of Rabbi Yoḥanan. Why smash it, if not because he heard from Rabbi Yoḥanan that **salted is
like boiling**? So Ravin's version must be mistaken.

**The radish and the taste-test.** **Rav Kahana**, Rav Yehuda's brother, taught that a **radish**
(*tzenon*) cut with a meat knife may be eaten with **kutaḥ** — even though its sharpness absorbs the
knife's fat. Why does the bowl forbid but the radish not? **Abaye:** the radish absorbed a **permitted**
substance (fat), the bowl a **forbidden** one (blood). **Rava** rejects this — permitted fat that leads
to eating meat-and-milk is no better — and offers the real distinction: **you can taste the radish**
before eating it with milk, but you cannot taste the contents of the bowl. **Rav Pappa:** then let a
**gentile cook** (*kapila*) taste it — as we say about a *teruma* pot, where a priest tastes! **Rava**
concedes: indeed, where a gentile cook is available he tastes; I spoke of where none is to be had.

**Fish in a meat dish — Rav vs. Shmuel.** A hot **fish** set into a bowl **in which meat was eaten**,
then eaten with **kutaḥ**:

> **רַב אָמַר: אָסוּר... נוֹתֵן טַעַם הוּא; וּשְׁמוּאֵל אָמַר: מוּתָּר, נוֹתֵן טַעַם בַּר נוֹתֵן טַעַם הוּא.**
> **Rav:** forbidden — the meat-flavor passes straight into the fish. **Shmuel:** permitted — the flavor
> went first into the **bowl** and only then into the fish: **flavor of a flavor**, too weak to forbid.

Rav's view, the Gemara notes, was only **inferred** — from his remark, when an eye-ointment's taste
carried through into a later dish, "**it gives so much flavor!**" — and even that is discounted, since
the ointment was exceptionally bitter. Two stories seal it: **Rabbi Elazar**, Rav's disciple, refused
the fish at Shmuel's table until Rav himself assured him, "God forbid the seed of Abba bar Abba would
feed me what I hold forbidden"; and two sages **across the Sura ford** traded the jibe "**Orphan!**" —
one holding like Shmuel on the fish, the other like Rabbi Ḥiyya, that bread and wine exempt all the foods
and drinks of the meal from a separate blessing. **Chizkiya in Abaye's name** rules the halakha: the
**fish is permitted** with kutaḥ, but the **radish is forbidden** — and *that* qualification runs into
tomorrow's daf.

## The distinctions, side by side

The hinge of the daf is what sits **on top of the meat** on the spit, and the three authorities split
cleanly by *which* prohibition — blood or milk — they weight more heavily.

| On top of the meat | Anonymous ruling | Rav Dimi of Neharde'a | Mareimar (halakha) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Liver** — blood, Torah law | Permitted — blood slides off | **Forbidden** | *Di'avad* yes, *l'chatchila* no |
| **Udder** — milk, rabbinic | **Forbidden** — milk clings | Permitted | *Di'avad* yes, *l'chatchila* no |

Underneath the meat, every view permits both; the whole dispute is only about the organ set *above*.

## Key concepts & terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **קַנְיָא בְּקוֹפֵיהּ** *(kanya b'kofei)* | The **windpipe with all its attachments** — lungs, heart, and liver — cooked as one; Rabbi Zerika's meal, offered as proof that liver's blood does not forbid the meat around it. |
| **חָלֵיט** *(chalit)* | To **scald / sear** in a boiling liquid (vinegar or water) at the outset, sealing the blood inside the liver before it can diffuse — Rav Ashi's alternative reading of the windpipe meal. |
| **כִּי הֵיכִי דְּפָלֵיט הֲדַר בָּלַע** *(ki heichi d'palit hadar bala)* | "Just as it expels, it absorbs back" — Rava's lever: were the scald to draw blood out, the liver would draw it back and be forbidden itself; since it is not, nothing was expelled. |
| **מְתוּבֶּלֶת** *(metubelet)* | **Spiced** liver; per Rabbi Yishmael b. R' Yoḥanan b. Beroka, spicing (like heavy stewing, *sheluka*) makes the liver reabsorb its blood, so it is forbidden as well as forbidding. |
| **סִמְפּוֹנָא** *(simpona)* | The large **blood vessel / artery** in the liver; blood soaked into the flesh is permitted, but blood standing in the *simpona* is forbidden and must be drained. |
| **שְׁתִי וָעֵרֶב** *(shesi va'erev)* | "**Warp and woof**" — tearing the liver **lengthwise and crosswise**, cut side down, so the vessel-blood runs out onto the fire. |
| **מִשְׁרָק שָׁרֵיק** *(mishrak sharik)* | "It merely **slides off**" — why liver set above meat does not forbid it: the blood glides over the surface rather than soaking in (contrast the udder's milk, which *saroche mesarech*, clings and absorbs). |
| **דִּיעֲבַד / לְכַתְּחִלָּה** *(di'avad / l'chatchila)* | **After the fact** vs. **at the outset**. Mareimar's ruling: liver or udder set over meat is tolerated *di'avad* but may not be arranged that way *l'chatchila*. |
| **מָלִיחַ הֲרֵי הוּא כְּרוֹתֵחַ** *(maliach k'rotei'ach)* | "**Salted is like boiling**" (and *kavush k'mevushal*, marinated like cooked) — Shmuel's principle that salting and pickling transfer flavor as heat does, so a bowl that salted meat forbids boiling food placed in it. |
| **כּוּתָּח** *(kutaḥ)* | A **Babylonian dip** made with milk (and bread-mold and salt); the milk food against which every case here is tested — may the meaty knife-cut radish, or the fish, be eaten with it? |
| **נוֹתֵן טַעַם בַּר נוֹתֵן טַעַם** *(notein ta'am bar notein ta'am)* | "**Flavor of a flavor**" — taste that reached the food only second-hand, through a vessel; Shmuel holds it too weak to forbid (so the fish is permitted), Rav holds it forbids. |
| **קַפִּילָא** *(kapila)* | A **gentile cook** who can be trusted to taste a dish and report whether it carries a forbidden flavor — Rav Pappa's proposal, which Rava accepts where such a cook is on hand. |

## Who's who in today's daf

**Tannaim**

- **Rabbi Eliezer** (ben Hyrcanus) — second-generation *Tanna*, student of Rabban Yoḥanan ben Zakkai;
  holds liver "forbids but is not forbidden." (Titled *Rabbi*; not the Amora Rabbi Elazar ben Pedat, who
  also appears below.)
- **Rabbi Yishmael son of Rabbi Yoḥanan ben Beroka** — fourth-generation *Tanna*; holds spiced or stewed
  liver becomes forbidden as well. A minor figure, known mainly from such rulings.
- **Rabbi Ḥiyya** (the Great) — transitional *Tanna*, uncle and teacher of Rav; cited at the end for the
  rule that bread and wine exempt the meal's other foods and drinks from a separate blessing.

**Eretz-Yisrael Amoraim**

- **Rabbi Yoḥanan** (bar Nappaḥa) — second generation, the towering authority of Tiberias; Ravin reports
  him as holding salted is *not* like boiling, and Abaye argues the citation is wrong.
- **Rabbi Zerika** — third/fourth generation; offers Rav Safra the windpipe meal as proof.
- **Rabbi Ami** — third generation, a leading authority in Tiberias and a student of Rabbi Yoḥanan; his
  smashing of the salting-bowl is Abaye's evidence.
- **Rabbi Elazar** (ben Pedat) — third generation, foremost student of Rabbi Yoḥanan; a disciple of Rav's
  teachings who declines the fish at Shmuel's table. (Rabbi *Elazar*; distinct from the *Tanna* Rabbi
  *Eliezer* above.)
- **Ravin** (Rav Avin) — fourth generation, famed carrier of Eretz-Yisrael traditions to Babylonia
  (*ki ata Ravin*); his report in Rabbi Yoḥanan's name is here disputed.

**Babylonian Amoraim**

- **Rav** (Abba Arikha) — first generation, founder of Sura; forbids the fish eaten with kutaḥ (*notein
  ta'am*), and reassures Rabbi Elazar that Shmuel never fed him anything he held forbidden.
- **Shmuel** (of Nehardea) — first generation, Rav's great colleague and son of **Abba bar Abba**;
  permits the fish (*notein ta'am bar notein ta'am*) and is the source of *maliach k'rotei'ach*. His
  servants prepared spleen for him on bloodletting days.
- **Rav Yehuda** (bar Yeḥezkel) — second generation, founder of Pumbedita; transmits Shmuel on the salted
  bowl. (This **Rav** Yehuda the Amora, not **Rabbi** Yehuda the *Tanna*.)
- **Rav Huna** — second generation, head of Sura after Rav; his liver was scalded with boiling vinegar.
- **Rav Naḥman** (bar Yaakov) — third generation, of Nehardea; his liver was scalded with boiling water,
  and he ordered "feed Shabba against his will."
- **Rav Kahana** — here specified as **the brother of Rav Yehuda**; teaches the permitted knife-cut
  radish. (The name spans several sages; this one is fixed only by the "brother of Rav Yehuda" tag.)
- **Rabba bar Rav Huna** — third generation, son of Rav Huna; the guest who found the blood-filled artery.
  (Not to be confused with **Rav bar Shabba**, the guest who declined the boiled liver just above him in
  the daf.)
- **Rabba bar Rav Naḥman** — the Shabbat host with the fine oil-and-honey bread.
- **Rava** (bar Yosef bar Ḥama) — fourth generation, of Meḥoza; supplies the lever that no blood is
  expelled, and the "you can taste the radish" distinction. (רבא; distinct from **Rabba** above — a
  classic look-alike.)
- **Abaye** — fourth generation, head of Pumbedita; refutes Ravin from Rabbi Ami's smashed bowl, and (via
  Chizkiya) rules the halakha on fish and radish. His was the original liver dilemma.
- **Rav Pappa** — fifth generation, student of Rava; proposes the boiling-vinegar liver is forbidden (Rava
  overrules), and proposes the gentile cook as taster.
- **Rav Ashi** — sixth generation, redactor of the Talmud at Sura-Mata Meḥasya; objects to the windpipe
  proof and scolds his father-in-law's son over the spit. (Rav *Ashi*, not Rav *Asi* the earlier Amora.)
- **Rav Dimi of Neharde'a** — late Amora; taught the reversed ruling on liver and udder over meat.
- **Mareimar** — late Amora of Sura; rules publicly on liver/udder over meat.
- **Chizkiya** — here the tradent who states, in Abaye's name, the final halakha on fish and radish.
- **Rav bar Shabba** — a minor figure; the guest who fasted rather than eat Rav Naḥman's boiled liver.
- **Rav Ḥiyya bar Ashi** — third generation, student of Rav; one of the two sages across the Sura ford.
  (Rav *Ḥiyya bar Ashi*, an Amora — not the *Tanna* Rabbi Ḥiyya above.)
- **Rav Shimi bar Ḥiyya** — Rav's great-grandson, at whose house the eye-ointment story occurs. I would
  not press further biographical detail on him.

## One line to carry with you

> Before you ask how far a flavor travels, ask whether it ever left home: sometimes, as with the liver
> that "expels and absorbs right back," the cleanest answer is that nothing was ever given off at all.
