## The big picture (read this first)

Two very different halves meet on this page. The first finishes the long fight carried over from
yesterday: **can a forbidden taste that soaked into a piece be "wrung back out" and forgiven?** The
Gemara pins Rabbi's compromise down to its logical spine, and the answer is *no* — a piece that turned
stays turned. The second half opens a fresh mishna about two organs, the **udder** and the **heart**,
and with it a question that sounds technical but is really about what *counts* as milk and blood at all.

> The udder is full of milk and yet, cooked whole, tastes of "meat seethed in its own milk" — and is
> permitted. Why? Because the milk in an udder **never became milk**: it was never *collected in the
> animal's innards*, only embedded in the flesh. That single test — **כָּנוּס בְּמֵעָיו**, "collected
> in the innards" — divides the permitted udder from the forbidden calf's stomach, and it is the hinge
> the whole second half turns on.

Three movements:

1. **The wringing dispute, closed** (109a) — Rav Aḥa of Difti's last challenge, Ravina's answer from the
   *shape* of Rabbi's ruling, and the sign-off *vetu la midi* — nothing more to say.
2. **The udder and the heart** (109a–109b) — the new mishna, Rav's two readings of "he does not
   transgress," and the baraita that decides between udder and stomach.
3. **Yalta's principle** (109b) — for everything forbidden, a permitted counterpart; the udder as the
   kosher answer to a craving for meat-in-milk.

## Walking through the sugya, step by step

### 109a — The wringing dispute, finally settled

**The objection to Rabbi Yehuda.** From Rabbi's accepting Rabbi Yehuda's view *only* when the pot was
not stirred or covered, one infers that Rabbi Yehuda himself forbids the pot **even when it was stirred
from first to last**. But why should he? The first piece **absorbed no more milk than the rest**
(*hā lo bala klal*) — the drop diffused evenly, and there is no measure in it to forbid them all.

**The answer.** Say Rabbi Yehuda's worry is that one might **not have stirred thoroughly** (*lo niár
yafeh yafeh*): initially the milk sat on the first piece alone, which absorbed it and turned forbidden,
and only afterward, when he stirred, did that piece expel the milk into the others. Rabbi Yehuda fears
this; Rabbi does not.

**The mirror inference, for the Rabbis.** "The Rabbis' view is right when he *stirred and covered*" —
what does that mean? Not "stirred at the end only," for Rabbi already granted Rabbi Yehuda's view in
that case. It must mean *stirred from beginning to end*. And from that you infer the **Rabbis** permit
even when he stirred only at the end — even the first piece is permitted — **evidently they hold *efshar
lisochato mutar***, a wrung-out piece is fine.

**Rav Aḥa of Difti's challenge.** *From where* do you know they dispute wringing at all?

> **דִּלְמָא אֶפְשָׁר לְסוֹחֲטוֹ דִּבְרֵי הַכֹּל אָסוּר, וְהָכָא בְּמִין בְּמִינוֹ קָא מִיפַּלְגִי.**
> Perhaps *everyone* agrees a wrung-out piece is forbidden, and here they argue about **like-in-like** —
> Rabbi Yehuda that a type is not nullified by its own type, the Rabbis that it is.

**Ravina's answer — from the shape of the ruling.** If that were the axis, Rabbi's *even-handed*
formulation makes no sense. He said "**Rabbi Yehuda's words appear right in this, and the Rabbis' in
that**" — a balance. But if they only argued about like-in-like, he should have said "Rabbi Yehuda's
words appear right **and do not appear right**" — a *one-sided* verdict. Since Rabbi split it evenly
between two cases, the live issue must be **when the piece is wrung out**. And with that —

> **וְתוּ לָא מִידֵּי.** And nothing more need be said.

**The upshot.** Rabbi comes down with Rabbi Yehuda on both fronts: **a wrung-out piece stays forbidden**
(*efshar lisochato asur*) and **like is not nullified by like** (*min bemino lo batil*). As Rashi spells
out, the halakha follows: the tainted piece itself *becomes neveila* and can forbid the other pieces
**even if they are a thousand** — unless there is sixty times its bulk in the **gravy alone**, which
nullifies it.

### 109a — A new mishna: the udder and the heart

> **הַכְּחָל — קוֹרְעוֹ וּמוֹצִיא אֶת חֶלְבּוֹ. הַלֵּב — קוֹרְעוֹ וּמוֹצִיא אֶת דָּמוֹ.**
> The **udder** — one tears it and removes its milk. The **heart** — one tears it and removes its blood.

And in each case, **if he did not tear it, "he does not transgress by it"** (*eino over alav*): no
lashes for meat-and-milk in the udder, no *karet* for blood in the heart. (The heart's exemption from
*karet* is read narrowly — the Gemara elsewhere fixes it to a *bird's* heart, whose blood is less than
a *kezayit*.) That deceptively mild phrase, *eino over alav*, is what the Gemara now takes apart.

### 109b — Is the udder merely exempt, or actually permitted?

**Rav, version one.** Rabbi Zeira in the name of Rav:

> **אֵינוֹ עוֹבֵר עָלָיו — וּמוּתָּר.**
> "He does not transgress by it" — **and it is permitted**, even *ab initio*.

**The objection.** But "he does not *transgress*" implies only that he is not *liable* — leaving room
for a **rabbinic** prohibition still in force. **The answer:** by right the mishna should have said
there is no prohibition at all; it used the guarded phrase only for **symmetry with the heart-clause**,
where there genuinely *is* a residual issue (**chatzi shiur** — half a measure of blood is forbidden by
Torah law though it carries no *karet*).

**Testing Rav from a baraita.** The baraita adds, for the heart, "if he did not tear it, **he tears it
after cooking and it is permitted**" — and says no such thing about the udder. **Infer:** only the heart
needs tearing after the fact; the udder needs nothing — it is simply permitted, supporting Rav.
**Rejected:** perhaps the opposite — tearing-after-cooking *helps the heart* (which is **smooth**,
*shia*, and barely absorbs), but *cannot help the udder*, whose soft flesh has already drunk the milk
in. The proof is inconclusive.

**Rav, version two (*ika de'amri*).** Others report Rav as saying **אֵינוֹ עוֹבֵר עָלָיו — וְאָסוּר**:
he does not transgress by Torah law, but it is **rabbinically forbidden**. The same mishna-reading and
the same baraita-test are run again from this side.

**The baraita that decides — udder versus stomach.** One baraita is taught **like Rav's first version**:

> **כְּחָל שֶׁבִּשְּׁלוֹ בַּחֲלָבוֹ — מוּתָּר; קֵבָה שֶׁבִּשְּׁלָהּ בַּחֲלָבָהּ — אָסוּר.**
> An **udder** roasted in its own milk is **permitted**; a **stomach** (*keiva*) roasted in its milk is
> **forbidden**.

**And what is the difference?**

> **זֶה כָּנוּס בְּמֵעָיו, וְזֶה אֵין כָּנוּס בְּמֵעָיו.**
> This one's milk **was collected in the innards**, and that one's **was not**.

The suckling calf's stomach holds milk that *became* milk the moment it left the mother's teat and was
merely gathered inside — real milk, and forbidden with the meat. The udder's milk never left the body,
never became milk at all; it is flesh, not milk, and so the udder is permitted (though one still tears
it, rabbinically, *ab initio*).

**How does one tear it?** Rav Yehuda: **lengthwise and crosswise** (*sheti va'erev*), then press it
against a wall to force the milk out. But Rabbi Elazar told his attendant simply "**tear it for me and
I'll eat**" — teaching that a single tear, one way or the other, is enough; *sheti va'erev* is not
required.

### 109b — Yalta's principle

Then the daf's most famous line. **Yalta**, wife of Rav Naḥman, lays down a rule of the whole Torah:

> **כֹּל דַּאֲסַר לַן רַחֲמָנָא — שְׁרָא לַן כְּוָותֵיהּ.**
> For **everything the Merciful One forbade us, He permitted us its like.**

Her list of paired prohibitions and their permitted twins:

| Forbidden | Its permitted counterpart |
|---|---|
| Blood | **Liver** — soaked in blood, tasting of it |
| A *niddah* | Relations during the **blood of purity** (*dam tohar*) after birth |
| Fat (*cheilev*) of a domestic animal | The **fat of a wild animal** — same taste |
| Pork | The **brain of the *shibuta*** fish — tastes like it |
| *Giruta* (a non-kosher species) | The **tongue of a fish** — tastes like it |
| A married woman | A **divorcee**, in her former husband's lifetime |
| A brother's wife | The ***yevama*** — his brother's childless widow |
| A gentile woman | The ***yefat toar*** — a beautiful captive of war |

"So," Yalta concludes, "**I want to eat something that tastes of meat-in-milk**" — where is *its*
permitted counterpart? Rav Naḥman answered by sending word to his cooks: **"Roast udders on a spit for
her"** (*zaviku lah kachlei*) — the udder, cooked in its own milk, is exactly that.

**But the mishna said "tear"!** The Gemara presses: Rav Naḥman told them only to roast, not to tear.
**Answer:** the tearing requirement is only **for a pot** (*lekdeira*), where expelled milk would
re-absorb into the meat — not for **roasting**, where it drips away. And when the baraita said "an udder
one *cooked*" (implying *after the fact* only), the Gemara answers it is permitted even *ab initio*; the
past-tense phrasing is only to lead into the stomach-clause — a thread the next daf picks up.

## The distinctions, side by side

Three organs, each asked the same three questions — what fills it, whether that filling counts as real
milk or blood, and what you must do about it:

| Organ | What fills it | Real milk / blood? | What it takes to eat it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Udder** (*kachal*) | milk soaked through the flesh | **No** — never *collected in the innards* | No Torah violation; tear once and press the milk out (rabbinic, before cooking in a pot) |
| **Stomach** (*keiva*) | milk the calf actually suckled | **Yes** — *collected in the innards* | Forbidden — this is true milk, and may not be cooked in it |
| **Heart** (*lev*) | blood | Blood — but the heart is *smooth* and barely absorbs | Tear and remove the blood; a tear even *after* cooking suffices |

## Key concepts & terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **כְּחָל** *(kachal)* | The **udder** of a slaughtered animal; full of milk, yet permitted because that milk never became milk — one tears it and presses the milk out before cooking. |
| **קֵבָה** *(keiva)* | The **stomach** (abomasum) of a suckling calf or lamb, holding milk it actually nursed; that milk is real milk, so the stomach cooked in it is forbidden. |
| **לֵב** *(lev)* | The **heart**; being *smooth*, it barely absorbs blood in cooking, so removing its blood by tearing works even after the fact. |
| **כָּנוּס בְּמֵעָיו** *(kanus b'me'av)* | "**Collected in the innards**" — the test that separates the forbidden stomach (its milk was gathered inside as milk) from the permitted udder (its milk never was). |
| **אֶפְשָׁר לְסוֹחֲטוֹ** *(efshar lisochato)* | "It can be wrung out" — whether an absorbed forbidden substance that could in principle be squeezed back out still forbids; settled here as **asur**, it does. |
| **מִין בְּמִינוֹ** *(min bemino)* | A forbidden food inside more of **its own type**; per Rabbi Yehuda, and now Rabbi, it is *never nullified*, so one tainted piece forbids all the rest. |
| **אֵינוֹ עוֹבֵר עָלָיו** *(eino over alav)* | "**He does not transgress by it**" — the mishna's guarded phrase, read by Rav either as *fully permitted* or as *exempt from the Torah but rabbinically forbidden*. |
| **חֲצִי שִׁיעוּר** *(chatzi shiur)* | A **half-measure** — here, less than a *kezayit* of blood in the heart; forbidden by Torah law though it carries no lashes or *karet*. |
| **קוֹרְעוֹ שְׁתִי וָעֵרֶב** *(kore'o sheti va'erev)* | Tearing an udder "**lengthwise and crosswise**" and pressing it to the wall — Rav Yehuda's thorough method, which Rabbi Elazar says is more than required. |
| **וְתוּ לָא מִידֵּי** *(vetu la midi)* | "**And nothing more**" — the Gemara's own sign-off that a line of argument is closed and the ruling settled. |
| **דַּם טוֹהַר** *(dam tohar)* | "**Blood of purity**" — the post-childbirth flow that does not render a woman impure; Yalta's example of a permitted counterpart to the forbidden *niddah*. |

## Who's who in today's daf

**Tannaim**

- **Rabbi Yehuda** (bar Ilai) — fourth-generation Tanna, leading student of Rabbi Akiva; the voice, here
  vindicated, of *efshar lisochato asur* and *min bemino lo batil*.
- **The Rabbis** (*Chachamim*) — the anonymous majority he disputes, holding a wrung-out piece permitted
  and like nullified by like.
- **Rabbi** (Yehuda HaNasi, *Rebbi*) — fifth/sixth-generation Tanna, redactor of the Mishna; his
  even-handed compromise is shown to rest on the *wringing* question, and he comes down with Rabbi Yehuda.

**Eretz-Yisrael Amoraim**

- **Rabbi Zeira** — third generation; a Babylonian who ascended to Eretz Yisrael, he transmits Rav's
  ruling on the udder in both its versions.
- **Rabbi Elazar** (ben Pedat) — third generation, a leading student of Rabbi Yoḥanan; teaches that a
  single tear suffices for the udder. (Not the Tanna *Rabbi Elazar ben Shamua*, nor Rabbi Eliezer.)

**Babylonian Amoraim**

- **Rav** (Abba Arikha) — first generation, founder of Sura; source of the two readings of "he does not
  transgress," and, through Rashi, of the ruling that follows Rabbi Yehuda.
- **Rav Yehuda** (bar Yeḥezkel) — second generation, founder of Pumbedita, student of Rav and Shmuel;
  prescribes tearing the udder *sheti va'erev*. (Note the clash of names: this **Rav** Yehuda the Amora
  is not **Rabbi** Yehuda bar Ilai the Tanna, who also appears on this very daf.)
- **Rav Naḥman** (bar Yaakov) — third generation, at Nehardea; Yalta's husband, who has the udders
  roasted for her.
- **Yalta** — Rav Naḥman's wife and daughter of the Exilarch's house (of Rabbah bar Avuha); a learned and
  forceful figure, author of the principle that every prohibition has a permitted twin.
- **Rav Aḥa of Difti** — a late Amora, frequent study-partner of Ravina; presses the challenge that the
  dispute might be about like-in-like rather than wringing. (*Of Difti* — from Be-Difti; not to be
  confused with Rav Aḥa bar Rav Ashi or the other many "Rav Aḥa"s.)
- **Ravina** — the answer from the *shape* of Rabbi's ruling that closes the sugya. (Whether the elder
  Ravina or his nephew, the Gemara does not fix; I would not press it.)
