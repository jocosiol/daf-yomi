## The big picture (read this first)

Today's page is a hinge. It **closes the chapter** *Kol HaBasar* with a mishna that sets **forbidden fat**
(*chelev*) beside **blood** (*dam*) and asks which is the graver — and then, on 117b, it **opens a new
chapter**, *HaOr VehaRotev*, "The Hide and the Gravy," about what counts as *food* when we measure impurity.

The two halves rhyme. Both are really asking the same question the whole tractate keeps circling: **when does
a thing count as the forbidden item itself, and when is it only attached to it?**

The mishna's contest is even-handed. Fat is stringent in four ways blood is not — misuse of consecrated
property (*me'ila*), and liability for *piggul*, *notar*, and eating while impure. Blood is stringent in one
sweeping way fat is not — it is forbidden in **every** creature, domesticated or wild, bird, kosher or not,
while forbidden fat lives only in a kosher domesticated animal. The Gemara then goes verse-hunting for each
claim, and in the middle of it surfaces a principle that reaches far past this daf:

> **אֵין לְךָ דָּבָר שֶׁנַּעֲשָׂה מִצְוָתוֹ וּמוֹעֲלִין בּוֹ.** "There is no item whose mitzva has been performed
> and is still subject to misuse." Once a consecrated thing has done its sacred job, benefiting from what is
> left is no longer *me'ila* — and that, R' Yochanan says, is exactly why blood carries no misuse.

Then the chapter turns over. The new mishna lists the bits of an animal that are not really food — the
**hide**, congealed **gravy**, **spices**, meat-scrapings, bones, tendons, horns, hooves — and rules that
they **join** a small scrap of meat to make up the egg-bulk that transmits **food-impurity**, but do **not**
join to make the olive-bulk that transmits the graver **carcass-impurity**. A protective cover, *shomer*,
counts as part of the food — but only where the impurity is light.

Four movements, in order:

1. **Fat versus blood** (117a). The closing mishna's two-way contest, and the verses for misuse, *piggul*,
   *notar*, and impurity.
2. **The fatty tail** (117a). Is the *alya* "fat"? Enough to carry misuse but not enough to be forbidden food
   — Rav Zevid and Rav Ashi work out how both can be true.
3. **Why blood is exempt from misuse** (117a). Ulla, the school of R' Yishmael, and R' Yochanan's
   *mitzva-already-done* principle — with the ash-removal and the broken-necked heifer as the telling
   exceptions.
4. **The new chapter** (117b). *HaOr VehaRotev* opens: what joins for light impurity but not severe, and the
   verse that makes a seed's husk count as food.

## Key concepts & terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **מְעִילָה** *(me'ila)* | **Misuse of consecrated property** — deriving benefit from something dedicated to the Temple. Forbidden fat of an offering carries it; blood does not. |
| **כַּאֲשֶׁר יוּרַם** *(ka'asher yuram)* | "**As it is taken off**" (Lev. 4:10). R' Yannai's source: it juxtaposes the peace-offering bull to the anointed priest's bull, teaching that the fat of lesser offerings is subject to misuse. |
| **אַלְיָה** *(alya)* | The **fatty tail** of a sheep. Called "fat" in one verse — enough to be caught by misuse, yet not enough to be forbidden as *chelev*, since it has no counterpart in an ox or goat. |
| **פִּיגּוּל / נוֹתָר** *(piggul / notar)* | An offering spoiled by wrong **intent of time** (*piggul*), or left **past its time** (*notar*). Eating fat can incur these; eating blood cannot. |
| **מַתִּיר** *(matir)* | A "**permitting factor**" — what releases an offering for eating or the altar. Blood is itself the *matir*, and a *matir* is never subject to *piggul*, so blood needs no special verse to exempt it. |
| **דָּבָר שֶׁנַּעֲשָׂה מִצְוָתוֹ** *(davar shenaasa mitzvato)* | An item **whose mitzva has been performed**. As a rule, once its sacred purpose is done there is no longer misuse — the ground of R' Yochanan's exemption for blood. |
| **תְּרוּמַת הַדֶּשֶׁן** *(terumat hadeshen)* | The daily **removal of ashes** from the altar. An exception: its mitzva is done, yet misuse still applies — one of the "two verses that come as one." |
| **עֶגְלָה עֲרוּפָה** *(egla arufa)* | The **heifer whose neck is broken** for an unsolved murder (Deut. 21). Also forbidden in benefit after its mitzva — paired with the ash-removal as the two exceptions. |
| **שְׁנֵי כְתוּבִים הַבָּאִים כְּאֶחָד** *(shnei ketuvim...)* | "**Two verses that come as one.**" A rule shared by exactly two cases teaches only itself, not a general principle — so the two exceptions above do not overturn the rule. |
| **שׁוֹמֵר** *(shomer)* | A **protective cover** — a husk, hide, or shell guarding food. It joins the food for **light** impurity but not for **severe** impurity. |
| **טוּמְאַת אֳכָלִין / טוּמְאַת נְבֵלוֹת** *(tumat ochlin / neveilos)* | **Food-impurity** (light; egg-bulk; passes only to food and drink) versus **carcass-impurity** (severe; olive-bulk; reaches people and vessels). The new chapter's whole axis. |
| **אָלָל** *(alal)* | The **meat-scrapings** left on a hide after flaying. Joins for food-impurity; R' Yehuda adds that if an olive-bulk is gathered in one place, it becomes carcass-flesh again. |

## Who's who in today's daf

**Tannaim**

- **Rabbi** (Yehuda HaNasi, "Rebbi") — last-generation Tanna and redactor of the Mishnah; taught the reading
  of "all the fat is the Lord's" to fold the portions of lesser offerings into misuse. R' Chanina calls the
  home-grown derivation the finer one.
- **Rabbi Dosa** (ben Harkinas) — an early Tanna; holds that the High Priest's Yom Kippur linen garments need
  not be interred, only not reused on a later Yom Kippur. His view removes one of the two paired exceptions,
  forcing the Gemara to re-pair the ash-removal with the broken-necked heifer.
- **The school of Rabbi Yishmael** (*bei Rabbi Yishmael*) — a Tannaitic study-house; supplies both the
  "to make atonement" reading for blood and the "any sowing seed" reading that makes a husk count as food.
- **Rabbi Yehuda** (bar Ilai) — fourth-generation Tanna, student of R' Akiva; the closing voice of the new
  mishna: gathered meat-scrapings of an olive-bulk in one place become carcass-flesh again.

**Eretz-Yisrael Amoraim**

- **Rabbi Yannai** — first-generation Amora, colleague of R' Chiyya and a teacher of R' Yochanan; derives the
  misuse of offering-fat from "as it is taken off."
- **Rabbi Chanina** (bar Chama) — first-generation Amora of Tzippori; objects that Rabbi's own derivation was
  already elegant, so why reach for a new one. (**R' Chanina** bar Chama — not **R' Chanina bar Papa** or the
  many later namesakes.)
- **Ulla** (bar Yishmael) — second-to-third-generation Amora who carried Eretz-Yisrael teachings down to
  Babylonia; derives blood's exemption from misuse from the word "to you."
- **Rabbi Yochanan** (bar Nafcha) — second-generation Amora and head of the Tiberias academy; grounds blood's
  exemption in "it is" — before atonement is like after, and after atonement there is no misuse.

**Babylonian Amoraim**

- **Abaye** — fourth-generation Amora of Pumbedita; shows both verses are needed, since neither the fat alone
  nor "as it is taken off" alone would cover every portion.
- **Rav Mari** — asks Rav Zevid the sharp question: if the fatty tail is called "fat," let it be forbidden to
  eat. Several Amoraim bear this name; which one this is, I would not claim with confidence.
- **Rav Zevid** — a Pumbedita Amora of the fifth-to-sixth generation; answers that forbidden fat is only what
  is shared by ox, sheep, and goat — and the fatty tail is not.
- **Rav Ashi** — sixth-generation Amora of Mata Meḥasya and a principal shaper of the Talmud; offers the
  alternate reading that the tail is called "the fat tail," not simply "fat," before the Gemara settles on Rav
  Zevid.

## Walking through the sugya, step by step

### 117a — Forbidden fat against blood

**The closing mishna of *Kol HaBasar*.** It sets the two substances side by side. Forbidden **fat** of an
offering is stringent in four ways: one is liable for **misuse** of consecrated property, for **piggul**, for
**notar**, and for eating it while **impure** — *"מַה שֶּׁאֵין כֵּן בַּדָּם"*, "which is not so with blood,"
where only the prohibition of blood itself applies. But **blood** is stringent in one sweeping way: it is
forbidden **in a domesticated animal, an undomesticated animal, and a bird, both kosher and non-kosher**,
while forbidden fat lives **only in a kosher domesticated animal**.

**Where does the fat's misuse come from?** *"מְנָא הָנֵי מִילֵּי?"* **Rabbi Yannai:** from **״כַּאֲשֶׁר
יוּרַם מִשּׁוֹר זֶבַח הַשְּׁלָמִים״**, "as it is taken off from the bull of the peace offering" (Lev. 4:10).
The verse comes to teach about the anointed priest's bull, but ends up *learning* from the peace-offering
bull — juxtaposing the two, so that just as the priest's bull (a most-holy offering) is subject to misuse,
so are the portions of the peace-offering, an offering of lesser sanctity that is the owner's own property.

**R' Chanina's objection.** *"כְּעוּרָה זוֹ שֶׁשָּׁנָה רַבִּי?"* — "Is the derivation **Rabbi** taught so
unattractive" that you need a new one? Rabbi taught that **״כׇּל חֵלֶב לַה׳״**, "all the fat is the Lord's"
(Lev. 3:16), said of *lesser* offerings, folds their portions into misuse.

**Abaye: both are needed.** Had the Torah written only "all the fat," we would say the **fat** is included but
not the **diaphragm and two kidneys** — so it wrote "as it is taken off." Had it written only "as it is taken
off," we would say only what is *found in a bull* is included, excluding the **fatty tail** of a sheep — so it
wrote "all the fat." Each verse closes a gap the other leaves.

### 117a — Is the fatty tail "fat"?

**Rav Mari presses Rav Zevid.** If the fatty **tail** (*alya*) is called "fat" — enough to be caught by
misuse — then **let it be forbidden to eat** like any *chelev*! **Rav Zevid's answer:** **״כׇּל חֵלֶב שׁוֹר
וְכֶשֶׂב וָעֵז״**, "no fat of ox, or sheep, or goat" (Lev. 7:23) — the Torah forbids as *chelev* only what is
found **equally in all three**, and the fatty tail belongs to the sheep alone. So it is "fat" for misuse but
not forbidden to eat.

**Rav Ashi's alternate, and why it fails.** Rav Ashi: the verse calls it **"the fat tail"** (*chelbo
ha'alya*), never simply "fat" — so it is not truly *chelev* at all. **The objection:** then it should not
carry **misuse** either, yet everyone agrees it does. **Rather** — *"מְחַוַּורְתָּא כִּדְרַב זְבִיד"* — the
matter is clear as Rav Zevid explained: it is fat enough for misuse, but not the fat the Torah forbids to eat.

### 117a — Why blood carries no misuse

**Where does blood's exemption come from?** **Ulla:** the verse says the blood is given **״לָכֶם״**, "to
you" — *it shall be yours*, not consecrated, so no misuse. **The school of R' Yishmael:** from **״לְכַפֵּר״**,
"to make atonement" — *"לְכַפָּרָה נְתַתִּיו וְלֹא לִמְעִילָה"*, "I gave it for atonement, not for misuse."

**Rabbi Yochanan's principle.** From **״הוּא״**, "it is" — the blood's status is **before atonement as after
atonement**; and just as *after* the blood is sprinkled there is no misuse (its mitzva is done), so *before*
sprinkling there is no misuse.

**But turn it around** — say after atonement is like before, so misuse *applies* even after! **The answer:**
**אֵין לְךָ דָּבָר שֶׁנַּעֲשָׂה מִצְוָתוֹ וּמוֹעֲלִין בּוֹ** — "there is no item whose mitzva has been performed
and is still subject to misuse." The rule runs one way only.

**The two stubborn exceptions.** But **terumat hadeshen** — the ash-removal — *has* had its mitzva performed,
and still carries misuse, since **״וְשָׂמוֹ אֵצֶל הַמִּזְבֵּחַ״**, "he shall put them beside the altar," fixes
the ashes in place! **The answer:** the ash-removal and the High Priest's Yom Kippur **vestments** (also
barred from reuse) are **שְׁנֵי כְתוּבִים הַבָּאִים כְּאֶחָד**, two verses that come as one — and two such
verses **do not teach** a general rule. So the principle stands.

**R' Dosa forces a re-pairing.** This works for the Rabbis, who read "and leave them there" to require the
vestments' **interment**. But **R' Dosa** says the verse only bars the High Priest from *reusing* them on a
later Yom Kippur — they stay fit for an ordinary priest. Then the vestments are no exception, and the
ash-removal stands **alone**, teaching a general rule again! **Rather** — re-pair it: the ash-removal and the
**broken-necked heifer** (*egla arufa*), also forbidden after its mitzva, are the two verses that come as one.
And should you hold that even two such verses *do* teach — **תְּרֵי מִיעוּטֵי כְּתִיבִי**, each has its own
**restricting word** ("and he shall put *it*"; "*whose neck was broken*") confining the rule to itself.

**Why three verses for blood, then?** *"לָכֶם / לְכַפֵּר / הוּא"* — **one** to exempt blood from **notar**,
**one** from **misuse**, **one** from **impurity**. **But not from piggul** — that needs no verse: a mishna
teaches that only something **with a permitting factor** is subject to *piggul*, and **blood is itself the
permitting factor** (*matir*), so it is exempt on its own. **הֲדַרַן עֲלָךְ כׇּל הַבָּשָׂר** — and here the
chapter ends.

### 117b — A new chapter: *HaOr VehaRotev*, "The Hide and the Gravy"

**The opening mishna.** For food to transmit **food-impurity** it must be an **egg-bulk**. The mishna rules
that even non-food parts count toward it: the **hide** (*or*), the congealed **gravy** (*rotev*), the
**spices** (*kipa*), the **meat-scrapings** (*alal*), the **bones**, the **tendons**, and the lower
**horns** and upper **hooves** all **join** a scrap of meat to reach the egg-bulk **״מִצְטָרְפִין לְטַמֵּא
טוּמְאַת אֳכָלִין, אֲבָל לֹא טוּמְאַת נְבֵלוֹת״** — but they do **not** join to reach the olive-bulk of
**carcass-impurity**. So too one who slaughters a **non-kosher animal for a gentile** while it is still
**twitching** (*mefarkeset*): it conveys food-impurity but not carcass-impurity **until it dies or its head
is severed**. The Torah **included more** for food-impurity than for carcass-impurity. **R' Yehuda** adds:
gathered scrapings (*alal hamechunas*) of an **olive-bulk in one place** are carcass-flesh again, and one is
liable.

**The Gemara reads the mishna into a baraita.** *"תְּנֵינָא לְהָא דְּתָנוּ רַבָּנַן"* — the mishna teaches
what the Sages taught outright: **שׁוֹמְרִים לְטוּמְאָה קַלָּה, וְלֹא שׁוֹמְרִים לְטוּמְאָה חֲמוּרָה** — a
protective cover (*shomer*) **joins for light impurity but not for severe impurity**.

**Light impurity — from where?** The school of R' Yishmael: **״עַל כׇּל זֶרַע זֵרוּעַ״**, "upon any sowing
seed" (Lev. 11:37) — *as people take seed out to sow it*: **wheat in its husk, barley in its husk, lentils in
their husks**. The protective husk is reckoned part of the food, so a cover joins for food-impurity.

**Severe impurity — from where the exclusion?** The Sages: **״בְּנִבְלָתָהּ״**, "its carcass" (Lev.
11:39) — *and not a hide with less than an olive-bulk of flesh on it*. For carcass-impurity the cover does
**not** join.

## The distinctions, side by side

The mishna that closes the chapter is itself a "side by side" — forbidden fat against blood, each graver on
its own ground. This grid is only that mishna, made explicit.

| Test | Forbidden fat (*chelev*) | Blood (*dam*) |
|---|---|---|
| Misuse of consecrated property (*me'ila*) | **Yes** — liable | No |
| *Piggul*, *notar*, eating while impure | **Yes** — liable for each | No — only the blood-prohibition |
| Which creatures it applies to | Kosher **domesticated** animal only | **Every** creature: beast, wild animal, bird — kosher **and** non-kosher |

> **אֵין לְךָ דָּבָר שֶׁנַּעֲשָׂה מִצְוָתוֹ וּמוֹעֲלִין בּוֹ** — there is no item whose mitzva has been performed
> and is still subject to misuse. Blood does its work on the altar and is spent; what has already served the
> sacred can no longer be stolen from it.
