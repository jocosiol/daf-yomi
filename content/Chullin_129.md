## The big picture (read this first)

Today closes chapter nine, and it does so around one beautifully economical phrase and one long, patient
argument.

The phrase is **כְּשֶׁשִּׁימֵּשׁ מַעֲשֵׂה עֵץ שִׁימֵּשׁ** — *"when it served, it performed the role of wood."* It
answers a puzzle that keeps recurring: there is a rule that food destined to impart a **severe** impurity does
not need to be rendered susceptible by liquid at all. So how can we have any food that both imparts severe
impurity *and* needs rendering? The Gemara's answer is disarmingly simple — at the very moment the item was
doing its severe-impurity work, **it was not functioning as food**. It was a seat, or an idol, or the wall of
a house, or the covering of a kidney. It "played the part of wood." Once you see it, Abaye, Rav Pappa, Rav
Mattana all line the same key into one lock after another.

> A thing can defile as one kind of object and be rendered susceptible as another — because at each moment it
> is only ever doing one job. Ask *what was it being* when the impurity struck.

The long argument is the chase after **Rabbi Shimon**. The mishna's three clauses each end "and Rabbi Shimon
deems it pure," and the Amoraim cannot agree which clause his purity-ruling attaches to, nor *why*. Is his
reason R' Yoḥanan's reading of the verse — *forbidden food is not "food"* — or is it *"since it is attached, it
is attached"*? R' Zeira keeps prying the two apart, moving R' Shimon's dissent from clause to clause until it
finally lands on the last one: the flesh of an animal that died.

Then a **new mishna** transposes the entire machine from animal to human — a limb or flesh dangling from a
*person* — and the chapter closes on the sharp, concrete question the whole tractate has been circling: when a
bone the size of a barley-grain, or an olive-bulk of flesh, drops off a limb of the living, does *it* defile?
Three tannaim, three answers — and then **הֲדַרַן**, we finish *HaOr VeHaRotev*.

## Key concepts & terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **מַעֲשֵׂה עֵץ שִׁימֵּשׁ** *(ma'aseh etz shimesh)* | **"It performed the role of wood."** When an item did its severe-impurity job it was serving as something other than food, so the rule that severe-impurity food needs no rendering does not apply — it can need rendering *and* defile severely. |
| **טוּמְאָה חֲמוּרָה** *(tum'a ḥamura)* | **Severe impurity** — the kind carried to people and vessels (a carcass, a corpse, midras, a leprous house), as against the "light" impurity of food. |
| **הֶכְשֵׁר** *(hechsher)* | Being **rendered susceptible** to impurity through contact with one of the seven liquids. Food normally needs it; food headed for severe impurity does not. |
| **טוּמְאַת בֵּית הַסְּתָרִים** *(tum'at beit ha-setarim)* | Impurity contracted in a **concealed, unexposed place**. As a rule it does not defile — R' Meir is the one who holds it does. |
| **אֵבֶר מִן הַחַי** *(ever min ha-ḥai)* | A **limb from the living** — severed from a living body, it defiles like a carcass, a severe impurity. |
| **אֵבֶר מִן הַמֵּת** *(ever min ha-met)* | A **limb from a corpse.** The mishna's dispute: after death, does a dangling limb defile as *this*, or still only as a limb from the living? |
| **מָה נַפְשָׁךְ** *(ma nafshach)* | **"Whichever way you take it"** — the two-horned challenge the Gemara throws at R' Shimon: either death makes the limb "fallen" or it does not, and either way it should defile. |
| **הוֹאִיל וּמְעוֹרֶה מְעוֹרֶה** *(ho'il u-me'oreh me'oreh)* | **"Since it is attached, it is attached."** A rival reason for R' Shimon: a dangling part is pure not because it is forbidden food, but because it still counts as joined to the body. |
| **מִכׇּל הָאֹכֶל אֲשֶׁר יֵאָכֵל** *(mikol ha-ochel asher ye'achel)* | **"From all food which may be eaten"** (Lev. 11:34). R' Yoḥanan's proof for R' Shimon — only food you *could* feed to others, even a gentile, is "food"; what is forbidden to all is not. |
| **כְּזַיִת בָּשָׂר** *(kezayit basar)* | An **olive-bulk of flesh** that separates from a limb of the living — the measure at the center of the closing dispute. |
| **עֶצֶם כִּשְׂעוֹרָה** *(etzem ki-se'ora)* | A **bone the size of a barley-grain** off a limb of the living — the other half of that dispute. |

## Who's who in today's daf

**Tannaim**

- **Rabbi Meir** and **Rabbi Shimon** (bar Yoḥai) — third-generation Tannaim, students of R' Akiva; theirs is
  the mishna dispute the whole daf is trying to pin down.
- **Rabbi Yehuda** (bar Ilai) — third-generation Tanna, also a student of R' Akiva; here the author of the
  fig-branch ruling *(a branch hanging by its bark is not susceptible)*. Note this is **Rabbi Yehuda the
  Tanna**, not the Amora Rav Yehuda.
- **Rabbi Eliezer** (ben Hyrcanus) and **Rabbi Yehoshua** (ben Ḥananya) — second-generation Tannaim,
  students of Rabban Yoḥanan ben Zakkai; their baraita ("I heard that a limb from the living defiles" — "from
  the living but not the dead?") mirrors the Meir–Shimon split.
- **Rabbi Neḥunya ben HaKana** — early Tanna; in the *Eduyyot* mishna he flips Eliezer and Yehoshua's rulings
  on flesh versus bone.

**Amoraim**

- **Rabbi Yoḥanan** — second-generation Eretz-Yisrael Amora, head of Tiberias; supplies R' Shimon's reason
  from the verse *"from all food which may be eaten."*
- **Rabbi Asi** (Assi) — third-generation Eretz-Yisrael Amora, colleague of R' Ami and student of R' Yoḥanan;
  transmits R' Yoḥanan's reason and defends it against R' Zeira. (This is **Rabbi Asi**, the Eretz-Yisrael
  tradent of R' Yoḥanan — distinct from **Rav Ashi** the later redactor.)
- **Rabbi Zeira** — third-generation Amora who went up to Tiberias; the persistent questioner here, prying
  R' Shimon's dissent loose from one clause after another.
- **Rabbi Abba bar Memel** — third-generation Eretz-Yisrael Amora; his suggestion (the baraita follows R'
  Meir on concealed-place impurity) opens the daf and is then set aside.
- **Rava** (bar Yosef bar Ḥama) — fourth-generation head of Maḥoza; brushes aside the concealed-place
  difficulty and roots the whole discussion in *"it performed the role of wood."*
- **Rabba bar Rav Ḥanan** — fourth-generation Babylonian Amora, a frequent interlocutor of Abaye and Rava;
  presses Rava on why rendering is needed at all.
- **Abaye** — fourth-generation head of Pumbedita; brings the leaven-seat, the idolatrous offering and the
  food-joined-to-a-vessel, each solved by *"it performed the role of wood."*
- **Rav Pappa** — fifth-generation Babylonian Amora; adds the forbidden fat of a village carcass.
- **Rav Mattana** — Babylonian Amora, a student of Rav; adds the house roofed with seeds.

## Walking through the sugya, step by step

### 129a — The concealed-place difficulty, and "it performed the role of wood"

The daf opens mid-argument. A baraita has flesh, once cut from a dangling limb, becoming impure — but *why*
should it? The limb touched the flesh at a **hidden seam** (*beit ha-setarim*), and impurity in a concealed
place does not normally defile. R' Zeira reports to R' Asi that R' Abba bar Memel pinned the baraita on
**R' Meir**, who holds concealed-place impurity *does* defile. R' Asi objects: R' Meir only says that for an
impurity that **does not need rendering** — where rendering is needed, even R' Meir concedes.

**Rava is unimpressed.** *"וּמַאי קוּשְׁיָא?"* — where is the difficulty? Perhaps the flesh **was rendered
susceptible before it was cut off.** Rabba bar Rav Ḥanan pushes back: why does it need rendering at all? The
flesh sits on a limb-from-the-living, which imparts *severe* carcass impurity — and food headed for severe
impurity **needs no rendering**. Rava's answer is the key to the whole daf:

> *"כְּשֶׁשִּׁימֵּשׁ, מַעֲשֵׂה עֵץ שִׁימֵּשׁ"* — while it was part of the limb it **served as wood**, not as food; so
> the "no rendering needed" rule never applied to it, and once cut it is ordinary food that must be rendered.

### 129a — The same key in five more locks

Rava's principle turns out to unlock a whole drawer of cases where a food seems to defile *severely* by Torah
law — which should be impossible. Each Amora raises one, worries "this must be only rabbinic, for otherwise we
have found food that catches severe impurity!", and each is answered with **מַעֲשֵׂה עֵץ שִׁימֵּשׁ**:

- **Abaye — the leaven-seat.** A **כּוֹפֶת שְׂאוֹר** (a lump of leaven) set aside to sit on is no longer food; it
  becomes a seat and catches *midras* from a *zav*.
- **Abaye — the idolatrous offering.** A food **תִּקְרוֹבֶת עֲבוֹדָה זָרָה** defiles in a tent, like a corpse.
- **Abaye — food joined to a vessel.** *"חִבּוּרֵי אֳכָלִין כְּכֵלִים דָּמוּ"* — dough stuck to a kneading-trough
  is treated as the vessel and catches vessel-impurity.
- **Rav Pappa — the villager's fat.** Forbidden **חֵלֶב נְבֵלָה** in the villages needs designation and
  rendering, yet defiles as a carcass through the kidney it covers.
- **Rav Mattana — the seeded roof.** A house *"שֶׁסִּכְּכוֹ בִּזְרָעִים"*, roofed with seeds, turns the seeds pure
  (they are now the house), and if leprosy strikes, the whole house — seeds included — is impure.

In every case the resolution is identical: when the item did its severe-impurity job it was a seat, an
offering, a wall, a covering — *"it performed the role of wood."* By Torah law, and no contradiction.

### 129a–b — Hunting for R' Shimon: which clause, and why?

Now to the mishna's refrain, "and Rabbi Shimon deems it pure." The Gemara throws the two-horned **מָה נַפְשָׁךְ**:
if death makes the dangling limb "fallen," let it defile as a limb from the living; if death does not, let it
defile as a limb from a carcass — so how can R' Shimon call it pure? Answer: **R' Shimon is not on the last
clause at all** — he is on the *first*, where the dangling limb and flesh defile as food and need rendering,
and he says they are not susceptible **at all.**

**R' Yoḥanan's reason** (via R' Asi): the verse *"מִכׇּל הָאֹכֶל אֲשֶׁר יֵאָכֵל"* — food you *can* feed to others,
even a gentile, is "food"; the limb and flesh of a living animal, forbidden to everyone, are **not food**, so
never susceptible.

**R' Zeira pries it loose (129b).** Maybe R' Shimon's real reason in the first clause is *"הוֹאִיל וּמְעוֹרֶה —
מְעוֹרֶה"*, since it is attached it counts as attached — exactly R' Yehuda's reason (per R' Asi's own teaching)
for the **fig branch** hanging by its bark being pure. So R' Asi re-aims R' Yoḥanan at the **middle** clause:
after slaughter, R' Meir says the blood renders the flesh susceptible, R' Shimon says it does not. R' Zeira
pries *that* loose too — R' Shimon's reason there could be Rava's (a body is a handle for impurity but not for
rendering) or R' Yoḥanan's earlier "grasp the small" reading. So finally: **R' Shimon is on the last clause**
— the animal that *died* — and not on the limb but on the **flesh**, which R' Meir says needs rendering and
R' Shimon says is not susceptible even with liquid. *There* the verse's reason fits cleanly.

### 129b — A new mishna: the dangling limb of a *person*

*"הָאֵבֶר וְהַבָּשָׂר הַמְדוּלְדָּלִין בְּאָדָם — טְהוֹרִים."* A limb or flesh half-severed and hanging from a living
person is pure. If the **person dies**, the hanging flesh is pure (it counts as flesh from a living person),
and the hanging limb defiles **as a limb from the living, not as a limb from a corpse** — R' Meir. And
**R' Shimon deems it pure.**

The Gemara asks the same **מָה נַפְשָׁךְ** — and answers differently this time: **R' Shimon is speaking in
general.** From R' Meir's "defiles as a limb from the living but not as a limb from a corpse," one hears that a
corpse's limb *does* generally defile; R' Shimon replies that **a limb of a corpse does not generally defile**
(when it lacks an olive of flesh).

This is exactly the old **Eliezer–Yehoshua** baraita: R' Eliezer — "I *heard* that a limb from the living
defiles"; R' Yehoshua — "from the living but not the dead? By *kal vaḥomer* the corpse's limb should be worse!"
(with his charming proof from *Megillat Ta'anit*: if one may not eulogize on Minor Passover, all the more so
not on the real Passover). R' Eliezer holds his ground: *"כָּךְ שָׁמַעְתִּי"* — this is the tradition I received.

**Where is the practical difference** between "limb from the living" and "limb from a corpse"? In a
**כְּזַיִת בָּשָׂר** (olive of flesh) or an **עֶצֶם כִּשְׂעוֹרָה** (barley-grain of bone) that drops off the limb. The
*Eduyyot* mishna: for the olive of flesh, R' Eliezer defiles, R' Neḥunya and R' Yehoshua purify; for the
barley-grain of bone, R' Neḥunya defiles, R' Eliezer and R' Yehoshua purify. And so, the Gemara closes,
R' Meir and R' Shimon split the same way — R' Meir like one of Eliezer/Neḥunya, **R' Shimon like R' Yehoshua**,
who purifies both. **הֲדַרַן עֲלָךְ הָעוֹר וְהָרוֹטֶב** — the chapter is complete.

## The distinctions, side by side

The recurring key of the daf: six items that each impart a **severe** impurity by Torah law, yet still need
rendering — because in each, when the item did its severe job it was serving as something other than food
(*"it performed the role of wood"*). Every cell traces to the walkthrough above.

| Item | What it was serving as | The severe impurity it imparts | Raised by |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Flesh on a dangling limb** | Part of the limb | Carcass impurity, via limb-from-the-living | Rava |
| **Leaven set aside to sit on** *(kofet se'or)* | A seat | *Midras* — a *zav*'s treading | Abaye |
| **Idolatrous food-offering** | An object of worship | Tent impurity, like a corpse | Abaye |
| **Dough joined to a vessel** | Part of the vessel | Vessel impurity | Abaye |
| **Forbidden fat of a carcass** | Covering for the kidney | Carcass impurity, via the kidney | Rav Pappa |
| **Seeds roofing a house** | The roof | Leprosy of the house | Rav Mattana |

---

## One line to carry with you

> A single object never wears two hats at once: when it defiled it was a seat, a wall, a covering — *not*
> food. So ask not "what is this thing" but "what was it *being*" — and a food that seems to defy the rules
> quietly obeys them.
