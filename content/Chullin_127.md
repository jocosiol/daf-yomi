## The big picture (read this first)

Today's page has two very different halves, joined by a single instinct — **reading one small word for
more than it seems to say.**

The first half is a tour of creeping things and the verses that catch them. The Torah's eight *sheratzim*
defile, but which mice? A mouse that swims out to sea? A mouse that (people believed) grows straight out
of the ground, half its body still earth? Everything turns on the phrase *"הַשּׁוֹרֵץ עַל הָאָרֶץ"* — "that
creeps upon the earth" — and the daf pulls it two ways at once:

> Read **"that creeps"** as *anywhere it creeps* and you have swept the **sea-mouse** in. Read it as
> *that breeds* (*mashritz*) and you have swept the **earth-mouse** out, since a creature born of mud does
> not breed. The Gemara wants both catches — and needs an extra "upon the earth" to pay for them.

Out of this grammar grows a stretch of wonder and folklore: the salamander born of fire, R' Akiva's cry of
*"how great are Your works,"* the claim that every land animal has a sea twin except the weasel, and the
crossbred snake-and-lizard that produced the deadly *arvad*.

The second half is pure *tohorot*, and it plants a seed that will grow for three whole dapim. The mishna
takes a **limb or a piece of flesh half-torn from a living animal** and left dangling, and asks the
question the rest of the chapter lives on: *is a thing that is neither fully attached nor fully detached
treated as attached, or as detached — and is the answer the same for every law?* A dangling limb turns out
to be **detached** for food-impurity yet **attached** for carcass-impurity; dried figs are **detached** for
impurity yet **attached** for Shabbat. One object, two verdicts, depending on which question you ask of it.

## Key concepts & terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **הַשּׁוֹרֵץ** *(hashoretz)* | **"That creeps."** Read one way it means *creeping anywhere*, even in the sea (so a sea-mouse would defile); read another it means *that breeds* (*mashritz*), which would exclude a mouse born of earth. The daf plays the two readings against each other. |
| **עַכְבָּר שֶׁחֶצְיוֹ בָּשָׂר וְחֶצְיוֹ אֲדָמָה** *(achbar she-ḥetzyo basar)* | The **half-flesh, half-earth mouse** — believed to generate spontaneously from mud. Because it "does not breed," a reading of *hashoretz* as *breeds* would leave it pure; the word *ba-sheretz* is brought to include it anyway. |
| **עַל הָאָרֶץ** *(al ha-aretz)* | **"Upon the earth."** Made to do double duty — one instance excludes a **sea-mouse**, the other excludes a case of *floating uncertain impurity*. The daf notes the phrase is written **twice**. |
| **סְפֵק טוּמְאָה צָפָה** *(sfek tum'a tzafa)* | **Uncertain floating impurity.** If you may or may not have touched a source floating on water, you stay pure — even in a private domain, where uncertain impurity is usually deemed impure (Rav Yitzḥak bar Avdimi). |
| **סָלָמַנְדְּרָא** *(salamandra)* | The **salamander**, thought to be generated from fire; smearing its blood was believed to make one fireproof. R' Akiva's example of a creature that lives where others instantly die. |
| **הָאֵבֶר וְהַבָּשָׂר הַמְדוּלְדָּלִין** *(ha-ever ve-ha-basar ha-meduldalin)* | A **limb or flesh partially severed and left hanging** from the animal. Not yet a "limb from the living" nor severed flesh — the mishna's central case. |
| **טוּמְאַת אֳכָלִין** *(tum'at ochalin)* | **Food-impurity** — the lesser impurity a food item contracts and transmits to other food and liquid, once one intends to eat it. |
| **הֶכְשֵׁר** *(hechsher)* | **Being rendered susceptible** to impurity through contact with one of the seven liquids (water, blood, wine, etc.). Without it, food cannot become impure. |
| **כִּי יִפּוֹל / עַד שֶׁיִּפּוֹל** *(ki yipol / ad she-yipol)* | **"If it falls / until it falls."** The verse that makes a hanging limb *not* a carcass until it drops off completely — even if joined by a single hair. |
| **יָד** *(yad)* | A **handle** — an inedible or unsusceptible appendage that, when the food it carries is touched, transmits or receives impurity for it. The question here: does the *whole animal* become a handle for its dangling limb? |
| **מוּחְלֶפֶת הַשִּׁיטָה** *(muchlefet ha-shita)* | **"The attribution is reversed."** R' Yoḥanan's move to resolve a contradiction in R' Meir by swapping the names on an outside mishna (*Tevul Yom*). |

## Who's who in today's daf

**Tannaim**

- **Rabbi Akiva** — second–third-generation Tanna of Bnei Brak, the towering master of his generation; here
  he reaches the verse of the creeping things and bursts out, "How great are Your works, O Lord."
- **Rav Huna bar Torta** — a Tanna (the title *Rav* here is not the Amora Rav Huna); he tells the story of
  the snake crossbred with the great lizard that produced an *arvad*. A minor figure; I would not build a
  biography on him.
- **Rabbi Shimon the Righteous** *(R' Shimon he-Ḥasid)* — the sage who explains the *arvad* as divine
  measure-for-measure. The name is used of more than one figure and I would not identify him confidently;
  he is not the Second-Temple high priest Shimon HaTzaddik.
- **Rabbi Meir** and **Rabbi Shimon** (bar Yoḥai) — third-generation Tannaim, both students of R' Akiva;
  they dispute the mishna at the foot of the page over whether the animal's own blood renders a dangling
  limb susceptible to impurity.

**Amoraim**

- **Rav** (Abba Arikha) — first-generation Babylonian Amora, founder of the academy at Sura; here he calls
  the birth of the *arvad* "a miracle within a miracle," a calamity aimed at the wicked.
- **Shmuel** — first-generation Amora of Nehardea, Rav's great colleague; his ruling (via Rav Ḥiyya bar
  Ashi) that figs dried on the tree are detached for impurity yet attached for Shabbat anchors the second
  half.
- **Rav Ḥiyya bar Ashi** — Babylonian Amora who transmits Shmuel's ruling. (Not to be confused with **Rav
  Ashi**, the much later redactor.)
- **Rav Yitzḥak bar Avdimi** — early Babylonian Amora; source of the rule that "upon the earth" excludes
  floating uncertain impurity.
- **Rabbi Zeira** — third-generation Amora who went up from Bavel to Tiberias; supplies the verse *yoshvei
  ḥeled* for the weasel's uniqueness.
- **Rav Huna, son of Rav Yehoshua** — fourth–fifth-generation Babylonian Amora; the beavers of Neresh "are
  not of the settled land."
- **Rav Pappa** — fifth-generation Babylonian Amora, student of Abaye and Rava, whose own academy was at
  Neresh; his sharp sayings about Neresh, Nehar Pekod and Pumbedita punctuate the aggada.
- **Rav Giddel** — Babylonian Amora, here transmitting Rav's quip about the thief of Neresh.
- **Rabba** (bar Naḥmani) — third-generation head of Pumbedita; opens the amoraic reading of the mishna's
  dispute (the animal as a "handle"). (**Rabbah**, רבה — not **Rava**, רבא, who appears above in the
  sea-mouse sugya; both are on this page.)
- **Abaye** — fourth-generation head of Pumbedita; reframes the dispute as "grasp the small part and the
  large does not rise with it."
- **Rabbi Yoḥanan** — second-generation Eretz-Yisrael Amora of Tiberias; endorses Abaye's reading and
  raises the contradiction in R' Meir from *Tevul Yom*.
- **Rava** (bar Yosef bar Ḥama) — fourth-generation head of Maḥoza; fields the challenge of "one of the
  Sages" over how to read the sea-mouse verses.

## Walking through the sugya, step by step

### 127a — Two ways to read "that creeps"

**The starting reading.** *"תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר הַשּׁוֹרֵץ – כׇּל מָקוֹם שֶׁשּׁוֹרֵץ"* — "that creeps" teaches that
creeping animals defile *anywhere they creep*, the sea included. On this reading, "upon the earth" is the
limiting word that keeps a genuine **sea-mouse** out.

**The rival reading.** *"אוֹ אֵינוֹ… יָכוֹל כׇּל הַמַּשְׁרִיץ יְטַמֵּא"* — or perhaps *hashoretz* means *that
breeds* (*mashritz*): only a breeding creature defiles, which would **exclude the half-earth mouse**, since
a creature born of mud does not breed. Logic pushes back and forth — a mouse is a mouse "whatever its
name," so include it; a mouse is like a weasel, which breeds, so exclude it — until the verse *"בַּשֶּׁרֶץ"*
is brought to **include** the half-earth mouse after all.

**Rava and the sea.** *"אֲמַר לֵיהּ הָהוּא מִדְּרַבָּנַן לְרָבָא"* — one of the Sages presses Rava to flip the
derivations, letting *bashoretz* include the earth-mouse and *hashoretz* include the sea-mouse. Rava cuts it
off: *"וּמֵאַחַר דְּשַׁוִּיתֵיהּ לְיָם מְקוֹם טוּמְאָה, מָה לִי הָכָא, מָה לִי הָכָא"* — **once you have made the
sea a place of impurity, what difference is here from there?** You cannot in the same breath make the sea a
place of impurity and then exempt a mouse for being in it.

**Paying for the second lesson.** *"וְהַאי עַל הָאָרֶץ מִיבְּעֵי לֵיהּ לְהוֹצִיא סְפֵק טוּמְאָה צָפָה"* — but
"upon the earth" is needed for something else: Rav Yitzḥak bar Avdimi's rule that **floating uncertain
impurity** leaves you pure even in a private domain. How can one phrase teach two things? *"תַּרְתֵּי עַל
הָאָרֶץ כְּתִיבִי"* — **"upon the earth" is written twice.** One instance excludes the sea-mouse, the other
excludes floating doubt.

### 127a — Creatures of sea, land, fire and air

**R' Akiva's wonder.** The verse *"הַצָּב לְמִינֵהוּ"* is expanded to include the *arvad*, the *nefilim* and
the **salamander**. Reaching it, *"וּכְשֶׁהָיָה רַבִּי עֲקִיבָא מַגִּיעַ לְפָסוּק זֶה"* R' Akiva would exclaim
*"מָה רַבּוּ מַעֲשֶׂיךָ ה׳"* — there are creatures of the **sea** that die on land, of the **land** that die in
the sea, of the **fire** (the salamander) that die in air, and of the **air** that die in fire.

**The weasel's loneliness.** *"כֹּל שֶׁיֵּשׁ בַּיַּבָּשָׁה יֵשׁ בַּיָּם, חוּץ מִן הַחוּלְדָּה"* — every land
creature has a sea counterpart except the weasel. R' Zeira anchors it in *"הַאֲזִינוּ כׇּל יֹשְׁבֵי חָלֶד"* —
dry land is *ḥeled*, the weasel's *(ḥulda)* sole home.

**Neresh and the arvad.** A run of sharp Amoraic sayings about the untrustworthy folk of Neresh, Nehar
Pekod and Pumbedita gives way to Rav Huna bar Torta's tale: he saw a snake wound about a great lizard, and
in time a deadly **arvad** emerged. Since a snake and a lizard have unequal gestation and cannot breed, Rav
calls it *"נֵס בְּתוֹךְ נֵס"* — **a miracle within a miracle**, a calamity contrived to punish the wicked who
forced the crossbreeding.

### 127a (mishna) — The limb and flesh left hanging

*"הָאֵבֶר וְהַבָּשָׂר הַמְדוּלְדָּלִין בַּבְּהֵמָה, מְטַמְּאִין טוּמְאַת אֳכָלִין בִּמְקוֹמָן, וּצְרִיכִין הֶכְשֵׁר"*
— a limb (with bone and sinew) or a piece of flesh **partially severed and still dangling** from a living
animal is *not* yet a limb-from-the-living (which would defile like a carcass) nor severed flesh (which is
pure). If one intends to eat it, it can catch **food-impurity in place** — but only after it is **rendered
susceptible** by a liquid.

### 127b — Slaughtered, or died

- **Slaughtered:** *"נִשְׁחֲטָה הַבְּהֵמָה – הוּכְשְׁרוּ בְּדָמֶיהָ"* — R' Meir says the animal's own blood
  renders the dangling limb and flesh susceptible. **R' Shimon:** they were *not* rendered susceptible by
  that blood.
- **Died (no slaughter):** the flesh still needs *hechsher*; the limb **defiles as a limb-from-the-living
  but not as the limb of a carcass** (R' Meir), because death "makes it fall" and it counts as separated
  before death. **R' Shimon deems the limb pure.**

**Why not a carcass?** *"הֵיכִי דָמֵי?"* — if the limb can heal it should not even take food-impurity; if it
cannot, it should defile as a carcass. The answer: *"כִּי יִפּוֹל – עַד שֶׁיִּפּוֹל"* — the verse's "if it
falls" means a limb is a carcass only once it has **fully fallen off**; joined even by a hair, it is not.
Yet — the daf stresses — it *is* detached enough for **food-impurity**.

### 127b — Attached here, detached there

*"מְסַיַּיע לֵיהּ לְרַב חִיָּיא בַּר אָשֵׁי"* — this supports Shmuel's ruling: **figs that dried on the tree**
take food-impurity (treated as picked), yet one who picks them on Shabbat is **liable for a sin-offering**
(treated as attached). One object, split down the middle by which law is asking.

The Gemara tests the principle against dried cabbage and gourd (inedible when dried, so *not* susceptible,
whereas other produce is), against fruit on a **branch that broke off** (detached for everything) versus
fruit **dried on the tree** (attached for Shabbat, detached for impurity), and settles each with *"הָא
כִּדְאִיתָא וְהָא כִּדְאִיתָא"* — this case as it is, that case as it is.

### 127b — Why do R' Meir and R' Shimon really disagree?

The blood touched the animal's body but not the dangling limb. So the dispute is *how the limb relates to
the body*:

- **Rabba:** *"בִּבְהֵמָה נַעֲשֵׂית יָד לְאֵבֶר קָמִיפַּלְגִי"* — whether **the animal becomes a handle** for
  its limb. R' Meir: yes, so the limb is rendered susceptible along with the body. R' Shimon: no.
- **Abaye:** they agree the animal is no handle; they split on whether a small part hanging off a large one
  — where you *"אוֹחֵז בְּקָטָן וְאֵין גָּדוֹל עוֹלֶה עִמּוֹ"*, grasp the small and the large does **not** rise
  with it — still counts as one body with it. **R' Yoḥanan** reads the dispute the same way.

**R' Yoḥanan's snag.** He sets R' Meir against R' Meir: here R' Meir treats "grasp the small, the large does
not rise" as one — but in *Tevul Yom* (3:1) a partially sliced piece of *teruma* seems to say the reverse.
His resolution, carried onto 128a, is *"מוּחְלֶפֶת הַשִּׁיטָה"* — **the names in that mishna are reversed.**

## The distinctions, side by side

The second half keeps asking one thing of very different objects: *attached, or detached — and for which
law?* Every cell traces to the walkthrough above.

| Case | For food / carcass impurity | For Shabbat / attachment |
|---|---|---|
| **Dangling limb or flesh** (*meduldal*) | Detached — takes food-impurity | Attached — not a carcass "until it falls" |
| **Figs dried on the tree** (Shmuel) | Detached — takes food-impurity | Attached — picking is a Shabbat *ḥatat* |
| **Fruit on a branch that broke off** (*nifshach*) | Detached | Detached — for all matters |
| **Fruit dried on the standing tree** (*yavshu*) | Detached | Attached — for Shabbat |

---

## One line to carry with you

> A single object can be *detached* for one law and *attached* for another in the very same instant — the
> Torah is not asking "is it joined?" but "is it joined **for this**?"
