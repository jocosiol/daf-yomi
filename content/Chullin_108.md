## The big picture (read this first)

The chapter of meat and milk now reaches its central kitchen accident: a **drop of milk** lands on a
piece of meat cooking in a pot. Not a theory but the thing that actually happens on a stove — and the
whole machinery of forbidden mixtures gets brought to bear on it. When is the piece forbidden? When is
the *whole pot* forbidden? And a deeper question underneath: is it the milk that is forbidden, or has
the *meat itself* changed?

> Two principles collide over one drop. **Notein ta'am** — a forbidden food that imparts *flavor*
> forbids what absorbs it, even with no forbidden *substance* left (**ta'am velo mamash**). And
> **min b'mino lo batel** — a forbidden food cannot be nullified inside more of its own type. Put
> them together as Rav does and the drop does not stay a drop: the piece it flavored **"becomes
> neveila"** in its own right, and being meat among meat, it can drag the entire pot down with it.

Three movements:

1. **Ta'am velo mamash — Torah or rabbinic?** (108a) — Abaye's proof from meat-in-milk that flavor
   forbids by Torah law everywhere, and Rava's answer that cooking is a special case.
2. **The piece that "becomes neveila"** (108a) — Rav's rule, why the gravy does or does not nullify
   it, and the discovery that Rav must hold *efshar lisochato asur*.
3. **Can you wring it out?** (108a–108b) — the named dispute, tested against the pot of milk and the
   half-and-half olive-bulk, and finally traced back to a dispute among the Tannaim themselves.

## Key concepts & terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **נוֹתֵן טַעַם** *(notein ta'am)* | "Imparting flavor" — the threshold of prohibition in a mixture: a forbidden food forbids only if enough remains to be *tasted* (roughly one part in sixty). |
| **טַעַם וְלֹא מַמָּשׁ** *(ta'am velo mamash)* | Flavor with no actual forbidden *substance* left; Abaye holds it forbids by Torah law everywhere, Rava that this is special to cooking. |
| **חֲתִיכָה נַעֲשֵׂית נְבֵלָה** *(chatichah naaseit neveila)* | Rav's rule: a piece that absorbs a forbidden flavor does not merely *carry* the ban — it *itself* turns non-kosher, and can then forbid others. |
| **מִין בְּמִינוֹ** *(min b'mino)* | A forbidden food inside more of its own type; per Rabbi Yehuda it is *never nullified*, which is how one neveila-piece forbids all the meat. |
| **אֶפְשָׁר לְסוֹחֲטוֹ** *(efshar lisochato)* | "It is possible to wring it out" — a piece whose absorbed forbidden substance could in principle be squeezed back out; is it still forbidden, or permitted? |
| **רוֹטֶב עָבֶה / רַכָּה** *(rotev ave / raka)* | Thick gravy (concentrated meat, hence the meat's own *type*) versus thin gravy (a different substance that *can* nullify the piece). |
| **חִדּוּשׁ** *(chidush)* | A novelty — meat and milk each permitted alone, forbidden only together; "from a novelty we do not derive" a rule for ordinary cases. |
| **דֶּרֶךְ בִּשּׁוּל** *(derech bishul)* | "The manner of cooking" — Rava's point that the Torah banned meat-in-milk *as cooking*, and cooking simply *is* the transfer of flavor. |
| **חֲצִי שִׁיעוּר** *(chatzi shiur)* | Half of the minimum measure; one is not flogged for it — the lever used to show Rav must hold the *milk*, not only the meat, is forbidden. |
| **גְּדִי אָסְרָה תּוֹרָה** *(gedi asra Torah)* | "The Torah forbade the *kid*" — the first reading of the verse, that only the meat is rendered non-kosher, not the milk it cooked in. |
| **קֵיפָה** *(kipa)* | The spice-and-froth layer floating on the stew; the Rabbis require the milk to flavor even *it* before the pot is forbidden. |
| **נִיעֵר וְכִסָּה** *(niár v'kisa)* | Stirred and covered — the two acts that speed the drop's diffusion through the pot, and so decide between Rabbi Yehuda and the Rabbis. |

## Who's who in today's daf

**Tannaim**

- **Rabbi Yehuda** (bar Ilai) — fourth-generation Tanna, a leading student of Rabbi Akiva; here the
  voice of *min b'mino lo batel* and the piece that "becomes neveila," and the source Rav follows.
- **The Rabbis** (*Chachamim*) — the anonymous majority against Rabbi Yehuda: judge the whole pot, not
  the single piece.
- **Rabbi** (Yehuda HaNasi, *Rebbi*) — fifth/sixth-generation Tanna, redactor of the Mishna; brokers the
  compromise between Rabbi Yehuda and the Rabbis by whether the pot was stirred and covered.

## Walking through the sugya, step by step

### 108a — Does flavor alone forbid by Torah law?

**The mishna.** A **drop of milk** falls on a piece of meat in the pot. If it is enough to **impart
flavor** (*notein ta'am*) to *that piece*, the piece is forbidden. If he then **stirred** the pot — so
the drop is no longer concentrated on one piece — the question is whether there is enough to impart
flavor to the *entire pot*; if so, the whole pot is forbidden.

**Abaye's principle.** He reads a rule out of the mishna that reaches far beyond meat and milk:

> **טַעְמוֹ וְלֹא מַמָּשׁוֹ בְּעָלְמָא — דְּאוֹרָיְיתָא.**
> Flavor without any actual substance — *in general* — forbids by Torah law.

**The proof.** Suppose flavor were only *rabbinic* elsewhere. Then why not learn from meat-in-milk,
where flavor plainly forbids by Torah law? You would answer: because meat-in-milk is a **chidush**, a
novelty — each is permitted alone, forbidden only cooked together — and *from a novelty we do not
derive*. But if it is a pure novelty, the prohibition should bite **even without notein ta'am**. Since
in fact the measure *is* notein ta'am, it behaves like an ordinary mixture — so one *may* learn from
it, and flavor is Torah law everywhere.

**Rava's answer.** Not so:

> **דֶּרֶךְ בִּשּׁוּל אָסְרָה תּוֹרָה.**
> The Torah forbade meat-in-milk **in the manner of cooking** — and cooking *is* the imparting of
> flavor.

So its measure is notein ta'am not because it is an ordinary mixture, but because the very act the
Torah named — cooking — is flavor-transfer. It remains a novelty, and Abaye's bridge to "everywhere"
collapses.

**Rav: the piece becomes neveila.** On the mishna's forbidden piece, Rav says something stronger than
"it is forbidden":

> **כֵּיוָן שֶׁנָּתַן טַעַם בַּחֲתִיכָה — חֲתִיכָה עַצְמָהּ נַעֲשֵׂית נְבֵלָה, וְאוֹסֶרֶת כׇּל הַחֲתִיכוֹת כּוּלָּן, מִפְּנֵי שֶׁהֵן מִינָהּ.**
> Once the milk flavors the piece, the piece *itself* becomes non-kosher — and it forbids **all** the
> pieces in the pot, even sixty times its size, because they are its own type.

The engine is **min b'mino lo batel**: like cannot nullify like.

**Does Rav argue with Rava?** Mar Zutra, son of Rav Mari, presses Ravina. Rav follows Rabbi Yehuda,
who holds min b'mino is not nullified. But Rava had explained Rabbi Yehuda more finely: in a mixture of
*a forbidden type, its permitted like, and a third thing*, you **set aside the like** as if absent and
let the **unlike** rise up and nullify. Here the unlike is the **gravy** — it should nullify the piece.
Does Rav reject that?

**Ravina's answer — thick or thin.** No. In **thin gravy** (*rotev raka*), a different substance, Rav
too would let it nullify. The mishna's case is **thick gravy** (*rotev ave*), which is itself
concentrated meat — so it is *like*, not *unlike*, and nullifies nothing.

**Which forces the deeper question.** If Rav held *efshar lisochato mutar* — that a piece you could
wring the forbidden substance back out of is permitted — the piece would never "become neveila"; the
milk would just redistribute and be nullified. So Rav must hold **efshar lisochato asur**: what soaked
in stays forbidden even in principle wringable.

**The named dispute.**

> **רַב וְרַבִּי חֲנִינָא וְרַבִּי יוֹחָנָן — אֶפְשָׁר לְסוֹחֲטוֹ אָסוּר; שְׁמוּאֵל וְרַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן בַּר רַבִּי וְרֵישׁ לָקִישׁ — אֶפְשָׁר לְסוֹחֲטוֹ מוּתָּר.**
> Rav, Rabbi Ḥanina and Rabbi Yoḥanan: a wringable piece is forbidden. Shmuel, Rabbi Shimon son of
> Rabbi, and Reish Lakish: a wringable piece is permitted.

**A challenge to Rav.** But an olive-bulk of meat that fell into a large pot of **milk** — Rav says the
**meat** is forbidden and the **milk permitted**. If a wringable piece is forbidden…

### 108b — Testing Rav, then finding the dispute among the Tannaim

**…why is the milk permitted?** The milk the meat absorbed is now non-kosher; seeping back out into its
own type, it should forbid the whole pot of milk.

**First answer — the verse.** Rav does hold *efshar lisochato asur*; the pot of milk is different,
because the Torah says **"You shall not cook a kid in its mother's milk"** —

> **גְּדִי אָסְרָה תּוֹרָה, וְלֹא חָלָב.**
> The Torah forbade the *kid* (the meat), not the *milk*.

So the meat, having absorbed, is forbidden — but the milk it soaked up is not itself rendered neveila.

**But wait — does Rav really say that?** He also ruled: **half an olive-bulk of meat and half of milk**
cooked together — one is **flogged for eating** the combined olive-bulk, though not for cooking. If the
Torah forbids only the meat, why flog him for eating a mere **half** olive-bulk of meat? That is
*chatzi shiur*, less than the measure.

**Second answer.** Rather, Rav holds the **milk is forbidden too**; the pot-of-milk case is one where
the meat fell into **boiling** milk — it **absorbs but does not expel** (*miv'la bala, mif'lat lo
palet*). And when the pot cools and it would expel? The case is where **he removed the meat first**.

**The half-and-half, on its own.** *Whichever way you look at it*: if the two halves **combine** to a
full measure, he should be flogged for cooking too; if they do **not** combine, he should not be flogged
for eating either. Answer: they do not combine — the eating-liability case is where they come from a
**large pot** already holding a full measure of the forbidden mixture. And **Levi** disagrees outright:
one is flogged for cooking as well — *as one is flogged for eating, so for cooking* — and the cooking
that counts is cooking **fit for others to eat**.

**And it is a Tannaic dispute all along.** *Efshar lisochato* itself splits the Tannaim:

- **Rabbi Yehuda** (baraita): once the drop flavors the piece, the piece becomes neveila and forbids all
  the pieces as its own type. (This is Rav's source.)
- **The Rabbis:** not until the milk imparts flavor to the **gravy, the spice-froth (*kipa*), and the
  pieces** — i.e., judge the whole pot, for the milk diffuses.

**Rabbi's compromise.**

> **נִרְאִין דִּבְרֵי רַבִּי יְהוּדָה בְּשֶׁלֹּא נִיעֵר וְשֶׁלֹּא כִּסָּה, וְדִבְרֵי חֲכָמִים בְּשֶׁנִּיעֵר וְכִסָּה.**
> Rabbi Yehuda is right when the pot was **not stirred and not covered**; the Rabbis when it **was
> stirred and covered**.

**Pinning down what that means.** *Not stirred, not covered* cannot mean *never at all* — then the piece
merely absorbs and never expels, and nothing spreads to forbid. It must mean: not at the start (while the
drop was concentrated), only later. But then it *did* absorb and *did* expel — so why is the pot
forbidden? Because Rabbi holds **efshar lisochato asur**: once the piece turned, wringing the milk back
out no longer helps it. That is where the daf leaves the knife poised for tomorrow.

## The distinctions, side by side

The mishna's own dispute, drawn out in the baraita and refereed by Rabbi: *when does one tainted piece
forbid the entire pot?* Three answers, each asked the same two questions —

| View | When does the tainted piece forbid the whole pot? | The reason |
|---|---|---|
| **Rabbi Yehuda** | The moment the drop gives flavor to **that one piece** | *min b'mino* — the piece is now neveila, and its own type cannot nullify it |
| **The Rabbis** | Only if the milk flavors the **gravy, spices, and all the pieces** | the milk diffuses through the pot; judge the whole, not one piece |
| **Rabbi (compromise)** | Rabbi Yehuda's rule when **unstirred and uncovered**; the Rabbis' when **stirred and covered** | stirring and covering decide whether the drop stayed put or spread |

## One line to carry with you

> A forbidden taste that soaks into the meat does not just sit there waiting to be diluted — for Rav,
> the piece *itself* turns, and once it has turned, wringing it back out no longer saves it.
