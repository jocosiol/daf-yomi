## The big picture (read this first)

Yesterday's mishnah opened the chapter with a flat rule — **all meat** is forbidden to cook in milk,
except fish and grasshoppers — and a rabbinic fence beside it: keep meat and cheese off the same
table. Today the Gemara pries a single, sharp question out of that mishnah and worries it from every
side:

> Is the flesh of **birds** cooked in milk forbidden by the **Torah**, or only by the **Sages**?
> The whole daf is an argument over how much weight one mishnah can bear — and the deciding move is a
> principle about the *architecture of rabbinic law*: the Sages do not build **a decree to guard a
> decree**. If placing fowl beside cheese is itself only a fence, then a fence around *that* fence
> would be one story too high.

Three movements:

1. **Whose mishnah is it?** (104a) — the two clauses seem to follow opposing Tannaim (the Rabbis on
   meat-and-milk, Rabbi Akiva on vows). Rav Yosef and Rav Ashi each rescue a single author.
2. **Torah or rabbinic — read from the fence** (104a–104b) — Rav Yosef's elegant inference from
   "don't share the table," the *challah* proof that the Sages avoid stacked decrees, and Abaye, Rav
   Sheshet and Abaye again dismantling and rebuilding it.
3. **Beit Shammai, Beit Hillel, and the table** (104b) — a new mishnah, Rabbi Yosei's list of
   "leniencies of Beit Shammai," a lesson in citing your sources, and the real-life manners of
   passing from fowl to cheese.

## Key concepts & terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **כׇּל הַבָּשָׂר** *(Kol HaBasar)* | "All meat" — the chapter, on cooking and eating meat with milk. |
| **חַיָּה וָעוֹף אֵינוֹ מִן הַתּוֹרָה** *(chaya va'of eino min haTorah)* | Rabbi Akiva's view that wild animals and birds in milk are forbidden only rabbinically, not by the Torah. |
| **גְּזֵירָה לִגְזֵירָה** *(gezeirah ligzeirah)* | "A decree [to guard] a decree." The Sages generally do not build one rabbinic fence on top of another — the daf's central lever. |
| **נָסֵיב לַהּ אַלִּיבָּא דְתַנָּאֵי** *(naseiv lah aliba d'tannai)* | Recording a mishnah's clauses each according to a different Tanna — Rav Yosef's way to keep Rebbi as sole author. |
| **כֹּל מִילֵּי דְּמִימְּלִיךְ עֲלֵיהּ שָׁלִיחַ** *(kol milei d'mimlich alei shaliach)* | Rabbi Akiva's rule on vows: whatever an agent would pause to ask about counts as that category — so a borderline gourd is a "vegetable." |
| **הַעֲלָאָה** *(ha'ala'ah)* | "Bringing up" onto the table — the act the rabbinic fence forbids, distinct from eating. |
| **צוֹנֵן בְּצוֹנֵן** *(tzonen betzonen)* | Cold-on-cold: two cold foods touching, which does not cook and so is itself only rabbinic. |
| **אִילְפָּס רִאשׁוֹן / כְּלִי שֵׁנִי** *(ilpas rishon / keli sheni)* | A primary stewpot straight off the fire, which cooks (Torah-level), versus a secondary vessel, which does not. |
| **מִקּוּלֵּי בֵּית שַׁמַּאי וּמֵחוּמְרֵי בֵּית הִלֵּל** *(mikulei Beit Shammai)* | A dispute in which Beit Shammai are lenient and Beit Hillel strict — the reverse of the usual pattern. |
| **בְּאַפִּיקוֹרַן** *(be'apikoran)* | "Freely" — Agra's ruling that fowl and cheese need no hand-washing or mouth-wiping in between. |
| **דָּבָר בְּשֵׁם אוֹמְרוֹ** *(davar beshem omro)* | Reporting a teaching in the name of the one who said it — which "brings redemption to the world." |

## Who's who in today's daf

**Tannaim**

- **Rabbi Akiva** — third-generation Tanna, the towering figure of the Mishnah's structure. Here:
  wild animals and birds in milk are only rabbinic, and his rule on vows via the "agent's inquiry."
- **The Rabbis** (*chachamim*) — Rabbi Akiva's disputants, who hold bird-in-milk is Torah law and
  that gourds are not "vegetables" for a vow.
- **Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi** (Rebbi) — fifth-generation Tanna, redactor of the Mishnah. Rav Yosef makes
  him the mishnah's single author, following different Tannaim in each clause.
- **Beit Shammai and Beit Hillel** — the two great schools of the late Second Temple / early
  post-Destruction era. Here, unusually, Beit Shammai are the lenient side.
- **Rabbi Yosei** (ben Chalafta) — fourth-generation Tanna, student of Rabbi Akiva. Lists this among
  six "leniencies of Beit Shammai," and is named to honour citing one's source.

**Babylonian Amoraim**

- **Rav Yosef** (bar Chiyya) — third generation, Pumbedita. Both the "Rebbi as author" reading and
  the inference that bird-in-milk is Torah law are his.
- **Rav Ashi** — sixth generation, the great redactor of the Talmud at Sura. Reads the whole mishnah
  as Rabbi Akiva. (Not Rabbi Asi the Eretz-Yisrael Amora of 103.)
- **Abaye** — fourth generation, head of Pumbedita. Dismantles Rav Yosef's *challah* proof, then
  supplies the "boiling stewpot" that keeps the fence standing.
- **Rav Sheshet** — third generation, a blind master of tradition at Nehardea/Mechoza. Objects that
  cold-on-cold is still only rabbinic.
- **Agra** — described only as the **father-in-law of Rabbi Abba**; a minor figure best known for
  this ruling on fowl and cheese. I would not press his identity further.
- **Rav Yitzchak son of Rav Mesharshiya** — a later Babylonian Amora; the guest at Rav Ashi's table
  who ate by daylight. A relatively minor figure.

*Look-alikes worth flagging:* **Rav Ashi** the redactor (Babylonian) is not **Rabbi Asi** the
Eretz-Yisrael Amora met on 103. **Rabbi Yosei** here is the Tanna ben Chalafta, not any of the later
Amoraim named Yosei. And **Rabbi Akiva** the Tanna should not be confused with the many *Rav*-titled
Amoraim.

## Walking through the sugya, step by step

### 104a — One mishnah, two Tannaim?

The Gemara reads the mishnah's two clauses against each other.

**The first clause implies too much.** "All meat" — with no distinction between animal and bird —
means **bird in milk is forbidden by Torah law**. That cannot be **Rabbi Akiva**, who held that
undomesticated animals and birds in milk are only rabbinic:

> **דְּאִי רַבִּי עֲקִיבָא – הָאָמַר: חַיָּה וָעוֹף אֵינוֹ מִן הַתּוֹרָה.**
> For if it were Rabbi Akiva, did he not say that wild animal and bird are not [forbidden] by the
> Torah?

**But the last clause implies the opposite.** "One who vows off meat may eat fish and grasshoppers"
— implying fowl *is* covered by his vow. That sounds like **Rabbi Akiva**, who held **כֹּל מִילֵּי
דְּמִימְּלִיךְ עֲלֵיהּ שָׁלִיחַ — בַּר מִינֵיהּ הוּא**: anything an agent would *pause to ask about* counts as
that type. The proof is the gourds baraita: a person tells his agent "buy vegetables," and the agent
returns "I found only gourds" — he asks, because gourds are a borderline vegetable; he would never
say "I found only legumes." So one clause seems to be the Rabbis, the other Rabbi Akiva.

**Two ways to make it one author.**

- **Rav Yosef:** the whole mishnah is **Rebbi** (Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi), who **נָסֵיב לַהּ אַלִּיבָּא
  דְתַנָּאֵי** — records it following different Tannaim: on vows like Rabbi Akiva, on meat-and-milk
  like the Rabbis.
- **Rav Ashi:** the whole mishnah is **Rabbi Akiva**, and "all meat" simply means *some by Torah law*
  (domesticated animals) and *some by rabbinic law* (wild animals and birds) — all forbidden, except
  fish and grasshoppers, which are neither.

### 104a–104b — Torah or rabbinic? Read it off the table

**Rav Yosef's inference from the fence.** The mishnah forbids even *bringing bird up onto the table*
with cheese. Conclude, he says, that bird-in-milk is **Torah law**:

> **דְּאִי סָלְקָא דַעְתָּךְ דְּרַבָּנַן – אֲכִילָה גּוּפַהּ גְּזֵירָה, וַאֲנַן נִגְזַר הַעֲלָאָה אַטּוּ אֲכִילָה?**
> For if you say it is only rabbinic, then *eating* it is already a decree — and would we then decree
> against *placing it on the table* to guard *eating*? We do not make **a decree to guard a decree**.

**The proof that the Sages avoid stacked decrees** comes from *challah* of the diaspora (*Challah*
4:8): challah separated outside Eretz Yisrael — itself only a rabbinic obligation — **may be eaten
with a non-priest at the same table**, and given to any priest. The Sages did not fence it, because
fencing a fence is one decree too many.

**Abaye's objection** (104b): that proof would only work if the diaspora challah had been *brought
into* Eretz Yisrael, where there would be a real reason to fence it (lest it be confused with true,
Torah-level challah) and yet the Sages still did not. But the mishnah speaks of challah that *stays*
in the diaspora, where there is simply nothing to fence — so it proves nothing about stacked decrees.
Here, by contrast, permitting fowl-and-cheese on the table could lead to **meat**-and-cheese on the
table and then to eating meat-in-milk, a Torah violation. So the fence around fowl is not a fence
around a mere fence.

**Rav Sheshet pushes back:** even so, meat-and-cheese sitting cold beside each other is only
**צוֹנֵן בְּצוֹנֵן**, cold-on-cold — itself no more than rabbinic — so we are *still* fencing a fence.
**Abaye:** no — the decree is **שֶׁמָּא יַעֲלֶה בְּאִילְפָּס רוֹתֵחַ**, lest he set it down in a boiling
stewpot. **Objection:** a stewpot is a **כְּלִי שֵׁנִי**, a secondary vessel, and a secondary vessel
does not cook. **Resolution:** the fear is an **אִילְפָּס רִאשׁוֹן**, a primary vessel straight off the
fire — that genuinely cooks meat in milk, a Torah prohibition. The fence stands.

### 104b — Beit Shammai, Beit Hillel, and citing your source

A new mishnah maps the table manners:

> **הָעוֹף עוֹלֶה עִם הַגְּבִינָה עַל הַשּׁוּלְחָן, וְאֵינוֹ נֶאֱכָל — דִּבְרֵי בֵּית שַׁמַּאי. וּבֵית הִלֵּל אוֹמְרִים: לֹא עוֹלֶה וְלֹא נֶאֱכָל.**
> Fowl may be brought up with cheese onto the table but not eaten with it — Beit Shammai. Beit
> Hillel: neither brought up nor eaten.

**Rabbi Yosei** calls this **מִקּוּלֵּי בֵּית שַׁמַּאי וּמֵחוּמְרֵי בֵּית הִלֵּל** — one of the rare cases
where Beit Shammai are the lenient ones and Beit Hillel the strict. And the mishnah clarifies: this
is only the table you *eat* on; on the table where you *plate the food*, you may set meat and cheese
side by side without concern.

**Isn't Rabbi Yosei just the anonymous first Tanna?** The Gemara wonders if they differ over *eating*
— but a baraita shows Rabbi Yosei's own list of **שִׁשָּׁה דְּבָרִים**, six such disputes, includes this
one in exactly the first Tanna's words. So they do not differ. Then why name him at all?

> **כׇּל הָאוֹמֵר דָּבָר בְּשֵׁם אוֹמְרוֹ מֵבִיא גְּאוּלָּה לָעוֹלָם.**
> Whoever reports a thing in the name of the one who said it brings redemption to the world —

as Esther told the king **בְּשֵׁם מׇרְדֳּכָי**, in Mordechai's name, and the rescue of the Jews turned on
it. The mishnah names Rabbi Yosei to teach that very lesson.

**And the manners themselves.** Agra, Rabbi Abba's father-in-law, taught that fowl and cheese may be
eaten **בְּאַפִּיקוֹרַן** — freely, without washing the hands or wiping the mouth between them. When Rav
Yitzchak son of Rav Mesharshiya ate cheese and then *meat* at Rav Ashi's house without washing, they
challenged him — Agra permitted only *fowl* after cheese, not *meat*. His answer: that caution is
only **at night**, when you cannot see your hands; **בִּימָמָא הָא חָזֵינָא**, by day I can see they are
clean. The daf ends turning to the palate itself — Beit Shammai say **מְקַנֵּחַ**, wipe; Beit Hillel say
**מֵדִיחַ**, rinse — which is where tomorrow begins.

## The distinctions, side by side

The daf's question in one grid — which flesh, and on whose authority:

| The flesh | Cooked in milk | Up on the table with cheese | Covered by a vow off "meat" |
|---|---|---|---|
| **בְּהֵמָה** — domesticated animal | Forbidden by **Torah** law, on every view | Forbidden — the Sages' fence around eating | Yes |
| **חַיָּה וָעוֹף** — wild animal and bird | **R' Akiva:** rabbinic only. **Rav Yosef:** Torah — else the table-fence would be a fence around a fence | Forbidden (Beit Hillel). **Beit Shammai:** may be brought up, only not eaten | Yes — an agent would pause to ask about it, so it counts as meat |
| **דָּגִים וַחֲגָבִים** — fish and grasshoppers | Permitted outright | Permitted | No — he may eat them |

## One line to carry with you

> The Sages fenced the law, but they did not fence their fences — a rule that keeps rabbinic caution
> from climbing forever. Read carefully, one line about a shared table tells you whether the pot
> forbids fowl by the Torah's word or only theirs.
