## The big picture (read this first)

Yesterday closed with Beit Shammai and Beit Hillel disagreeing over how to clear the mouth between
fowl and cheese — **מְקַנֵּחַ**, wipe, or **מֵדִיחַ**, rinse. Today opens by dissolving that dispute
into agreement and then widens the lens: not just *how* you separate meat from milk in one meal, but
*how long* you wait, and — once hands are in view — the whole rabbinic law of washing them.

> Two halves make one daf. The **first** is tightly legal: clear the palate with bread, wait after
> meat before cheese but not the reverse, and keep straight your **first**, **middle**, and **last
> waters** — which are obligation, which are optional, and why. The **second** is a long, almost
> hypnotic string of Abaye's confessions — *"at first I thought the reason was X; then the Master
> told me it was really Y"* — that turn ordinary table manners into a map of health, danger, and the
> unseen world.

Three movements:

1. **Clearing the palate, and waiting** (105a) — the Beit Shammai / Beit Hillel dispute collapses into
   "each said one thing, they do not argue"; Rabbi Zeira on wiping with bread; and how long to wait
   between meat and cheese.
2. **"Vinegar, son of wine"** (105a) — Mar Ukva and Shmuel measure themselves against their fathers,
   which opens onto the value of *patrolling your property* and three sages who did.
3. **The waters, and the reasons behind the customs** (105a–105b) — first / middle / last waters as
   obligation, mitzva, or optional; then Abaye's chain of "I used to think… the Master told me,"
   where each familiar practice hides a reason of health, poverty, or demons.

## Walking through the sugya, step by step

### 105a — Clearing the palate, and how long to wait

**Resolving Beit Shammai and Beit Hillel.** The Gemara tries three readings of their dispute over
clearing the mouth after meat. The first two fail — one would make Rabbi Zeira rule like Beit Shammai
(unlikely), the other would demand the case be listed in *Eduyyot* among the "leniencies of Beit
Shammai." The third holds:

> **בֵּית שַׁמַּאי אוֹמְרִים: מְקַנֵּחַ, וְהוּא הַדִּין לְמֵדִיחַ, וּבֵית הִלֵּל אוֹמְרִים: מֵדִיחַ, וְהוּא הַדִּין לִמְקַנֵּחַ. מָר אָמַר חֲדָא, וּמָר אָמַר חֲדָא, וְלָא פְּלִיגִי.**
> Beit Shammai say *wipe* — and the same is true of rinsing; Beit Hillel say *rinse* — and the same is
> true of wiping. One Sage stated one thing and one the other, and they do not disagree.

**Rabbi Zeira on wiping.** Cited in passing, now examined for itself: **אֵין קִינּוּחַ הַפֶּה אֶלָּא
בְּפַת** — the mouth is wiped only with **bread**. And only wheat bread (barley crumbles), only cold
(warm bread softens and sticks), only soft (hard bread will not clean). The bottom line: **everything**
serves to wipe **except flour, dates, and vegetables**.

**How long between meat and cheese?** Rav Asi asked Rabbi Yochanan how long to wait — "no time at
all," came the answer. But that clashes with Rav Chisda, so the question is re-read: it was about
**cheese, then meat** — there, no wait. Meat first is different:

> **אָכַל בָּשָׂר – אָסוּר לֶאֱכוֹל גְּבִינָה, גְּבִינָה – מוּתָּר לֶאֱכוֹל בָּשָׂר.**
> If one ate meat, he may not eat cheese [at once]; if he ate cheese, he may eat meat.

Rav Acha bar Yosef pressed: what about **meat lodged between the teeth**? Rav Chisda answered by
quoting the verse of the quail — **"the meat was still between their teeth"** (Bamidbar 11:33) — it
is still meat.

**"Vinegar, son of wine."** Two sages rate themselves below their fathers. **Mar Ukva:** my father,
after meat, would not eat cheese until the same hour the next day; I only skip it for *this* meal.
**Shmuel:** my father patrolled his fields twice a day; I only once — for **מַאן דְּסָיַיר נִכְסֵיהּ כֹּל
יוֹמָא מַשְׁכַּח אִסְתֵּירָא**, one who inspects his property daily finds a coin. Two vignettes follow:
**Abaye** caught his sharecropper carrying off firewood ("to the Master's house," he lied) — "the
Sages preempted you." And **Rav Asi**, skeptical there was any coin to be found, one day plugged a
burst irrigation channel with his own cloak and saved his field: "*Now* I have found all of Mar
Shmuel's coins."

**The three waters.** Rav Idi bar Avin in the name of Rav Yitzchak bar Ashyan: **first waters** (before
bread) are a **mitzva**; **last waters** (before *bircat hamazon*) an **obligation** (*chova*). A
baraita calls the first waters *chova* too — resolved: relative to the **optional** middle waters, a
mitzva may be called an obligation. The baraita is then unpacked: first waters may be poured into a
vessel **or onto the ground**; last waters only into a vessel (the practical difference: wood
slivers). First waters may be **hot or cold**; last waters only cold, since hot water **מְפַעְפְּעִין
אֶת הַיָּדַיִם** — softens the hands and fails to carry off the grease (*zuhama*). Rabbi Yannai, via
Rav Yitzchak bar Yosef, adds the limit: hot is fine unless **הַיָּד סוֹלֶדֶת בָּהֶן**, the hand recoils
from the heat.

### 105b — The reasons hiding inside the customs

**Middle waters.** "Optional," said the baraita — but Rav Nachman narrows it: only **between one dish
and another**; between a **cooked dish and cheese**, washing is an **obligation**.

**Why are last waters obligatory?** Rav Yehuda son of Rabbi Chiyya: because **מֶלַח סְדוֹמִית**,
Sodomite salt, may cling to the hands and **blind the eyes**. Abaye: it turns up as little as a pinch
in a whole *kor* — and yet, Rav Ashi rules, even someone who merely *handled* salt must wash.

**Abaye's chain of "at first I would say."** Here the daf becomes a litany. For each ordinary custom
Abaye offers the tidy, rational reason he once assumed — and then reports the deeper reason **the
Master** (Rabba) gave him:

- **Not pouring last waters on the ground** — not mere messiness, but **רוּחַ רָעָה**, an evil spirit,
  rests on them.
- **Not removing anything from the table while someone holds his cup to drink** — not fear of a mishap,
  but that it is bad for the health, bringing on **רוּחַ צְרָדָא**, a migraine. (Only if you take and
  do not return it, only beyond four cubits, only something needed for the meal. Mar bar Rav Ashi was
  strict even about a mortar and pestle.)
- **Gathering up the crumbs** — not tidiness, but because leaving them **invites poverty**. A man the
  angel of poverty could not touch — so careful was he with crumbs — nearly slipped when crumbs fell
  in the grass; he uprooted and drowned the grass, and heard the angel wail that he had been evicted
  from his home.
- **Not drinking the foam** off a drink — not distaste, but because it is bad for **catarrh**; blowing
  it off harms the head, brushing it aside invites poverty; the remedy is to sink it back in.
- **Not eating vegetables from a bundle the gardener tied** — not the look of gluttony, but that it is
  bad for **witchcraft**. When a sorceress could not bewitch Rav Chisda and Rabba bar Rav Huna on a
  boat, she named three habits that shielded them — and this was one.
- **Not eating vegetables that fell on the table** — not repulsiveness, but bad breath.
- **Not sitting under a gutter** (*marzeiv*) — not the runoff, but that **demons** frequent it. Porters
  who rested a wine barrel under a gutter had it burst; Mar bar Rav Ashi sounded the horns and
  excommunicated the demon, who protested, "they set the barrel on my ear!" — and was ordered to pay.
- **Pouring a little water from the mouth of a pitcher** before drinking — not twigs, but **foul
  waters**. A demon-servant in Rav Pappa's house explained his delay at the river the same way: he
  had waited "for the bad waters to pass."

## The distinctions, side by side

Three washings, four questions, and the prose spends three paragraphs getting there:

| The waters | Grade | Poured into | Temperature |
|---|---|---|---|
| **First** — before bread | **Mitzva** (the baraita's *chova* is only relative to the optional middle) | A vessel **or** the ground | Hot or cold — up to *הַיָּד סוֹלֶדֶת בָּהֶן*, where the hand recoils |
| **Middle** — between dishes | **Optional** — but Rav Nachman: an **obligation** between a cooked dish and cheese | — | — |
| **Last** — before *bircat hamazon* | **Obligation** (*chova*) — Sodomite salt on the hands can blind the eyes | A vessel only; the practical difference is wood slivers | Cold only — hot *מְפַעְפְּעִין אֶת הַיָּדַיִם*, softens the hands and leaves the *zuhama* |

## Key concepts & terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **מְקַנֵּחַ / מֵדִיחַ** *(mekaneiach / mediach)* | Wiping the mouth with solid food versus rinsing it with water — Beit Shammai and Beit Hillel's two ways to clear the palate. |
| **אֵין קִינּוּחַ אֶלָּא בְּפַת** *(ein kinuach ela be'fat)* | Rabbi Zeira's rule that only bread wipes the mouth effectively — and only wheat, cold, and soft. |
| **חַלָּא בַּר חַמְרָא** *(chala bar chamra)* | "Vinegar, son of wine" — a modest son measuring himself as the lesser produce of a greater father. |
| **סָיַיר נִכְסֵיהּ** *(sayar nichseih)* | Patrolling one's property; the sage's daily habit that "finds a coin" by heading off loss and theft. |
| **מַיִם רִאשׁוֹנִים / אֶמְצָעִיִּים / אַחֲרוֹנִים** *(mayim rishonim / emtza'iyim / acharonim)* | The first (before bread), middle (between courses), and last (before Grace) washings of the hands. |
| **חוֹבָה / מִצְוָה / רְשׁוּת** *(chova / mitzva / reshut)* | Obligation, mitzva, and optional — the three grades the daf assigns to the three waters. |
| **זוּהֲמָא** *(zuhama)* | The greasy filth on the hands that last waters must remove — which hot water fails to carry off. |
| **הַיָּד סוֹלֶדֶת בּוֹ** *(hayad soledet bo)* | "The hand recoils from it" — the threshold at which water is too hot to use. |
| **מֶלַח סְדוֹמִית** *(melach sedomit)* | Sodomite salt, a trace of which can blind the eyes — the reason last waters are an obligation. |
| **מֵרֵישׁ הֲוָה אָמֵינָא** *(mereish hava amina)* | "At first I would say" — Abaye's refrain, giving the surface reason before the Master's deeper one. |
| **רוּחַ רָעָה** *(ruach ra'a)* | An evil spirit — said to rest on the used last-waters if poured on the ground. |

## Who's who in today's daf

**Tannaim**

- **Beit Shammai and Beit Hillel** — the two great schools of the late Second Temple and early
  post-Destruction generations; here shown, in the end, not to disagree about clearing the palate.
- **Rabbi Elazar ben Arakh** and **Rabban Gamliel son of Rebbi** appear only at the very edge of the
  daf as its argument spills into 106; the working cast of 105 is Amoraic.

**Eretz-Yisrael Amoraim**

- **Rabbi Yochanan** (bar Nappacha) — second generation, head of the academy in Tiberias, the central
  voice of the Jerusalem Talmud. Rules "no wait" between cheese and meat.
- **Rabbi Zeira** — a Babylonian who ascended to Eretz Yisrael (third generation there); gives the
  rule that only bread wipes the mouth.
- **Rabbi Yannai** — first-generation Eretz-Yisrael Amora, a teacher of Rabbi Yochanan's circle;
  supplies the "hand recoils" limit on hot water.
- **Rav Asi** — a Babylonian who settled in Eretz Yisrael, close colleague of Rav Ami and a frequent
  questioner of Rabbi Yochanan; he asks about waiting between meat and cheese, and is the sage of the
  burst-channel story. **Not** Rav Ashi the redactor (below).

**Babylonian Amoraim**

- **Shmuel** — first generation, head of Nehardea; his "father" is Abba bar Abba. Source of the maxim
  that patrolling your property daily "finds a coin."
- **Mar Ukva** — first-generation Exilarch and judge, a contemporary of Shmuel; measures his caution
  after meat against his stricter father's.
- **Rav Chisda** — third generation, Sura; that meat-then-cheese must wait but not the reverse is his,
  and he travels the boat with Rabba bar Rav Huna.
- **Rav Acha bar Yosef** — a Babylonian Amora who asks Rav Chisda about meat between the teeth.
- **Rabba bar Rav Huna** — third generation, son of Rav Huna of Sura; the sorceress cannot touch him
  or Rav Chisda on the boat.
- **Abaye** — fourth generation, head of Pumbedita; the whole "at first I would say" chain is his,
  reporting what he learned from **the Master, Rabba** — his teacher and foster-father Rabba bar
  Nachmani.
- **Rav Nachman** (bar Yaakov) — third generation, Nehardea; narrows "optional" middle waters to
  require washing before cheese.
- **Rav Idi bar Avin** and **Rav Yitzchak bar Ashyan** — the pair behind the ruling that first waters
  are a mitzva and last an obligation. Both are relatively minor tradents; I would not press their
  biographies further.
- **Rav Yitzchak bar Yosef** — a traveling Amora who carried Rabbi Yannai's teachings from Eretz
  Yisrael to Babylonia. **Not** Rav Yitzchak bar Ashyan, despite the shared first name.
- **Rav Yehuda son of Rabbi Chiyya** — gives the Sodomite-salt reason for last waters.
- **Rav Ashi** — sixth generation, redactor of the Talmud at Sura; rules on one who handled salt.
  **Not** Rav Asi above.
- **Rav Acha son of Rava** — a later Babylonian who questions Rav Ashi here.
- **Mar bar Rav Ashi** — son of Rav Ashi, a seventh-generation head at Sura; strict about the table
  during drinking, and the sage who excommunicates the gutter-demon.
- **Rav Pappa** — fifth generation, founder of the academy at Naresh; a demon-servant works in his
  house.

*Look-alikes worth flagging:*
- **Rav Asi** (Eretz-Yisrael, asks Rabbi Yochanan, plugs the channel) is **not** **Rav Ashi** the
  redactor — and both appear on this very daf.
- **Rabba** — the "Master" whom Abaye quotes — is Rabba bar Nachmani, **not** **Rava**; and **Rabba
  bar Rav Huna** on the boat is a third, distinct figure.
- **Mar bar Rav Ashi** (son of Rav Ashi) is **not** Rav Acha bar Rav Ashi.
- **Rav Yitzchak bar Ashyan** and **Rav Yitzchak bar Yosef** are two different tradents.

## One line to carry with you

> A daf that starts by wiping crumbs from your mouth ends by teaching that the small, half-forgotten
> habits of a table — save the crumbs, pour off the first water, don't sit under the gutter — are
> never only about tidiness. Under each one, the Sages saw health, fortune, and a whole unseen world.
