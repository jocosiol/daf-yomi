The big picture (read this first)

Yesterday’s page left Abaye’s question hanging in the air: liver bleeds into whatever it touches — so does it forbid the piece of meat cooked with it? Today the Gemara refuses to let it go. It brings a real meal as proof, watches that proof crumble under Rav Ashi’s cross-examination, and finally lands on a startling resolution from Rava: perhaps the whole premise is wrong and liver expels no blood at all once it meets heat.

כִּי הֵיכִי דְּפָלֵיט — הֲדַר בָּלַע. “Just as it expels, it absorbs right back.” Rava’s lever: if you say the scalding vinegar drinks in the liver’s blood, then the liver must drink it back too and be forbidden itself — which no one holds. So nothing was expelled to begin with.

That is the first half of the daf. The second half changes subject but keeps the same engine — absorbed flavor — and asks how far it travels. A knife that slaughtered, a bowl that salted meat, a radish cut on a meaty blade, a hot fish set down in a meat dish: in each, a forbidden or meaty taste has soaked into something that then meets milk. Here Rav and Shmuel divide over a principle that will echo through all of kashrut: is second-hand flavor — taste that passed through a vessel before reaching your food — still strong enough to forbid?

Three movements:

  1. Closing the liver question (111a) — the windpipe meal, Rav Ashi’s objections, the boiling-vinegar scald, and the baraita that turns Abaye’s dilemma into a tannaitic dispute.
  2. How to prepare a liver (111a) — the artery full of blood, tearing it crosswise, the spleen that needs no tearing, and liver-over-meat versus udder-over-meat on the spit.
  3. Absorbed flavor through vessels (111b) — the salted bowl, maliach k’rotei’ach, the cut radish, and Rav vs. Shmuel on the fish in the meat dish: notein ta’am bar notein ta’am.

Key concepts & terms

Term Meaning
קַנְיָא בְּקוֹפֵיהּ (kanya b’kofei) The windpipe with all its attachments — lungs, heart, and liver — cooked as one; Rabbi Zerika’s meal, offered as proof that liver’s blood does not forbid the meat around it.
חָלֵיט (chalit) To scald / sear in a boiling liquid (vinegar or water) at the outset, sealing the blood inside the liver before it can diffuse — Rav Ashi’s alternative reading of the windpipe meal.
כִּי הֵיכִי דְּפָלֵיט הֲדַר בָּלַע (ki heichi d’palit hadar bala) “Just as it expels, it absorbs back” — Rava’s lever: were the scald to draw blood out, the liver would draw it back and be forbidden itself; since it is not, nothing was expelled.
מְתוּבֶּלֶת (metubelet) Spiced liver; per Rabbi Yishmael b. R’ Yoḥanan b. Beroka, spicing (like heavy stewing, sheluka) makes the liver reabsorb its blood, so it is forbidden as well as forbidding.
סִמְפּוֹנָא (simpona) The large blood vessel / artery in the liver; blood soaked into the flesh is permitted, but blood standing in the simpona is forbidden and must be drained.
שְׁתִי וָעֵרֶב (shesi va’erev) “Warp and woof” — tearing the liver lengthwise and crosswise, cut side down, so the vessel-blood runs out onto the fire.
מִשְׁרָק שָׁרֵיק (mishrak sharik) “It merely slides off” — why liver set above meat does not forbid it: the blood glides over the surface rather than soaking in (contrast the udder’s milk, which saroche mesarech, clings and absorbs).
דִּיעֲבַד / לְכַתְּחִלָּה (di’avad / l’chatchila) After the fact vs. at the outset. Mareimar’s ruling: liver or udder set over meat is tolerated di’avad but may not be arranged that way l’chatchila.
מָלִיחַ הֲרֵי הוּא כְּרוֹתֵחַ (maliach k’rotei’ach) “Salted is like boiling” (and kavush k’mevushal, marinated like cooked) — Shmuel’s principle that salting and pickling transfer flavor as heat does, so a bowl that salted meat forbids boiling food placed in it.
כּוּתָּח (kutaḥ) A Babylonian dip made with milk (and bread-mold and salt); the milk food against which every case here is tested — may the meaty knife-cut radish, or the fish, be eaten with it?
נוֹתֵן טַעַם בַּר נוֹתֵן טַעַם (notein ta’am bar notein ta’am) “Flavor of a flavor” — taste that reached the food only second-hand, through a vessel; Shmuel holds it too weak to forbid (so the fish is permitted), Rav holds it forbids.
קַפִּילָא (kapila) A gentile cook who can be trusted to taste a dish and report whether it carries a forbidden flavor — Rav Pappa’s proposal, which Rava accepts where such a cook is on hand.

El panorama general (leer esto primero)

La página de ayer dejó la pregunta de Abaie flotando en el aire: el hígado sangra sobre todo lo que toca — entonces, ¿prohíbe la pieza de carne cocida con él? Hoy la Guemará se niega a soltarla. Trae una comida real como prueba, ve esa prueba desmoronarse bajo el interrogatorio de Rav Ashi, y al fin llega a una resolución asombrosa de parte de Rava: quizás toda la premisa esté errada y el hígado no expulse sangre en absoluto una vez que encuentra el calor.

כִּי הֵיכִי דְּפָלֵיט — הֲדַר בָּלַע. “Así como expulsa, absorbe de vuelta”. La palanca de Rava: si dices que el vinagre hirviendo se bebe la sangre del hígado, entonces el hígado también debe bebérsela de vuelta y quedar prohibido él mismo — cosa que nadie sostiene. Así que nada se expulsó desde un principio.

Esa es la primera mitad del daf. La segunda mitad cambia de tema pero conserva el mismo motor —el sabor absorbido— y pregunta hasta dónde llega. Un cuchillo que degolló, una fuente que saló carne, un rábano cortado sobre una hoja cárnica, un pescado caliente puesto en un plato de carne: en cada caso un sabor prohibido o cárnico se ha empapado en algo que luego encuentra leche. Aquí Rav y Shmuel se dividen sobre un principio que resonará por toda la kashrut: ¿es el sabor de segunda mano —el que pasó por un recipiente antes de llegar a tu comida— todavía lo bastante fuerte para prohibir?

Tres movimientos:

  1. Cerrando la cuestión del hígado (111a) — la comida del gaznate, las objeciones de Rav Ashi, el escaldado con vinagre hirviendo, y la baraita que convierte el dilema de Abaie en una disputa de tanaím.
  2. Cómo preparar un hígado (111a) — la arteria llena de sangre, desgarrarlo en cruz, el bazo que no necesita desgarrarse, e hígado-sobre-carne frente a ubre-sobre-carne en el asador.
  3. Sabor absorbido a través de recipientes (111b) — la fuente salada, maliaj kerotéaj, el rábano cortado, y Rav frente a Shmuel sobre el pescado en el plato de carne: notein taam bar notein taam.

Conceptos y términos clave

Término Significado
קַנְיָא בְּקוֹפֵיהּ (kaniá bekofei) El gaznate con todas sus adherencias — pulmones, corazón e hígado — cocidos como una unidad; la comida de Rabí Zeriká, ofrecida como prueba de que la sangre del hígado no prohíbe la carne que lo rodea.
חָלֵיט (jalit) Escaldar / sellar en un líquido hirviente (vinagre o agua) desde el inicio, sellando la sangre dentro del hígado antes de que se difunda — la lectura alternativa de Rav Ashi de la comida del gaznate.
כִּי הֵיכִי דְּפָלֵיט הֲדַר בָּלַע (ki heiji depalit hadar bala) “Así como expulsa, absorbe de vuelta” — la palanca de Rava: si el escaldado sacara sangre, el hígado la reabsorbería y quedaría prohibido él mismo; como no lo está, nada se expulsó de entrada.
מְתוּבֶּלֶת (metubélet) Hígado especiado; según Rabí Ishmael hijo de R’ Iojanán ben Beroká, el especiado (como la cocción intensa, sheluká) hace que el hígado reabsorba su sangre, y queda prohibido además de prohibir.
סִמְפּוֹנָא (simponá) El gran vaso sanguíneo / arteria del hígado; la sangre empapada en la carne está permitida, pero la sangre acumulada en la simponá está prohibida y debe drenarse.
שְׁתִי וָעֵרֶב (shetí vaérev) “Urdimbre y trama” — desgarrar el hígado a lo largo y a lo ancho, con el corte hacia abajo, para que la sangre de los vasos escurra al fuego.
מִשְׁרָק שָׁרֵיק (mishrak sharik) “Simplemente resbala” — por qué el hígado puesto sobre la carne no la prohíbe: la sangre se desliza por la superficie en vez de empaparse (a diferencia de la leche de la ubre, que saroje mesarej, se adhiere y penetra).
דִּיעֲבַד / לְכַתְּחִלָּה (diavad / lejatjilá) A posteriori frente a de entrada. El fallo de Mareimar: hígado o ubre puestos sobre la carne se toleran diavad, pero no se pueden disponer así lejatjilá.
מָלִיחַ הֲרֵי הוּא כְּרוֹתֵחַ (maliaj kerotéaj) “Lo salado es como lo hirviente” (y kavush kimvushal, lo marinado como lo cocido) — el principio de Shmuel de que salar y encurtir transfieren sabor como el calor, así que una fuente que saló carne prohíbe la comida hirviente puesta en ella.
כּוּתָּח (kutaj) Un aderezo babilónico hecho con leche (y moho de pan y sal); la comida láctea contra la que se prueba cada caso aquí — ¿pueden comerse con él el rábano cortado con cuchillo cárnico, o el pescado?
נוֹתֵן טַעַם בַּר נוֹתֵן טַעַם (notein taam bar notein taam) “Sabor de un sabor” — sabor que llegó a la comida solo de segunda mano, a través de un recipiente; Shmuel lo tiene por demasiado débil para prohibir (así el pescado está permitido), Rav lo tiene por prohibitivo.
קַפִּילָא (kapilá) Un cocinero gentil de quien puede fiarse que cate un plato e informe si lleva un sabor prohibido — la propuesta de Rav Papa, que Rava acepta donde tal cocinero está a mano.

התמונה הגדולה (לקרוא קודם)

הדף של אתמול הותיר את שאלת אביי תלויה באוויר: הכבד מדמם אל כל מה שהוא נוגע בו — האם הוא אוסר את חתיכת הבשר המתבשלת עמו? היום הגמרא מסרבת להרפות ממנה. היא מביאה סעודה ממשית כראיה, רואה את הראיה מתפוררת תחת חקירתו של רב אשי, ולבסוף נוחתת על תשובה מפתיעה של רבא: אולי כל ההנחה שגויה, והכבד אינו פולט דם כלל משפגע בו החום.

כִּי הֵיכִי דְּפָלֵיט — הֲדַר בָּלַע. המנוף של רבא: אם תאמר שהחומץ הרותח בולע את דם הכבד, הרי שהכבד בולע אותו בחזרה ונאסר אף הוא — דבר שאיש אינו אומר. מכאן שלא נפלט דבר מלכתחילה.

זו המחצית הראשונה של הדף. המחצית השנייה מחליפה נושא אך שומרת על אותו מנוע — טעם הבלוע — ושואלת עד כמה הוא מרחיק. סכין ששחט, קערה שמלחו בה בשר, צנון שחתכוהו על להב של בשר, דג חם שהניחוהו בכלי של בשר: בכל אחד מהם טעם אסור או בשרי נספג בדבר שפגש אחר כך חלב. כאן נחלקו רב ושמואל על עיקרון שיהדהד בכל הכשרות: האם טעם ממדרגה שנייה — טעם שעבר דרך כלי בטרם הגיע למאכל — עדיין חזק דיו לאסור?

שלוש תנועות:

  1. סגירת שאלת הכבד (קיא ע״א) — סעודת הקנה, השגותיו של רב אשי, החליטה בחומץ רותח, והברייתא ההופכת את שאלת אביי למחלוקת תנאים.
  2. כיצד מכינים כבד (קיא ע״א) — הסמפון המלא דם, הקריעה שתי וערב, הטחול שאינו צריך קריעה, וכבד־על־בשר מול כחל־על־בשר בשיפוד.
  3. טעם בלוע דרך כלים (קיא ע״ב) — הקערה המלוחה, מליח הרי הוא כרותח, הצנון החתוך, ורב מול שמואל על הדג שעלה בקערה: נותן טעם בר נותן טעם.

כּוּתָּח טיבול בבלי העשוי מחלב (ופירורי לחם מעופש ומלח); המאכל החלבי שכנגדו נבחן כל מקרה כאן — האם מותר לאכול עמו את הצנון שנחתך בסכין בשרי, או את הדג?

Who’s who in today’s daf

Tannaim

  • Rabbi Eliezer (ben Hyrcanus) — second-generation Tanna, student of Rabban Yoḥanan ben Zakkai; holds liver “forbids but is not forbidden.” (Titled Rabbi; not the Amora Rabbi Elazar ben Pedat, who also appears below.)
  • Rabbi Yishmael son of Rabbi Yoḥanan ben Beroka — fourth-generation Tanna; holds spiced or stewed liver becomes forbidden as well. A minor figure, known mainly from such rulings.
  • Rabbi Ḥiyya (the Great) — transitional Tanna, uncle and teacher of Rav; cited at the end for the rule that bread and wine exempt the meal’s other foods and drinks from a separate blessing.

Eretz-Yisrael Amoraim

  • Rabbi Yoḥanan (bar Nappaḥa) — second generation, the towering authority of Tiberias; Ravin reports him as holding salted is not like boiling, and Abaye argues the citation is wrong.
  • Rabbi Zerika — third/fourth generation; offers Rav Safra the windpipe meal as proof.
  • Rabbi Ami — third generation, a leading authority in Tiberias and a student of Rabbi Yoḥanan; his smashing of the salting-bowl is Abaye’s evidence.
  • Rabbi Elazar (ben Pedat) — third generation, foremost student of Rabbi Yoḥanan; a disciple of Rav’s teachings who declines the fish at Shmuel’s table. (Rabbi Elazar; distinct from the Tanna Rabbi Eliezer above.)
  • Ravin (Rav Avin) — fourth generation, famed carrier of Eretz-Yisrael traditions to Babylonia (ki ata Ravin); his report in Rabbi Yoḥanan’s name is here disputed.

Babylonian Amoraim

  • Rav (Abba Arikha) — first generation, founder of Sura; forbids the fish eaten with kutaḥ (notein ta’am), and reassures Rabbi Elazar that Shmuel never fed him anything he held forbidden.
  • Shmuel (of Nehardea) — first generation, Rav’s great colleague and son of Abba bar Abba; permits the fish (notein ta’am bar notein ta’am) and is the source of maliach k’rotei’ach. His servants prepared spleen for him on bloodletting days.
  • Rav Yehuda (bar Yeḥezkel) — second generation, founder of Pumbedita; transmits Shmuel on the salted bowl. (This Rav Yehuda the Amora, not Rabbi Yehuda the Tanna.)
  • Rav Huna — second generation, head of Sura after Rav; his liver was scalded with boiling vinegar.
  • Rav Naḥman (bar Yaakov) — third generation, of Nehardea; his liver was scalded with boiling water, and he ordered “feed Shabba against his will.”
  • Rav Kahana — here specified as the brother of Rav Yehuda; teaches the permitted knife-cut radish. (The name spans several sages; this one is fixed only by the “brother of Rav Yehuda” tag.)
  • Rabba bar Rav Huna — third generation, son of Rav Huna; the guest who found the blood-filled artery. (Not to be confused with Rav bar Shabba, the guest who declined the boiled liver just above him in the daf.)
  • Rabba bar Rav Naḥman — the Shabbat host with the fine oil-and-honey bread.
  • Rava (bar Yosef bar Ḥama) — fourth generation, of Meḥoza; supplies the lever that no blood is expelled, and the “you can taste the radish” distinction. (רבא; distinct from Rabba above — a classic look-alike.)
  • Abaye — fourth generation, head of Pumbedita; refutes Ravin from Rabbi Ami’s smashed bowl, and (via Chizkiya) rules the halakha on fish and radish. His was the original liver dilemma.
  • Rav Pappa — fifth generation, student of Rava; proposes the boiling-vinegar liver is forbidden (Rava overrules), and proposes the gentile cook as taster.
  • Rav Ashi — sixth generation, redactor of the Talmud at Sura-Mata Meḥasya; objects to the windpipe proof and scolds his father-in-law’s son over the spit. (Rav Ashi, not Rav Asi the earlier Amora.)
  • Rav Dimi of Neharde’a — late Amora; taught the reversed ruling on liver and udder over meat.
  • Mareimar — late Amora of Sura; rules publicly on liver/udder over meat.

And the other food is not forbidden due to the liver’s blood, but rather due to the fat of the liver it absorbed. But if permitted liver is cooked with another piece of meat, and the concern is only that the meat might be prohibited due to the meat absorbing excess blood from the liver, what is the halakha? Perhaps blood is absorbed less easily than fat.
When Rav Safra again ascended to Eretz Yisrael, he found Rabbi Zerika once more and asked him about liver cooked with another piece of meat. Rabbi Zerika said to him: You need not ask this question either, as I and Yannai, son of Rabbi Ami, arrived at the house of Yehuda, son of Rabbi Shimon ben Pazi, and they brought before us the windpipe of an animal with all the parts attached to it, i.e., the lungs, heart, and liver, all of which had been cooked together, and we ate it. This proves that the blood emitted from the liver does not render prohibited other pieces of meat cooked with it.
Rav Ashi, and some say Rabbi Shmuel from Zerokinya, objects to this conclusion: But perhaps in that incident the mouth of the windpipe was positioned outside of the pot, allowing the liver’s excess blood to run out of the pot rather than being absorbed by the other pieces of meat. Alternatively, perhaps they poured boiling liquid on the liver at the outset, before it was cooked with the lung and heart, like that custom of Rav Huna, for whom they would pour boiling vinegar on liver, and that of Rav Naḥman, for whom they would pour boiling water on liver. This would cook the excess blood into the liver and prevent it from diffusing into the other pieces. Abaye’s question remains unresolved.
And with regard to the pouring of boiling vinegar on the liver, Rav Pappa, when he was a student before Rava, thought to say that the vinegar becomes prohibited for consumption in the process, since it absorbs blood from the liver. Rava said to Rav Pappa: If you claim that the vinegar is prohibited, then the liver itself should also be prohibited, since just as the liver expels blood and prohibits the vinegar, so too it then absorbs the blood back from the forbidden vinegar. Rather, one must say that no blood is expelled from the liver during this process at all, which is why the liver is permitted afterward.
The Gemara relates that Rav bar Shabba visited the house of Rav Naḥman. They brought him cooked liver, but Rava bar Shabba did not partake of it. The members of the household said to Rav Naḥman: There is a student of Torah inside who is not eating. And who is he? Rav bar Shabba. Rav Naḥman said to them: Feed Shabba against his will.
The Gemara notes: Abaye’s question above with regard to liver cooked with other meat is subject to a dispute between tanna’im, as it is taught in a baraita: Rabbi Eliezer says: The liver that was cooked with other pieces of meat prohibits them, but it itself is not prohibited, because it expels blood as it cooks but does not absorb it again. Rabbi Yishmael, son of Rabbi Yoḥanan ben Beroka, says: If the liver was spiced when cooking, it prohibits the other meat and it becomes prohibited as well, as the spices cause the liver to reabsorb the blood that was expelled. Likewise, if the liver was stewed, i.e., heavily cooked, it renders the other pieces prohibited and is itself prohibited.
The Gemara relates: Rabba bar Rav Huna visited the house of Rabba bar Rav Naḥman and dined with him on Shabbat. They brought before him three se’a of fine bread that had been kneaded in oil and honey. Rabba bar Rav Huna said to the members of Rabba bar Rav Naḥman’s household: Did you know that I was coming, that you prepared such superior food? They said to him: Are you more distinguished than Shabbat, as it is written with regard to Shabbat: “If you proclaim Shabbat a delight, the sacred day of God honored” (Isaiah 58:13).
Meanwhile, Rabba bar Rav Huna found among the dishes before him a certain liver that contained an artery suffused with blood. He said to the members of the household: Why do you do this? Although the blood absorbed in the liver is permitted, that which is collected in the blood vessels is prohibited. The members of the household said to him: Rather, what should we do in order to prepare the liver? Rabba bar Rav Huna said to them: First tear the liver lengthwise and widthwise, and position the side with the tear downward, so that the blood will flow out when you place it on the fire.
The Gemara comments: And this statement applies only to liver, due to the blood that collects in its blood vessels; but there is no need to tear the spleen in this manner, as it merely contains fat. And this ruling accords with that which is reported about Shmuel, that his attendants would prepare a dish of spleens for him on the day that he performed the practice of bloodletting.
new sugyaIt was stated: If liver and other meat are roasted on spits in an oven such that the liver is positioned on top of the meat, the meat is permitted even though blood from the liver flows onto it. This is because the blood that flows from an item roasting in the oven slides over meat located underneath it and is not absorbed. But if an udder is positioned on top of the meat when roasted in the oven, the meat is prohibited. What is the reason? It is that the milk expelled by the roasting udder adheres to and is absorbed by the meat.
Rav Dimi from Neharde’a would teach the opposite: Whenever an udder is positioned on top of the meat, the meat is permitted. What is the reason? The prohibition of meat cooked in milk of a slaughtered animal applies by rabbinic law, and is treated less stringently. But if liver is positioned on top of the meat, the meat is prohibited, as the prohibition of blood applies by Torah law, and one must be concerned that perhaps the meat will absorb blood from the liver.
Mareimar taught in public: The halakha is: Whether in the case of liver or in the case of an udder, if it is underneath the meat, the meat is permitted, but if it is on top of the meat, then after the fact, yes, the meat is permitted, but ab initio, no, one may not situate them in this manner.

new sugyaIt was stated: If a fish was removed from the fire and placed, still hot, in a bowl in which meat had been eaten, Rav says: It is prohibited to eat the fish with the milk dish kutaḥ, since the fish has absorbed meat from the bowl. And Shmuel says: It is permitted to eat the fish with kutaḥ.
The Gemara explains: Rav says that it is prohibited to eat the fish with kutaḥ because this is a case of imparted flavor, i.e., from the meat to the fish. And Shmuel says that it is permitted because the flavor is first imparted to the bowl, and only then from the bowl to the fish. This is therefore a case of imparted flavor derived from imparted flavor.
The Gemara notes: And this opinion of Rav was not stated explicitly; rather, it was stated by inference. As Rav arrived at the house of Rav Shimi bar Ḥiyya, the son of his son. He felt pain in his eyes, and they prepared for him an ointment in an earthenware bowl as a remedy. Later they placed a dish for him in that same bowl. This is because the blood that flows from an item roasting in the oven slides over meat located underneath it and is not absorbed. Rav Dimi from Neharde’a would teach the opposite: Whenever an udder is positioned on top of the meat, the meat is permitted. What is the reason? The prohibition of meat cooked in milk of a slaughtered animal applies by rabbinic law, and is treated less stringently. But if liver is positioned on top of the meat, the meat is prohibited, as the prohibition of blood applies by Torah law, and one must be concerned that perhaps the meat will absorb blood from the liver.

דָּרֵשׁ מָרִימָר: הִלְכְתָא, בֵּין כַּבְדָּא בֵּין כַּחְלָא, תּוּתֵי בִּשְׂרָא – שְׁרֵי, עִילָּוֵי בִּשְׂרָא – דִּיעֲבַד אִין, לְכַתְּחִלָּה לָא.

Mareimar taught in public: The halakha is: Whether in the case of liver or in the case of an udder, if it is underneath the meat, the meat is permitted, but if it is on top of the meat, then after the fact, yes, the meat is permitted, but ab initio, no, one may not situate them in this manner.

ומ"ש מדמא דבשרא — והרי אף דם הבשר כשאין שם כבד זב עם השומן ואנו אוכלין אותו:

ומשני דמא דבשרא שכן — יורד לשולי הכלי והשומן צף למעלה ומניחין לתוך הכלי מראה אדמומית שבשולים:

The Gemara asks: And in what way is this case different from roasting a piece of meat by itself over such a vessel, which is permitted? Here too the blood of the meat drips into the fat in the vessel. The Gemara answers: Blood of most meat sinks to the bottom of the vessel, while the fat floats on top. Since the fat can be separated from the blood, it is permitted. By contrast, the blood of the liver floats above the fat and cannot be removed from it, and therefore the entire mixture is prohibited.

new sugyaThe Gemara cites other statements of Shmuel. Rav Yehuda says that Shmuel says: With regard to a bowl in which meat was salted to remove its blood before cooking, it is prohibited to eat any boiling food placed in it, as that food absorbs blood of the meat from the bowl. And in this Shmuel conforms to his standard line of reasoning, as Shmuel said: A salted food imparts its flavor like a boiling food, and a food item marinated in vinegar, brine, or the like absorbs flavor from the liquid or vessel as would a cooked food.

הא דרבין — דאמר רבי יוחנן מיקל ליתא:

כִּי אֲתָא רָבִין, אָמַר רַבִּי יוֹחָנָן: מָלִיחַ אֵינוֹ כְּרוֹתֵחַ, וְכָבוּשׁ אֵינוֹ כִּמְבוּשָּׁל. אָמַר אַבָּיֵי: הָא דְּרָבִין לֵיתַהּ, דְּהַהִיא פִּינְכָּא דַּהֲוָה בֵּי רַבִּי אַמֵּי, דְּמָלַח בֵּיהּ בִּשְׂרָא, וְתַבְרֵיהּ. מִכְּדֵי, רַבִּי אַמֵּי תַּלְמִיד דְּרַבִּי יוֹחָנָן הֲוָה, מַאי טַעְמָא תַּבְרֵיהּ? לָאו מִשּׁוּם דִּשְׁמִיעָא לֵיהּ מִינֵּיהּ דְּרַבִּי יוֹחָנָן דְּאָמַר: מָלִיחַ הֲרֵי הוּא כְּרוֹתֵחַ!

When Ravin came from Eretz Yisrael to Babylonia he said that Rabbi Yoḥanan said: A salted food is not considered like a boiling food, and a marinated food is not considered like a cooked food. Abaye said: I can prove that this ruling that Ravin cited is not correct, as there was a certain bowl [pinka] in Rabbi Ami’s house in which meat was salted, and Rabbi Ami broke it so that it would no longer be used. Now Rabbi Ami was a student of Rabbi Yoḥanan. What is the reason he broke that bowl? Is it not because he heard that Rabbi Yoḥanan said: A salted food is considered like a boiling food? Ravin’s citation was evidently in error.

מליח אינו כרותח — והא דתניא (לקמן חולין דף קיג.) דטמא מליח וטהור תפל אסור היינו בדגים דרפו קרמייהו:

וצנון שחתכו בסכין — אע"ג דאגב חורפיה דצנון איגר"ש בלע"ז הוא בולע משמנונית הסכין הקרוש עליו והוי האי צנון נתינת טעם ראשון שהרי בלע איסור ממש והוי כבשר:

מותר לאכלו — לאותו צנון בכותח שיש בו נסיובי דחלבא כדמפרש טעמא דכיון שבלע מן השמנונית דהיתרא בלע:

יָתֵיב רַב כָּהֲנָא אֲחוּהּ דְּרַב יְהוּדָה קַמֵּיהּ דְּרַב הוּנָא, וְיָתֵיב וְקָאָמַר: קְעָרָה שֶׁמָּלַח בָּהּ בָּשָׂר – אָסוּר לֶאֱכוֹל בָּהּ רוֹתֵחַ, וּצְנוֹן שֶׁחֲתָכוֹ בְּסַכִּין – מוּתָּר לְאׇכְלוֹ בְּכוּתָּח.

Rav Kahana, the brother of Rav Yehuda, sat before Rav Huna, and he sat and said: With regard to a bowl in which meat was salted, it is prohibited to eat any boiling food placed in it. And he added: With regard to a radish that one cut with a knife used for cutting meat, it is permitted to eat it with kutaḥ, a food that contains milk, even though the sharpness of the radish causes it to absorb the fat of the meat from the knife.

והאי איסורא בלע — קערה בלעה דם:

מַאי טַעְמָא? אָמַר אַבָּיֵי: הַאי – הֶיתֵּרָא בָּלַע, וְהַאי – אִיסּוּרָא בָּלַע.

The Gemara asks: What is the reason to distinguish between blood absorbed in a bowl and fat absorbed by the radish? Abaye said: This radish absorbed a permitted substance, as the fat on the knife is permitted for consumption by itself, but that bowl in which meat was salted absorbed a prohibited substance, i.e., blood.

אמר אביי האי היתרא בלע כו' — לא ר"ל משום דהוי נותן טעם בר נותן טעם כמו דגים שעלו בקערה דאביי לטעמיה דחשיב ליה חד טעמא משום חורפיה דצנון ואע"ג דלקמן אסר אביי אליבא דרב כהנא קאמר לה:

סוף סוף האי היתירא לידי איסורא אתי — כשנותנו בכותח:

Rava said to Abaye: And if the radish absorbed a permitted substance, what of it? Ultimately, if one desires to eat the radish with kutaḥ, it is a permitted substance that leads to a prohibition, as he will eat a prohibited substance. Rather, Rava said: The distinction is that with regard to this radish, it is possible for a Jew to taste it before eating it with milk to see if it has acquired the flavor of meat. But with regard to that bowl, it is not possible for a Jew to taste its contents to see whether they have absorbed blood.

קפילא — נחתום עובד כוכבים:

אֲמַר לֵיהּ רַב פָּפָּא לְרָבָא: וְלִיטְעֲמֵיהּ קַפִּילָא אֲרַמָּאָה, מִי לָא תְּנַן: קְדֵרָה שֶׁבִּישֵּׁל בָּהּ בָּשָׂר לֹא יְבַשֵּׁל בָּהּ חָלָב, וְאִם בִּשֵּׁל – בְּנוֹתֵן טַעַם, בִּשֵּׁל בָּהּ תְּרוּמָה לֹא יְבַשֵּׁל בָּהּ חוּלִּין, וְאִם בִּשֵּׁל – בְּנוֹתֵן טַעַם.

Rav Pappa said to Rava: But let a gentile cook taste the contents of the bowl to see whether they have the taste of blood. Didn’t we learn in the Tosefta (Terumot 8:12): With regard to a pot in which one cooked meat, one may not cook milk in it, and if he cooked milk in it, the meat absorbed in the pot renders the milk forbidden if it imparts flavor to the milk. Likewise, if one cooked teruma in the pot, he may not cook non-sacred food in it, and if he cooked non-sacred food in it, the non-sacred food is prohibited if there is sufficient teruma absorbed in the pot to impart flavor to the non-sacred food.

כי קאמינא — דאסור לאוכל בה רותח בדליכא קפילא:

וְאָמְרִינַן: בִּשְׁלָמָא תְּרוּמָה – טָעֵים לַהּ כֹּהֵן, אֶלָּא בָּשָׂר בְּחָלָב – מַאן טָעֵים לַהּ? וַאֲמַר לַן: לִיטְעֲמֵיהּ קַפִּילָא! הָכִי נָמֵי לִיטְעֲמֵיהּ קַפִּילָא! הָכִי נָמֵי, כִּי קָאָמֵינָא – דְּלֵיכָּא קַפִּילָא.

And we said with regard to this baraita: Granted, one can know whether the non-sacred food has acquired the flavor of teruma, as a priest can taste it. But with regard to the prohibition of meat cooked in milk, who can taste it? And you, Rava, said to us: Let a gentile cook taste it. So too here, with regard to the food in the bowl, let a gentile cook taste it. Rava responded: Indeed, a gentile cook can discover whether the food in the bowl has absorbed the taste of blood. When I said my statement I was referring to a case where there is no gentile cook available.

אִיתְּמַר: דָּגִים שֶׁעָלוּ בַּקְּעָרָה, רַב אָמַר: אָסוּר לְאָכְלָן בְּכוּתָּח, וּשְׁמוּאֵל אָמַר: מוּתָּר לְאָכְלָן בְּכוּתָּח.

new sugyaIt was stated: If a fish was removed from the fire and placed, still hot, in a bowl in which meat had been eaten, Rav says: It is prohibited to eat the fish with the milk dish kutaḥ, since the fish has absorbed meat from the bowl. And Shmuel says: It is permitted to eat the fish with kutaḥ.

נותן טעם בר נותן טעם הוא — אם היו מבושלים עם בשר ממש היה אסור לאוכלן בכותח אי נמי מודה שמואל שאסור לאכול חלב רותח בקערה דנותן טעם ראשון הוי כבשר גמור אבל קערה זו היא עצמה אינה בשר אלא על ידי נותן טעם ואינה כבשר:

רַב אָמַר: אָסוּר, נוֹתֵן טַעַם הוּא; וּשְׁמוּאֵל אָמַר: מוּתָּר, נוֹתֵן טַעַם בַּר נוֹתֵן טַעַם הוּא.

The Gemara explains: Rav says that it is prohibited to eat the fish with kutaḥ because this is a case of imparted flavor, i.e., from the meat to the fish. And Shmuel says that it is permitted because the flavor is first imparted to the bowl, and only then from the bowl to the fish. This is therefore a case of imparted flavor derived from imparted flavor.

Walking through the sugya, step by step

111a — The liver question, run to ground

The page opens mid-argument. The worry about liver cooked with meat is really two worries: the meat might drink in the liver’s fat, or it might drink in its blood — and the Gemara suggests blood may be absorbed less easily than fat. So the dilemma narrows: does permitted liver’s blood cross into the meat at all?

A meal offered as proof. When Rav Safra went up to Eretz Yisrael again he found Rabbi Zerika, who told him the question needs no asking: he and Yannai, son of Rabbi Ami, were once served a whole windpipe with everything hanging from it — lungs, heart, and liver cooked together —

קָרִיבוּ לַן קַנְיָא בְּקוֹפֵיהּ, וַאֲכַלְנָא. “They brought us the windpipe with its attachments, and we ate it.”

— and no one worried that the liver’s blood had forbidden the rest.

The proof is picked apart. Rav Ashi (some say Rabbi Shmuel of Zerokinya) objects: maybe the mouth of the windpipe stuck out of the pot, so the liver’s blood drained off instead of soaking in; or maybe they scalded the liver first (chalit) — like Rav Huna, whose liver was doused with boiling vinegar, and Rav Naḥman, whose was doused with boiling water — searing the blood inside before it could spread. Abaye’s question stands unresolved.

The scald generates its own ruling. Rav Pappa, before Rava, thought the boiling vinegar becomes forbidden, having absorbed the liver’s blood. Rava overturns it with a lever:

אִי חַלָּא אֲסִיר — אִיהוּ נָמֵי אֲסִיר, כִּי הֵיכִי דְּפָלֵיט — הֲדַר בָּלַע. “If the vinegar is forbidden, the liver is forbidden too — for just as it expels, it absorbs back.”

Since everyone agrees the scalded liver stays permitted, the only coherent reading is that no blood is expelled at all. (Then a small table-scene: Rav bar Shabba declined the boiled liver at Rav Naḥman’s; told a Torah scholar was fasting on it, Rav Naḥman said, “Feed Shabba against his will.”)

The dispute behind it all. Abaye’s question, the Gemara notes, is really tannaitic:

הַכָּבֵד אוֹסֶרֶת וְאֵינָהּ נֶאֱסֶרֶת… שֶׁפּוֹלֶטֶת וְאֵינָהּ בּוֹלַעַת. Rabbi Eliezer: liver forbids others but is never forbidden — it expels and does not absorb. Rabbi Yishmael son of Rabbi Yoḥanan ben Beroka: if it was spiced (metubelet) or thoroughly stewed (sheluka), it forbids others and is itself forbidden — the spices and heavy cooking drive the blood back in.

111a — Preparing a liver, and the spit

Rabba bar Rav Huna dined at Rabba bar Rav Naḥman’s on Shabbat — three se’a of fine bread kneaded in oil and honey — and found among the dishes a liver with an artery (simpona) still full of blood. He objected: the blood soaked through the liver is permitted, but blood standing in the vessels is forbidden. The fix:

קְרַעוּ שְׁתִי וָעֵרֶב, וְחִיתּוּכָא לְתַחַת. “Tear it lengthwise and crosswise, and lay the cut side downward” — so the vessel-blood runs out onto the fire.

And only the liver. The spleen (techala) needs no such tearing — it holds only fat, not pooled blood, which is why Shmuel’s servants would make him a spleen dish on his bloodletting days.

On the spit. An anonymous ruling: liver skewered above meat in the oven leaves the meat permitted, because the blood merely slides off (mishrak sharik); an udder above the meat forbids it, because the milk clings and soaks in (saroche mesarech). Rav Dimi of Neharde’a taught the reverse: an udder on top is permitted (its milk-in-meat is only rabbinic), while a liver on top is forbidden (blood is Torah law). Mareimar ruled publicly for the public: liver or udder, underneath the meat is permitted; on top, only after the fact (di’avad), not ab initio (l’chatchila). When Rav Ashi saw his father-in-law Rami bar Abba’s son skewering liver over meat —

111b — Blood that floats, and flavor through vessels

— he scolded, “How haughty this scholar! Granted the Sages permitted meat roasted under liver after the fact — did they permit doing it ab initio?” And he adds: if there is a drip-pan (bei dugei) beneath the spit, even meat placed above the liver is forbidden — the blood falls into the collected fat and one may come to eat the mixture. Why is this worse than roasting plain meat over such a pan?

דְּמָא דְּבִשְׂרָא — שָׁכֵן, דְּמָא דְּכַבְדָּא — קָפֵי. “The blood of meat sinks; the blood of liver floats.” Meat’s blood settles below the fat and can be skimmed away; liver’s blood rides on top and cannot be separated — so the whole panful is forbidden.

Now the pivot to vessels. Rav Naḥman in Shmuel’s name: the slaughtering knife absorbs blood from the animal’s heat, so one may not cut boiling food with it; for cold food, some require a rinse of the cut piece, some do not. Rav Yehuda in Shmuel’s name: a bowl in which meat was salted may not hold boiling food — and this is Shmuel to his own principle:

מָלִיחַ הֲרֵי הוּא כְּרוֹתֵחַ, וְכָבוּשׁ הֲרֵי הוּא כִּמְבוּשָּׁל. “Salted is like boiling, and marinated is like cooked” — salt and pickling drive flavor as heat does.

Ravin reported the opposite in Rabbi Yoḥanan’s name — salted is not like boiling. Abaye refutes the citation: a bowl in Rabbi Ami’s house, used to salt meat, was smashed by Rabbi Ami — a student of Rabbi Yoḥanan. Why smash it, if not because he heard from Rabbi Yoḥanan that salted is like boiling? So Ravin’s version must be mistaken.

The radish and the taste-test. Rav Kahana, Rav Yehuda’s brother, taught that a radish (tzenon) cut with a meat knife may be eaten with kutaḥ — even though its sharpness absorbs the knife’s fat. Why does the bowl forbid but the radish not? Abaye: the radish absorbed a permitted substance (fat), the bowl a forbidden one (blood). Rava rejects this — permitted fat that leads to eating meat-and-milk is no better — and offers the real distinction: you can taste the radish before eating it with milk, but you cannot taste the contents of the bowl. Rav Pappa: then let a gentile cook (kapila) taste it — as we say about a teruma pot, where a priest tastes! Rava concedes: indeed, where a gentile cook is available he tastes; I spoke of where none is to be had.

Fish in a meat dish — Rav vs. Shmuel. A hot fish set into a bowl in which meat was eaten, then eaten with kutaḥ:

רַב אָמַר: אָסוּר… נוֹתֵן טַעַם הוּא; וּשְׁמוּאֵל אָמַר: מוּתָּר, נוֹתֵן טַעַם בַּר נוֹתֵן טַעַם הוּא. Rav: forbidden — the meat-flavor passes straight into the fish. Shmuel: permitted — the flavor went first into the bowl and only then into the fish: flavor of a flavor, too weak to forbid.

Rav’s view, the Gemara notes, was only inferred — from his remark, when an eye-ointment’s taste carried through into a later dish, “it gives so much flavor!” — and even that is discounted, since the ointment was exceptionally bitter. Two stories seal it: Rabbi Elazar, Rav’s disciple, refused the fish at Shmuel’s table until Rav himself assured him, “God forbid the seed of Abba bar Abba would feed me what I hold forbidden”; and two sages across the Sura ford traded the jibe “Orphan!” — one holding like Shmuel on the fish, the other like Rabbi Ḥiyya, that bread and wine exempt all the foods and drinks of the meal from a separate blessing. Chizkiya in Abaye’s name rules the halakha: the fish is permitted with kutaḥ, but the radish is forbidden — and that qualification runs into tomorrow’s daf.

The distinctions, side by side

The hinge of the daf is what sits on top of the meat on the spit, and the three authorities split cleanly by which prohibition — blood or milk — they weight more heavily.

On top of the meat Anonymous ruling Rav Dimi of Neharde’a Mareimar (halakha)
Liver — blood, Torah law Permitted — blood slides off Forbidden Di’avad yes, l’chatchila no
Udder — milk, rabbinic Forbidden — milk clings Permitted Di’avad yes, l’chatchila no

Underneath the meat, every view permits both; the whole dispute is only about the organ set above.

One line to carry with you

Before you ask how far a flavor travels, ask whether it ever left home: sometimes, as with the liver that “expels and absorbs right back,” the cleanest answer is that nothing was ever given off at all.

Recorriendo la suguiá, paso a paso

111a — La cuestión del hígado, llevada hasta el fondo

La página abre a mitad del argumento. La inquietud sobre el hígado cocido con carne son en realidad dos inquietudes: la carne podría beberse la grasa del hígado, o podría beberse su sangre — y la Guemará sugiere que la sangre acaso se absorba con más dificultad que la grasa. Así el dilema se estrecha: ¿cruza siquiera a la carne la sangre del hígado permitido?

Una comida ofrecida como prueba. Cuando Rav Safra subió de nuevo a Erretz Israel encontró a Rabí Zeriká, que le dijo que la pregunta no hace falta hacerla: él y Ianai, hijo de Rabí Amí, fueron servidos una vez con un gaznate entero con todo lo que le cuelga — pulmones, corazón e hígado cocidos juntos —

קָרִיבוּ לַן קַנְיָא בְּקוֹפֵיהּ, וַאֲכַלְנָא. “Nos trajeron el gaznate con sus adherencias, y comimos.”

— y nadie se preocupó de que la sangre del hígado hubiera prohibido el resto.

La prueba se desarma. Rav Ashi (algunos dicen Rabí Shmuel de Zerokinia) objeta: quizás la boca del gaznate sobresalía de la olla, y así la sangre del hígado escurrió afuera en vez de empaparse; o quizás escaldaron el hígado primero (jalit) — como a Rav Huná, cuyo hígado rociaban con vinagre hirviendo, y Rav Najmán, cuyo hígado rociaban con agua hirviendo — sellando la sangre adentro antes de que pudiera esparcirse. La pregunta de Abaie queda sin resolver.

El escaldado genera su propio fallo. Rav Papa, ante Rava, pensó que el vinagre hirviendo queda prohibido, por haber absorbido la sangre del hígado. Rava lo revierte con una palanca:

אִי חַלָּא אֲסִיר — אִיהוּ נָמֵי אֲסִיר, כִּי הֵיכִי דְּפָלֵיט — הֲדַר בָּלַע. “Si el vinagre está prohibido, el hígado también lo está — pues así como expulsa, absorbe de vuelta.”

Como todos coinciden en que el hígado escaldado sigue permitido, la única lectura coherente es que no se expulsa sangre en absoluto. (Luego una pequeña escena de mesa: Rav bar Shabá rechazó el hígado hervido en casa de Rav Najmán; al decírsele que un erudito de la Torá ayunaba con él, Rav Najmán dijo: “Alimenten a Shabá contra su voluntad.”)

La disputa que hay detrás de todo. La pregunta de Abaie, señala la Guemará, es en realidad de tanaím:

הַכָּבֵד אוֹסֶרֶת וְאֵינָהּ נֶאֱסֶרֶת… שֶׁפּוֹלֶטֶת וְאֵינָהּ בּוֹלַעַת. Rabí Eliezer: el hígado prohíbe a los demás pero nunca queda prohibido — expulsa y no absorbe. Rabí Ishmael, hijo de Rabí Iojanán ben Beroká: si fue especiado (metubélet) o muy cocido (sheluká), prohíbe a los demás y queda prohibido él mismo — las especias y la cocción intensa devuelven la sangre adentro.

111a — Preparar un hígado, y el asador

Rabá bar Rav Huná comió en casa de Rabá bar Rav Najmán en Shabat — tres seá de pan fino amasado con aceite y miel — y halló entre los platos un hígado con una arteria (simponá) aún llena de sangre. Objetó: la sangre empapada en el hígado está permitida, pero la sangre acumulada en los vasos está prohibida. La solución:

קְרַעוּ שְׁתִי וָעֵרֶב, וְחִיתּוּכָא לְתַחַת. “Desgárrenlo a lo largo y a lo ancho, y pongan el lado del corte hacia abajo” — para que la sangre de los vasos escurra al fuego.

Y solo el hígado. El bazo (tejalá) no necesita ese desgarro — contiene solo grasa, no sangre acumulada, por lo que los sirvientes de Shmuel le preparaban un plato de bazo en sus días de sangría.

En el asador. Un fallo anónimo: hígado ensartado encima de la carne en el horno deja la carne permitida, porque la sangre simplemente resbala (mishrak sharik); una ubre encima de la carne la prohíbe, porque la leche se adhiere y penetra (saroje mesarej). Rav Dimi de Neardea enseñó lo contrario: una ubre encima está permitida (su leche-en-carne es solo rabínica), mientras que un hígado encima está prohibido (la sangre es de la Torá). Mareimar falló públicamente para el público: hígado o ubre, debajo de la carne está permitido; encima, solo a posteriori (diavad), no de entrada (lejatjilá). Cuando Rav Ashi vio al hijo de su suegro Ramí bar Abá ensartando hígado sobre carne —

111b — Sangre que flota, y sabor a través de recipientes

— reprendió: “¡Qué arrogante este erudito! Concedido que los Sabios permitieron carne asada bajo el hígado a posteriori — ¿acaso permitieron hacerlo de entrada?” Y añade: si hay un recipiente receptor (bei duguei) bajo el asador, hasta la carne puesta encima del hígado está prohibida — la sangre cae en la grasa acumulada y uno podría comer la mezcla. ¿Por qué es esto peor que asar carne sola sobre tal recipiente?

דְּמָא דְּבִשְׂרָא — שָׁכֵן, דְּמָא דְּכַבְדָּא — קָפֵי. “La sangre de la carne se hunde; la sangre del hígado flota.” La sangre de la carne se asienta bajo la grasa y puede retirarse; la sangre del hígado cabalga encima y no puede separarse — así que toda la sartén queda prohibida.

Ahora el giro hacia los recipientes. Rav Najmán en nombre de Shmuel: el cuchillo de degüello absorbe sangre por el calor del animal, así que no se puede cortar comida hirviente con él; para comida fría, algunos exigen enjuagar la pieza cortada, otros no. Rav Iehudá en nombre de Shmuel: una fuente en la que se saló carne no puede contener comida hirviente — y esto es Shmuel según su propio principio:

מָלִיחַ הֲרֵי הוּא כְּרוֹתֵחַ, וְכָבוּשׁ הֲרֵי הוּא כִּמְבוּשָּׁל. “Lo salado es como lo hirviente, y lo marinado es como lo cocido” — la sal y el encurtido impulsan el sabor como lo hace el calor.

Ravin reportó lo contrario en nombre de Rabí Iojanán — lo salado no es como lo hirviente. Abaie refuta la cita: una fuente en casa de Rabí Amí, usada para salar carne, fue rota por Rabí Amí — discípulo de Rabí Iojanán. ¿Por qué romperla, si no porque oyó de Rabí Iojanán que lo salado es como lo hirviente? Así que la versión de Ravin ha de ser errónea.

El rábano y la cata. Rav Kahana, hermano de Rav Iehudá, enseñó que un rábano (tzenón) cortado con un cuchillo cárnico puede comerse con kutaj — aunque su acritud absorba la grasa del cuchillo. ¿Por qué la fuente prohíbe pero el rábano no? Abaie: el rábano absorbió una sustancia permitida (grasa), la fuente una prohibida (sangre). Rava lo rechaza — la grasa permitida que lleva a comer carne-con-leche no vale más — y ofrece la distinción real: al rábano puedes catarlo antes de comerlo con leche, pero el contenido de la fuente no puedes catarlo. Rav Papa: entonces que lo cate un cocinero gentil (kapilá) — ¡como decimos de una olla de trumá, que un cohén cata! Rava concede: en efecto, donde hay cocinero gentil disponible, él cata; yo hablé de donde no lo hay.

Pescado en un plato de carne — Rav frente a Shmuel. Un pescado caliente puesto en una fuente en la que se comió carne, y luego comido con kutaj:

רַב אָמַר: אָסוּר… נוֹתֵן טַעַם הוּא; וּשְׁמוּאֵל אָמַר: מוּתָּר, נוֹתֵן טַעַם בַּר נוֹתֵן טַעַם הוּא. Rav: prohibido — el sabor cárnico pasa directo al pescado. Shmuel: permitido — el sabor fue primero a la fuente y solo luego al pescado: sabor de un sabor, demasiado débil para prohibir.

La opinión de Rav, señala la Guemará, solo fue inferida — de su comentario, cuando el sabor de un ungüento para los ojos pasó a un plato posterior, “¡da tanto sabor!” — y hasta eso se descarta, pues el ungüento era excepcionalmente amargo. Dos relatos lo sellan: Rabí Elazar, discípulo de Rav, rechazó el pescado en la mesa de Shmuel hasta que Rav mismo le aseguró: “Dios libre que la simiente de Abá bar Abá me diera de comer lo que tengo por prohibido”; y dos sabios a ambos lados del vado de Sura se lanzaron la pulla “¡Huérfano!” — uno sosteniendo como Shmuel sobre el pescado, el otro como Rabí Jiiá, que el pan y el vino eximen de una bendición aparte a todas las comidas y bebidas de la comida. Jizkiiá en nombre de Abaie dicta la halajá: el pescado está permitido con kutaj, pero el rábano está prohibido — y esa salvedad corre hacia el daf de mañana.

Las distinciones, lado a lado

El eje del daf es lo que se pone encima de la carne en el asador, y las tres autoridades se separan limpiamente según cuál prohibición —sangre o leche— pesa más para ellas.

Encima de la carne Fallo anónimo Rav Dimi de Neardea Mareimar (halajá)
Hígado — sangre, ley de la Torá Permitido — la sangre resbala Prohibido Diavad sí, lejatjilá no
Ubre — leche, rabínica Prohibido — la leche se adhiere Permitido Diavad sí, lejatjilá no

Debajo de la carne, toda opinión permite ambos; toda la disputa gira solo en torno al órgano puesto encima.

Una línea para llevarte

Antes de preguntar hasta dónde viaja un sabor, pregunta si alguna vez salió de casa: a veces, como con el hígado que “expulsa y absorbe de vuelta”, la respuesta más limpia es que nunca se dio nada.

עוברים על הסוגיה, צעד אחר צעד

קיא ע״א — שאלת הכבד, עד הסוף

הדף נפתח באמצע הטיעון. החשש מכבד המתבשל עם בשר הוא בעצם שני חששות: הבשר עלול לבלוע את שומן הכבד, או לבלוע את דמו — והגמרא מציעה שהדם אולי נבלע בקושי רב יותר מן השומן. כך מצטמצמת השאלה: האם דם הכבד המותר חוצה אל הבשר בכלל?

סעודה המוצעת כראיה. כשעלה רב ספרא לארץ ישראל שוב, מצא את רבי זריקא, שאמר לו שאין צורך לשאול: הוא וינאי בנו של רבי אמי הוגש להם פעם קנה שלם עם כל התלוי בו — ריאה, לב וכבד שבושלו יחד —

קָרִיבוּ לַן קַנְיָא בְּקוֹפֵיהּ, וַאֲכַלְנָא. הביאו לנו את הקנה עם אבריו, ואכלנו.

— ואיש לא חשש שדם הכבד אסר את השאר.

הראיה מתפרקת. רב אשי (ויש אומרים רבי שמואל מזרוקיניא) משיג: שמא פי הקנה היה מחוץ לקדרה, ודם הכבד נשפך החוצה במקום להיספג; או שמא חלטו את הכבד תחילה — כמו רב הונא, שחלטו לו את הכבד בחומץ רותח, ורב נחמן, שחלטו לו ברותחין — צוֹרבים את הדם בפנים בטרם יתפשט. שאלת אביי נותרה בלא הכרעה.

החליטה מולידה הלכה משלה. רב פפא, לפני רבא, סבר לומר שהחומץ הרותח נאסר, לאחר שבלע את דם הכבד. רבא הופך זאת במנוף:

אִי חַלָּא אֲסִיר — אִיהוּ נָמֵי אֲסִיר, כִּי הֵיכִי דְּפָלֵיט — הֲדַר בָּלַע. אם החומץ אסור, הכבד אסור אף הוא — שכשם שהוא פולט, כך הוא בולע בחזרה.

וכיוון שהכול מודים שהכבד החלוט נותר מותר, הקריאה היחידה המתיישבת היא שאין נפלט דם כלל. (ואז סצנת שולחן קטנה: רב בר שבא סירב לכבד המבושל בבית רב נחמן; משנאמר לרב נחמן שתלמיד חכם צם עליו, אמר: “גַּאמוּ לְשַׁבָּא” — האכילוהו לשבא בעל כורחו.)

המחלוקת שמאחורי הכול. שאלת אביי, מציינת הגמרא, היא באמת מחלוקת תנאים:

הַכָּבֵד אוֹסֶרֶת וְאֵינָהּ נֶאֱסֶרֶת… שֶׁפּוֹלֶטֶת וְאֵינָהּ בּוֹלַעַת. רבי אליעזר: הכבד אוסרת אחרים אך לעולם אינה נאסרת — פולטת ואינה בולעת. רבי ישמעאל בנו של רבי יוחנן בן ברוקה: אם הייתה מתובלת או שלוקה היטב, היא אוסרת אחרים וגם נאסרת — התבלין והבישול הכבד מחזירים את הדם פנימה.

קיא ע״א — הכנת כבד, והשיפוד

רבה בר רב הונא סעד אצל רבה בר רב נחמן בשבת — שלוש סאין של פת נקייה הלושה בשמן ובדבש — ומצא בין המאכלים כבד שבו סמפון (עורק) עודנו מלא דם. הוא השיג: הדם הבלוע בכבד מותר, אך הדם העומד בעורקים אסור. הפתרון:

קְרַעוּ שְׁתִי וָעֵרֶב, וְחִיתּוּכָא לְתַחַת. קרעו אותו לאורכו ולרוחבו, וצד החתך כלפי מטה — כדי שדם העורקים יזוב אל האש.

ורק הכבד. הטחול אינו צריך קריעה כזו — אין בו אלא שומן, לא דם עומד, ולכן משרתי שמואל היו מכינים לו תבשיל של טחול בימי הקזת הדם שלו.

בשיפוד. הוראה סתמית: כבד התחוב מעל לבשר בתנור מותיר את הבשר מותר, שכן הדם פשוט מחליק ואינו נבלע; כחל מעל לבשר אוסר אותו, שכן החלב נסרך ונבלע. רב דימי מנהרדעא שנה להפך: כחל מלמעלה מותר (בשר בחלב של שחוטה אינו אלא מדרבנן), ואילו כבד מלמעלה אסור (דם הוא מן התורה). מרימר דרש ברבים: כבד או כחל, מתחת לבשר — מותר; מעל לבשר — בדיעבד אין, לכתחילה לא. כשראה רב אשי את בנו של חמיו רמי בר אבא תוחב כבד מעל בשר —

קיא ע״ב — דם שצף, וטעם דרך כלים

— גער: “כמה יהיר חכם זה! נניח שהתירו חכמים בשר הצלוי תחת כבד בדיעבד — וכי התירו לעשות כן לכתחילה?” ומוסיף: אם יש בֵּי דוּגֵי (כלי קיבול) תחת השיפוד, אף בשר המונח מעל לכבד אסור — הדם נופל אל השומן הנקווה ועלול אדם לאכול את התערובת. במה שונה זה מצליית בשר לבדו מעל כלי כזה?

דְּמָא דְּבִשְׂרָא — שָׁכֵן, דְּמָא דְּכַבְדָּא — קָפֵי. דם הבשר שוקע; דם הכבד צף. דם הבשר יורד מתחת לשומן וניתן להסירו; דם הכבד רוכב מעליו ואי אפשר להפרידו — ולכן כל המחבת אסורה.

כעת המעבר אל הכלים. רב נחמן בשם שמואל: הסכין ששחט בה בולעת דם מחום הבהמה, ואסור לחתוך בה דבר רותח; דבר צונן — יש אומרים שהחתיכה שנחתכה צריכה הדחה, ויש אומרים שאינה צריכה. רב יהודה בשם שמואל: קערה שמלחו בה בשר אסור לאכול בה דבר רותח — וזה שמואל לשיטתו:
