Daf Yomi — Chullin 98 (חולין צח)

Chapter 7: Gid HaNasheh (the sciatic nerve) · Study date: Thu, 6 August 2026

The big picture (read this first)

Yesterday’s daf built the two roads to permitting a mixture: taste it, or — when nobody can taste it — fall back on a fixed ratio. Today the Gemara does the hard engineering work on that second road. Where does the number come from? What exactly do you count? And is the whole ratio even a real, general rule?

Today’s daf is the birth certificate of בְּשִׁשִּׁים — nullification in sixty, the number that still runs a modern kosher kitchen. And it turns out the number is not a tradition handed down at Sinai; it is derived, from a single cooked foreleg of a nazirite’s ram.

Three movements:

  1. Finishing the egg (98a top) — Abaye’s answer from 97b gets tested and re-tested: does a non-kosher egg really impart flavor, or only an egg with a chick inside it?
  2. Practical measuring (98a) — what counts toward the sixty (does a pot’s absorption help you?), what happens when the forbidden piece is less than an olive-bulk, and a wonderful sequence of earlier sages who permitted at forty-seven, forty-five, forty-three — before the number hardened.
  3. Sixty or a hundred? (98a end – 98b) — a dispute in the name of bar Kappara, both sides reading the same verse, and then a serious challenge: maybe the nazirite’s ram teaches nothing general at all, because מֵחִידּוּשׁ לָא גָּמְרִינַן — we do not derive rules from an anomaly.

Walking through the sugya, step by step

98a — Abaye finishes the egg answer (and gets pushed hard)

The answer. 97b ended with Rav Idi bar Avin’s needle: people call something tasteless “mere egg-water,” so how can a forbidden egg impart flavor? Abaye’s answer lands here: the case is בְּבֵיצַת אֶפְרוֹחַ — an egg with a chick in it. That imparts flavor. But a plain egg from a non-kosher bird, with no chick, לָא — it does not.

The objection. Rav Idi bar Avin cites a baraita (Tosefta, Terumot 9:5): kosher eggs boiled together with non-kosher eggs — if the ratio is such that they impart flavor, all are forbidden. So a non-kosher egg does have flavor. Abaye holds his ground: there too it means an egg with a chick — and the baraita calls it “non-kosher” precisely because the chick makes it forbidden.

The pushback. But the baraita’s second clause says explicitly, “eggs that one boiled and a chick was found in one of them” — so by inference the first clause must be talking about an egg without a chick.

Two ways to read a baraita. The Gemara offers פָּירוּשֵׁי קָא מְפָרֵשׁ — the second clause isn’t a new case, it is explaining the first: “all forbidden — how so? Where a chick was found in one of them.” And there’s a logic to support it: if the first clause already dealt with a chick-less egg, teaching the chick case afterward would be redundant (an obvious kal vachomer).

But the logic is refused. אִי מִשּׁוּם הָא לָא אִירְיָא — that proof is inconclusive. One can equally say the second clause was taught to reveal the first: so you wouldn’t mistakenly read the first clause as only about a chick-egg. On that reading the first clause really is a chick-less non-kosher egg, and it really does forbid. The Gemara leaves the reading unresolved and moves on — worth noting, because it means Abaye’s narrow reading is defended, not proven.

98a — What counts toward the sixty?

Case 1: don’t double-count the pot. An olive-bulk of forbidden fat (כְּזֵיתָא תַּרְבָּא) fell into a pot of kosher meat (דִּיקּוּלָא דְּבִשְׂרָא). Rav Asi thought to measure the permitted volume including what the pot had absorbed. The Rabbis shut it down with one line: אַטּוּ דְּהֶיתֵּרָא בָּלַע, דְּאִיסּוּרָא לָא בָּלַע? — “Is that to say the pot absorbed only the permitted meat and not the forbidden fat?” You can’t count the vessel’s absorption as pure credit on the permitted side of the ledger; it drank in both.

(A textual note: the manuscript tradition here has “Rav Asi” proposing and “Rav Ashi” being answered. Most likely one figure is intended — see “Who’s who” below.)

Case 2: no discount for a small piece. A half-olive-bulk of forbidden fat fell into a pot of meat. Mar bar Rav Ashi reasoned that you’d only need thirty half-olive-bulks to nullify it, not sixty. His father Rav Ashi answered with two blows: (i) לָא תְּזַלְזֵל בְּשִׁיעוּרִין דְּרַבָּנַן — “don’t treat the Sages’ measures lightly,” even for a rabbinic prohibition; and (ii) Rabbi Yochanan taught חֲצִי שִׁיעוּר אָסוּר מִן הַתּוֹרָה — a half-measure is forbidden by Torah law. A half-olive of forbidden fat is fully forbidden, so it needs a full sixty times its own volume.

98a — The egg in sixty-one, and a chain of transmission

The longest chain on the daf. Rav Shemen bar Abba → in the name of Rav Idi bar Idi bar Gershom → Levi bar Perata → Rabbi Nachum → Rabbi Biryam → “a certain elder, and his name was Rabbi Yaakov” → and they of the Nasi’s house (דְּבֵי נְשִׂיאָה) said: a non-kosher egg among sixty — forbidden; among sixty-one — permitted.

Why R’ Zeira was startled. Rabbi Zeira said to Rav Shemen bar Abba: “See that you are fixing a limit of permission!” — i.e. you have just resolved something the two greatest sages of the generation, Rabbi Yaakov bar Idi and Rabbi Shmuel bar Nachmani (both quoting Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi), left open. They had said the same words — sixty forbidden, sixty-one permitted — and the question was raised before them:

בַּהֲדֵי דִּידַהּ, אוֹ דִילְמָא לְבַר מִינַּהּ? — is it sixty-one including the forbidden egg, or sixty-one besides it?

They didn’t resolve it. Rav Shemen’s phrasing implies the count includes it.

A third voice. Rabbi Chelbo in the name of Rav Huna states it the other way and explicitly: sixty and it — forbidden; sixty-one and it — permitted. That is, sixty-one kosher eggs besides the non-kosher one.

98a — Before the number hardened: 47, 45, 43, 30

A little gallery of cases that shows the ratio was once elastic:

  • Someone came before Rabban Gamliel bar Rebbi: “Father did not measure at forty-seven — and I will measure at forty-five.” (He permitted at a ratio of 45:1.)
  • Someone came before Rabbi Shimon bar Rebbi: “Father did not measure at forty-five — and I will measure at forty-three.”
  • Someone came before Rabbi Chiyya: “Is there even thirty?” — meaning, obviously not; forbidden.

But wait. If R’ Chiyya’s objection was “there isn’t even thirty,” does that imply that with thirty he would have permitted — against the sixty rule? Rabbi Chanina answers in a word: גּוּזְמָא — it was rhetorical exaggeration. There wasn’t remotely enough; he wasn’t legislating a threshold of thirty.

98a end — Sixty or one hundred?

Rabbi Chiyya bar Abba said Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi in the name of bar Kappara: כָּל אִיסּוּרִין שֶׁבַּתּוֹרָה בְּשִׁשִּׁים — every Torah prohibition is nullified in sixty. Rabbi Shmuel bar Rav Yitzchak objects to his face: “My teacher, you say that?” — because Rav Asi reported the very same chain (R’ Yehoshua ben Levi in the name of bar Kappara) as saying בְּמֵאָה — in one hundred.

And both learned it from the same three words. Neither number is a tradition; both are read out of “זְרוֹעַ בְּשֵׁלָה” — the nazirite’s ram: “And the priest shall take the cooked foreleg of the ram” (Bamidbar 6:19). The foreleg belongs to the kohen; the rest of the ram is eaten by the nazir, kohen or not. Yet the Torah has them cooked together — and the rest of the ram stays permitted to a non-kohen. That is nullification, written into the Torah.

98b — Reading the verse, and then doubting the whole derivation

What does “בְּשֵׁלָה” mean? A tannaitic dispute:

  • The first tanna: אֵין “בְּשֵׁלָה” אֶלָּא שְׁלֵימָה — it means the foreleg must be whole.
  • Rabbi Shimon ben Yochai: it means the foreleg is cooked together with the whole ram.

Two ways to frame that dispute (two lishanot):

What both agree Where they split
First lishna The foreleg is cooked with the ram Cut it off first and then cook, or cook the whole ram and then cut?
Second lishna You cut it off first and then cook Cook it with the ram (RaShBY), or in a separate pot?

Why it matters: the nullification proof needs the foreleg to be cooked together with the ram. On the first lishna that’s agreed by all — the derivation is universal. On the second, it holds only according to RaShBY; the other tanna’s foreleg cooks alone, so nothing is derived.

Now the arithmetic of the dispute. How do sixty and a hundred come out of one animal?

  • בְּשִׁשִּׁים — you measure meat and bones of the foreleg against the meat and bones of the rest of the ram. That ratio is roughly 1:60.
  • בְּמֵאָה — you measure meat against meat only (bones don’t count). That ratio is 1:100.

One animal, two accounting methods, two halachic numbers. This is the most satisfying line on the daf.

The serious challenge. A baraita on the nazirite’s ram says: זֶהוּ הֶיתֵּר הַבָּא מִכְּלַל אִיסּוּר — “This is a permitted thing that emerges from within a prohibition.” That emphatic “זֶהוּ” — this must be excluding something. What? Presumably all other Torah prohibitions — meaning the ram is a one-off and teaches nothing general. So how can 60 or 100 be derived from it?

Abaye’s answer. The exclusion is narrow: it’s needed only for Rabbi Yehuda, who holds מִין בְּמִינוֹ לֹא בָּטֵיל — a food mixed with its own type is never nullified. The baraita tells us that here, exceptionally, it is nullified.

  • Objection: then let R’ Yehuda learn from here that min b’mino is always nullified!
  • Answer: the Torah revealed otherwise — “וְלָקַח מִדַּם הַפָּר וּמִדַּם הַשָּׂעִיר” (Vayikra 16:18): the bull’s blood and the goat’s blood are mixed together, and the verse still names the goat’s blood separately — so like-in-like keeps its identity and is not nullified.
  • Objection: why derive from that verse rather than from the ram?
  • Answer: חִידּוּשׁ הוּא, וּמֵחִידּוּשׁ לָא גָּמְרִינַן — the ram is an anomaly, because normally one may not deliberately nullify a forbidden food, yet here the Torah instructs you to cook them together. And we don’t build general rules on anomalies.

Then the killer follow-up — and the elegant escape. If the ram is an anomaly, then don’t derive sixty or a hundred from it either! The answer is a beautiful piece of legal reasoning:

אַטּוּ אֲנַן לְקוּלָּא גָּמְרִינַן? לְחוּמְרָא גָּמְרִינַן, דְּמִדְּאוֹרָיְיתָא בְּרוּבָּא בָּטֵיל. “Are we deriving a leniency? We are deriving a stringency — because by Torah law nullification happens with a simple majority.”

That is, without this derivation the Torah-level rule would be the far more lenient rov — a bare majority of permitted food. Sixty and a hundred are restrictions, and a restriction may safely be learned even from an anomaly.

Rava’s alternative. Rava reads the baraita’s “זֶהוּ” differently: it is needed only for טַעַם כְּעִיקָּר — flavor counting as substance. In the realm of קָדָשִׁים (sacrificial food) absorbed flavor does forbid and is not nullified; the baraita therefore has to tell us that here, in the nazirite’s ram, the rest of the animal is permitted even to non-kohanim.

Key concepts & terms

Term Meaning
בֵּיצַת אֶפְרוֹחַ — beitzat efroach An egg with a chick formed inside. Abaye’s whole answer: this imparts flavor; a chick-less non-kosher egg does not.
פָּירוּשֵׁי קָא מְפָרֵשׁ — peirushei ka mefaresh “It is explaining” — a baraita’s second clause isn’t a new case but a clarification of the first. A standard Talmudic repair.
אִי מִשּׁוּם הָא לָא אִירְיָא “If that’s your reason, it proves nothing” — the formula for rejecting an inference without rejecting the conclusion.
דִּיקּוּלָא — dikula The cooking vessel/pot (Aramaic). Its absorption cannot be counted on the permitted side alone.
כְּזֵיתָא תַּרְבָּא — k’zeita tarba An olive-bulk of forbidden fat — the standard unit of a forbidden quantity.
חֲצִי שִׁיעוּר אָסוּר מִן הַתּוֹרָה R’ Yochanan: a half-measure is Torah-forbidden. Hence no proportional discount for a small piece — still sixty.
לָא תְּזַלְזֵל בְּשִׁיעוּרִין דְּרַבָּנַן “Don’t treat rabbinic measures lightly” — Rav Ashi’s rebuke to his son.
בַּהֲדֵי דִּידַהּ / לְבַר מִינַּהּ “Including it” / “besides it” — the unresolved dilemma about whether sixty-one counts the forbidden item.
דְּבֵי נְשִׂיאָה — d’vei Nesi’ah “The house of the Nasi” — the Patriarch’s court, cited as a halachic body.
גּוּזְמָא — guzma Rhetorical exaggeration. R’ Chanina’s rescue of R’ Chiyya’s “is there even thirty?”
בְּשִׁשִּׁים / בְּמֵאָה Nullification in sixty or in one hundred — the dispute reported in bar Kappara’s name.
זְרוֹעַ בְּשֵׁלָה — zeroa besheila The nazirite’s “cooked foreleg” (Bamidbar 6:19) — the Torah source for both numbers.
הֶיתֵּר הַבָּא מִכְּלַל אִיסּוּר “A permitted thing emerging from a prohibition” — the baraita’s description of the nazir’s ram.
מִין בְּמִינוֹ לֹא בָּטֵיל R’ Yehuda: like mixed into like is never nullified (you can’t tell the flavors apart).
חִידּוּשׁ הוּא, וּמֵחִידּוּשׁ לָא גָּמְרִינַן “It is an anomaly, and we don’t derive from an anomaly” — the objection to using the ram at all.
מִדְּאוֹרָיְיתָא בְּרוּבָּא בָּטֵיל By Torah law, nullification is by simple majority — which is why 60/100 count as stringencies.
טַעַם כְּעִיקָּר — taam k’ikar Flavor has the status of substance. Rava’s reading of what the baraita’s “this” excludes.

Who’s who in today’s daf

Tannaim and the transitional generation

  • Rebbi (רבי, quoted as “Abba” by his sons) — Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi, last-generation Tanna, Beit Shearim then Tzippori, redactor of the Mishnah. Here he is the father who permitted at forty-seven (and, per the next story, at forty-five). Title tell: “Rebbi” standing alone always means him.
  • Rabban Gamliel bar Rebbi (רבן גמליאל בר רבי) — Rabban Gamliel III, son and successor of R’ Yehuda HaNasi as Nasi; transitional Tanna/first-generation Amora, Eretz Yisrael. Permits at 45.
  • Rabbi Shimon bar Rebbi (רבי שמעון בר רבי) — the other son of R’ Yehuda HaNasi, brother of Rabban Gamliel III, same transitional generation. Permits at 43.
  • Rabbi Chiyya (רבי חייא) — Chiyya Rabbah, transitional Tanna; born in Bavel, moved to Eretz Yisrael, close colleague-disciple of Rebbi. He of the “is there even thirty?” line. Look-alike alert: not to be confused with Rabbi Chiyya bar Abba, a much later Eretz-Yisrael Amora who also appears on this daf.
  • Rabbi Shimon ben Yochai (רבי שמעון בן יוחאי, RaShBY) — Tanna, 4th generation, Eretz Yisrael, a leading student of Rabbi Akiva. Here he reads “besheila” as “cooked with the whole ram.”
  • Rabbi Yehuda (רבי יהודה) — Rabbi Yehuda bar Ilai, Tanna, 4th generation, Eretz Yisrael, also a student of R’ Akiva and RaShBY’s frequent disputant. The daf’s anonymous holder of min b’mino lo batel.

Eretz-Yisrael Amoraim

  • Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi (רבי יהושע בן לוי) — 1st-generation Eretz-Yisrael Amora, Lod; senior contemporary of Rabbi Yochanan. Both the “sixty” and the “one hundred” traditions run through him.
  • Rabbi Chanina (רבי חנינא) — Rabbi Chanina bar Chama, 1st-generation Eretz-Yisrael Amora, Tzippori, a disciple of Rebbi. He explains R’ Chiyya’s line as guzma.
  • Rabbi Yochanan (רבי יוחנן) — 2nd-generation Eretz-Yisrael Amora, head of the academy in Tiberias, the central figure of the Yerushalmi; famous disputant Reish Lakish. Quoted here for chatzi shiur asur min haTorah.
  • Rabbi Zeira (רבי זירא) — 3rd-generation Amora; born and trained in Bavel, then emigrated to Eretz Yisrael (Tiberias), where he is the classic exemplar of Babylonian rigor meeting EY tradition. He challenges Rav Shemen bar Abba.
  • Rav Shemen bar Abba (רב שמן בר אבא) — 3rd-generation Eretz-Yisrael Amora, Tiberias, a student of Rabbi Yochanan. Transmits the “sixty-one” ruling of the Nasi’s house.
  • Rabbi Yaakov bar Idi (רבי יעקב בר אידי) — 3rd-generation Eretz-Yisrael Amora, a leading transmitter of R’ Yehoshua ben Levi. Look-alike alert: distinct from Rav Idi bar Avin and from Rav Idi bar Idi bar Gershom, both of whom also appear on this daf.
  • Rabbi Shmuel bar Nachmani (רבי שמואל בר נחמני) — 3rd-generation Eretz-Yisrael Amora, the great aggadist of his generation, also transmitting R’ Yehoshua ben Levi. Paired here with R’ Yaakov bar Idi as “the two greatest of the generation.”
  • Rabbi Chiyya bar Abba (רבי חייא בר אבא) — 3rd-generation Eretz-Yisrael Amora, a student of Rabbi Yochanan. He reports the sixty version. (See the look-alike note on Rabbi Chiyya above.)
  • Rabbi Shmuel bar Rav Yitzchak (רבי שמואל בר רב יצחק) — 3rd-generation Amora, Bavel-born, active in Eretz Yisrael. He confronts R’ Chiyya bar Abba with the hundred version.
  • Rav Asi (רב אסי) — in the 60-vs-100 report, the Eretz-Yisrael Rav Asi/Assi, a colleague of Rabbi Ami in Tiberias, 3rd generation. Look-alike alert: spelled a hair away from Rav Ashi (רב אשי) the Babylonian redactor, and from Rav Assi the Babylonian colleague of Rav Ami. In the pot-of-fat story on 98a the texts read “Rav Asi” proposing and “Rav Ashi” answering, which most commentators treat as one Babylonian figure; I’d flag this as a genuine textual uncertainty rather than pretend it’s settled.
  • Rabbi Chelbo (רבי חלבו) — 3rd-generation Amora, Bavel-born and active in Eretz Yisrael, a regular transmitter of Rav Huna. States the “sixty-one besides it” version.
  • bar Kappara (בר קפרא) — the Eretz-Yisrael sage in whose name both the sixty and the hundred are reported. A genuinely obscure figure; the sources give us little beyond the name, and I would not want to guess at his generation or teachers.
  • Rabbi Nachum, Rabbi Biryam, Levi bar Perata, “a certain elder named Rabbi Yaakov” — the middle links in 98a’s long chain of transmission. All appear to be Eretz-Yisrael figures of the earlier Amoraic generations, but they are minor and poorly documented; honestly, the chain itself is the main thing we know about them.

Babylonian Amoraim

  • Rav Huna (רב הונא) — 2nd-generation Babylonian Amora, head of the academy at Sura, the foremost student of Rav. Quoted via R’ Chelbo on the sixty-one count.
  • Abaye (אביי) — 4th-generation Babylonian Amora, head of Pumbedita; raised by his uncle Rabbah bar Nachmani (his principal teacher). His lifelong dialectic with Rava defines the period. Here he narrows the baraita’s “this” to R’ Yehuda’s min b’mino.
  • Rava (רבא) — 4th-generation Babylonian Amora of Machoza, student of Rabbah and of Rav Nachman, Abaye’s great disputant (and the one whose view we usually follow). He reads the baraita’s “this” as being about taam k’ikar in kodashim. Look-alike alert: רבא (Rava) is a different person from רבה (Rabbah) — Rabbah bar Nachmani, the 3rd-generation head of Pumbedita and Abaye’s teacher. One letter, one generation apart.
  • Rav Idi bar Avin (רב אידי בר אבין) — 4th-generation Babylonian Amora, a contemporary of Abaye and Rava. He is the one pressing Abaye on the eggs, carried over from 97b.
  • Rav Ashi (רב אשי) — 6th-generation Babylonian Amora, head of the academy at Mata Mechasya (Sura) for roughly half a century, and the principal redactor of the Babylonian Talmud. Here, the father correcting his son on measures.
  • Mar bar Rav Ashi (מר בר רב אשי) — also called Tavyomi; 7th-generation Babylonian Amora, Rav Ashi’s son, later head of Sura. He proposes the thirty-half-olives shortcut. Look-alike alert: Mar bar Rav Ashi (the son) is not Rav Acha bar Rav Ashi, a separate, earlier figure who appeared on 97b in the salted-thigh dispute.

One line to carry with you

The number sixty is not a received tradition — it is an act of reading: one cooked foreleg in one ram, counted with its bones. And the reason we may learn from so strange a case at all is that we are learning a stringency — because left to the Torah’s own arithmetic, a simple majority would already have been enough.
